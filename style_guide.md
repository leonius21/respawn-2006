# STYLE GUIDE

- Genre: esports drama with time-travel regression, set in real China from 2006. Grounded, warm, and very funny, with real stakes underneath. Wins are earned; opponents and adults are not stupid.
- POV: Lin Chen's chapters are first person, close inside his head. Chapters assigned to another POV character (Boss Mei, Fang Hu, Xu Qing, others) are close third person on that character. One POV per scene; mark a POV switch with a scene break. Boss Mei chapters may plant loop clues, but her thoughts never state the loop plainly before the arc-5 midpoint: show what she does and notices, not what she knows.
- Tense: past. Language: American English throughout, spelling and vocabulary (no British words or spellings, in narration or dialogue). Use: apartment, bangs, cookie, fall, mailbox, mail carrier, line (not queue), bathroom, faucet, sidewalk, pants, trash, period, semester, math, schedule, lunch box, cell phone, gray, toward; mahjong call "peng".
- Voice: Lin Chen is a tired, sarcastic 31-year-old in a 15-year-old body. His comedy comes from the gap: old-man habits (groaning when he stands, expecting back pain that never comes, calling everyone "kids"), slips about things that don't exist yet (covered badly), forgotten schoolwork, and dry commentary on teen drama. He softens slowly over the story. Let sincere moments land without a joke on top, especially with Grandma and Fang Hu.
- Comedy: at least one genuinely funny beat per chapter. Vary the source: Big Bird's appetite and volume, Xu Qing's formal fury, Fang Hu's cheek, Grandma's dialect bluntness, Boss Mei's insults, Qin Shuo's five-word replies, Alina's shameless flirting. Let some jokes fall flat in-world.
- Ration habits: the "It isn't X. It's Y." reversal and "Not because X. Because Y." at most once a chapter. At most one future-slip joke per chapter. Comparisons built on "the way" (the way a man does something, the way she always did): at most two per chapter. Comparisons to a generic person ("like a man who...", "as if a boy had...", "like someone..."): at most two per chapter. Go beyond two only for a deliberate callback; otherwise use a direct image, a concrete detail, or nothing. Vary sentence rhythm and paragraph length.
- Minor characters: most walk-on people (customers, clerks, neighbors, passers-by) are plain and just do their job, with no quirk and no memorable line. Give a vivid detail to at most one or two minor characters per chapter.
- Runs of very short narration sentences (three or more tiny beats in a row): at most two per chapter. Vary sentence length; let some sentences run long and loose.
- Trust the reader: when an action, object or line of dialogue already carries the feeling, do not add a sentence that explains what it meant or why it mattered. Leave the subtext under the surface.
- Comparisons (similes, "as if", "as X as a"): no more than about 3 per 1000 words, and never at an even rate. Most description is plain and literal. Save a comparison for a moment that matters, and let whole pages go without one.
- Wry tag endings (a sentence closed with a comma and a "which..." comment): at most two per chapter.
- Insults, curses and sayings: only real Chinese or Sichuan expressions, translated plainly (the established ones in canon and the voice sheet), or blunt plain words. Never invent "folk" insults or proverbs that sound translated. Real speakers are blunter and less polished than a clever line.
- Dialogue voices: follow the voice sheet in the cast roster. Not everyone is witty; most people talk plainly.
- The old-man groan (groaning or creaking when he stands, bracing for back pain that never comes): rare, and only when it costs Lin Chen something (it gives him away, embarrasses him in front of someone who matters, or marks a change in him). Never as filler.
- Signature phrases (Grandma's "melon-brained", the "chopstick" insult, "turtle's egg" and any later catchphrase): at most once a chapter each, and only when the moment gives the phrase a new angle (a new speaker, a new target, a turn in meaning).
- Chapter endings: vary them. No more than one chapter in three ends on a punchline; many should end on an image, a silence, a gesture or a plain small decision. Not every chapter ends on a quiet, moving image either: some end flat, on an ordinary moment, mid-action, or on a line of plain dialogue.
- Day numbers ("Day 8") are bookkeeping for plans and the state; never put them in prose. Characters say "last Saturday", "ten days ago" or the date.
- What Lin Chen knew in his first life: he left Qingshui at 17 and barely looked back, so he never knew other people's private lives there (illnesses, money trouble, how long someone hid a pain, family secrets, prices and small dates). When his narration reaches back for such a fact, it is a guess, an assumption or something heard secondhand, and the prose marks it as one. He states as known only what canon or his own first-life experience supports (games, players, the scene, public events, his own memories).
- Hometown recognition: Lin Chen grew up in Qingshui (birth to 17 in his first life). When he sees a Qingshui place or person for the first time in this life, he usually recognizes them from his first life, in a line or two: what it was, what it became, what he thought of it then. Public, ordinary knowledge only; private facts about people stay guesses (see the line above).
- Theme: lateness is the book's quiet spine (Lin Chen was too late for Grandma in his first life; Mei's loops failed by being too late). Let small moments touch it now and then (arriving late, a call ended too soon, something said after it no longer helps, a clock) without ever naming the theme.
- Imperfect people: in dialogue, people interrupt, trail off, misunderstand, dodge and leave things unsaid. Not every line is polished or quotable.
- Language: dialogue is in Chinese rendered as natural English. Names are surname first (Lin Chen, Su Wen). Keep a light Chinese flavor with a few recurring words, each made clear by context the first time: "Ge" (big brother, said to an older boy), "Nainai" (grandma), "wa" (Sichuan for kid), "aiya". No pinyin walls, no fake broken English. Everyone speaks Chinese by default (Sichuan dialect for locals). Whenever someone speaks another language (English, Russian, Cantonese...), the prose says so plainly at that moment (in English, in Russian) and the foreign-language words go in italics, so readers always know which language they are hearing; say when they switch back if it matters. Alina's Chinese is fluent but slightly off (odd word choices, too direct).
- Profanity: realistic but controlled. People swear when it fits (rage at a monitor, a lost fight, Boss Mei's insults); mostly mild words (damn, hell, crap, shit), the f-word only at big moments and rarely. Lin Chen's inner voice swears a little, like a tired adult. Grandma and kids around adults sometimes swear "off-page" as a joke instead, described freshly each time (never the same wording twice); creative Sichuan curses, translated, are often funnier than English swearing. Never slurs.
- Nicknames: whenever a nickname appears for the first time, tie it clearly to the person's full name in the same passage, phrased differently each time. If a nickname hasn't appeared for a while, remind readers who it is with a quick tag. English readers can't tell Chinese names and nicknames apart without help.
- Game scenes: readable for non-gamers. Tell matches through feelings, pressure, choices and a few key moments: a short, sharp play-by-play of the decisive fight, then the players' reactions. Fights read like action scenes. Allow one or two technical details per match (a timing, an item choice, a draft trick), each explained in one line, woven into the narration. Never announce them (no "here's the nerd part", "for the non-gamers", "quick explanation"). Never list full item builds or long stat numbers. Skip or summarize dull games.
- Lin Chen's edge is knowledge, reading people and game sense, not hands: his 15-year-old fingers are slow at first and improve with training. Show the gap.
- Chapter shape: each chapter has a clear objective, a turn, and ends on the outline's hook. Don't end on an ominous one-liner more than two chapters in a row; some endings are warm, funny, quiet, or a small decision.
- Promises, progress, payoff: every chapter moves at least one open promise forward (a thread, a match, a relationship, the mystery). Plant before you pay off. Don't open new mysteries faster than old ones advance.
- Loop clues: plant them small and deniable, as flavor readers can skip (a combo his hands know, déjà vu, a dream, Mei's odd look, Fang Hu catching his breath). Never point at them with narration ("This would matter later").
- Time and money on the page: Lin Chen pays Starlight by the hour (2 yuan) and tracks every fen. When a scene at the cafe runs past what he paid for, say so (he pays more, Mei lets it run, he stands and watches). Keep clock times and money consistent with the state's numbers.
- Era accuracy: 2006-2012 China. Novia phones, SMS, QiQi chat, CRT monitors, ADSL, cash, MP3 players, DVDs. Nothing appears before its time (see the world bible). Prices in yuan as listed there.
- Real events (the 2008 earthquake, the financial crisis): treat with weight and respect. No jokes about the dead, no disaster spectacle; focus on people, small acts and aftermath.
- Describing people: give main and secondary characters a clear, specific look when they first appear, colored by the POV character's attitude. Vary the method from person to person; never use the same formula (skin, then face, then eyes) twice in a row. Pick the one to three details that make this person different, from a wide range: build and height, posture, the way they move or stand, hands, hair, clothes and how they wear them, voice, smell, what they carry, a habit, an injury or mark, how other people react to them, what they remind the POV character of. Skin tone is optional, mentioned only when it matters or is noticeable, in plain words (light, tan, brown, pale, sunburned, weathered); do not use the word "undertone". Never compare skin to food or drink. Some people are described mostly through one striking thing and almost nothing else; some through action rather than a still picture; some only later, a little at a time. Background characters get one quick detail or none. No self-portraits of the POV character (no mirror catalogues); show Lin Chen's look through others' reactions and small physical details.
- Known places and people: readers already know the recurring settings (Starlight's main room and back room, Grandma's apartment, the market, the school, the noodle shop) and the main cast. Do not describe them again or re-explain names and nicknames (who someone is, what everybody calls him). Mention at most one detail that is new or that matters to this scene, then get on with it.
- Romance: none in the early arcs. Lin Chen sees the teens as kids. Xu Qing's and Alina's crushes are played for comedy, and he reacts like an embarrassed uncle. Nothing romantic or sexual involving anyone under 18.
- Never: stat screens, system windows or game-like progress numbers for Lin Chen; info-dump lectures (reveal history through conflict and scene); "little did he know" foreshadowing; idiot villains who exist to be humiliated; harem dynamics; ending a chapter by waking from a dream; using italics for Lin Chen's thoughts (use plain text; italics only for emphasis, game text on screen, or clearly introduced written notes).
