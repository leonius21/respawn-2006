# Chapter 17: The Arithmetic Page

The fan was still pointed at Xu Qing's chair.

Nobody had turned it back. It had spent the whole night and all of Saturday aimed at an empty folding chair, ruffling nothing, while the Uncle Kong boxes in the corner sat in the heat and waited. When she came into the back room at ten past five with her schoolbag on both shoulders, she looked at the fan, then at the chair, then at me.

"It was like that when I got here," I said.

"I didn't ask."

She sat down in the breeze anyway. Then she unloaded the table: a stack of papers squared to the edge, a red pen, a pencil, an eraser cut clean in half so it would have a sharp corner, and the green notebook. She had a watch with a cracked plastic strap, and she unbuckled it and laid it face up beside the papers, where everyone could see the second hand.

"Saturday," she said. "Grade check."

Big Bird arrived at five-fifteen smelling so strongly of beef broth that the room seemed to grow a second kitchen. He still had his apron on. He said his father had let him go because the hour between lunch and dinner was dead anyway, and that he had to be back at the shop by a quarter to seven to carry up Mei's bowl, and that he was very hungry, which had not been part of the question.

Fang Hu came last, at a run, red scarf twisted halfway round his neck, schoolbag bouncing. He dropped onto the noodle carton that had become his seat sometime in the last week and pulled out a crumpled exercise book and a sheet of composition paper folded into quarters.

"I did it," he announced. "All of it. Ask me anything."

"I will," said Xu Qing.

She took me first, which was worse than going last. My Friday quiz lay in front of her with Teacher Gao's red marks all over it, the ink so heavy in places it had soaked through to the back. Sixty-one. In the other life I had never once looked at a quiz long enough to see a number on it; I'd folded them into paper planes or let them go soft in my pants pocket until Grandma washed them into pulp.

Xu Qing read every line. She turned the page over to read the soak-through.

"Question four," she said. "Factoring. You wrote the correct method."

"Su Wen. Wednesday."

"And then you wrote the wrong number at the end."

"That part was me."

She made a small mark in the notebook. I tried to see it upside down and couldn't. "Sixty-one is passable," she said. "Top twenty-five is not passable. It is twenty-five." She slid the quiz back to me. "Pending."

"That word's doing a lot of work for you."

"It's a hardworking word."

Then she pulled out two sheets of graph paper and laid one each in front of Big Bird and Fang Hu. She had written twelve arithmetic problems by hand in rows so straight they could have been printed: fractions, decimals, one word problem about a train. At the top of each sheet, in small neat characters: *Sixth-grade level. Twenty minutes.*

Big Bird read the top line twice.

"Sixth grade," he said. "I'm sixteen."

"Then it will be quick."

"He's in sixth grade." Big Bird pointed at Fang Hu with a pencil that looked like a toothpick in his fist. "We have the same paper. That's an insult to one of us."

"Yes," said Fang Hu happily. "You."

Xu Qing turned the watch toward herself. "Begin."

For four minutes the only sounds were the fan, the main room's clicking through the wall, and Fang Hu breathing through his mouth while he worked. He wrote fast and badly, pressing hard, and every time he finished a problem he was sure of, he drew a tiny tiger face next to it. Two ears, two dots, a line for a mouth. By the fourth minute he had three tigers.

Big Bird had none. He'd written the number 1 next to question one and then underlined it, as if the underline might help. He hummed. He shifted his weight, and the stool complained. He looked at the ceiling, where there was nothing, then at Xu Qing, who was writing in her notebook and not looking at him, and then, very slowly, at Fang Hu's paper.

Fang Hu felt it. I watched him feel it: the tiny stiffening of an eleven-year-old who has just realized he holds power over somebody six times his size. He didn't cover his paper. He moved his arm out of the way, a careful inch, and kept writing, and his tigers got a little bigger.

At eleven minutes Big Bird was keeping pace with him. At fifteen he was ahead.

"Time," said Xu Qing, at twenty minutes exactly.

She took the two sheets and laid them side by side. She looked from one to the other. Then she held Big Bird's up to the light from the doorway, and I saw what she saw: down the right side of his paper, next to the answers, a column of small, shaky tiger faces.

"Zhao Dapeng," she said.

"I like tigers."

"Since when?"

"Since about ten minutes." He crossed his arms over the apron. "They're nice. They're friendly animals."

"They are not friendly animals." She put her finger on the seventh problem. Two-fifths plus one-half. Fang Hu had written nine-tenths and drawn a tiger. Big Bird's paper said *three-sevenths*, and had no tiger, because at question seven Fang Hu, having apparently decided that power should be used once and not wasted, had put his whole elbow over his answer.

"He moved his arm," Big Bird said, betrayed.

"I was itchy," said Fang Hu.

"For one question?"

"It was a very itchy question."

Xu Qing went pink along the tops of her ears, which I had learned in the last two weeks meant that her temper was arriving and her vocabulary was about to put on a uniform to meet it. She turned her chair a few degrees so she faced Big Bird directly. The fan lifted the end of her ponytail and set it down.

"You added the tops," she said, "and then you added the bottoms."

"Yeah."

"You cannot add the bottoms. The bottom is the number of pieces the whole has been divided into. Two-fifths is two pieces of something cut into five. One-half is one piece of something cut into two. They are not the same size of piece. You cannot add pieces that are not the same size of piece." She uncapped the red pen. "You must first convert both to a common denominator, which in this case is ten, so two-fifths becomes four-tenths and one-half becomes five-tenths, and then—" She stopped, because Big Bird was staring at her, and she seemed to hear how she sounded. Her chin came up. "This is not tutoring. This is the correction of an error."

The room waited for Big Bird to get loud. He usually got loud when people used more than three long words in a row at him; I'd seen him do it to his father's menu supplier and to a vending machine.

He didn't. He uncrossed his arms and put both hands flat on the table, on either side of the paper, and leaned over it.

"Again," he said.

Xu Qing blinked. "What?"

"Say it again. The pieces part." He frowned at the paper. "Slower. I got lost at the bottom."

She looked at me. It was a short look, quick and suspicious, the look of somebody checking whether she was being made fun of. I kept my face exactly where it was. Fang Hu, on his carton, had stopped swinging his legs.

Xu Qing turned back. She took the pencil instead of the red pen. On the margin of his paper she drew a circle and cut it into five slices with four short lines, and shaded two of them. Next to it she drew another circle and cut it in half.

"Two pieces of this," she said. Her voice was still formal, but it had slowed down, as if each word were being put on the table separately. "One piece of this. Which piece is bigger?"

"That one." He pointed at the half.

"So can you call them both 'a piece'?"

He thought about it. You could watch him think; Big Bird thought with his whole forehead. "No," he said. "That's cheating. It's like if a customer orders a big bowl and you charge him small."

"...Yes," said Xu Qing, after a moment, sounding as if she'd been handed something she hadn't ordered. "That is exactly it."

She cut both circles again, into tenths, lines crossing lines, until the margin looked like a spiderweb. She shaded four little slices in the first and five in the second. Big Bird counted them out loud, with his finger. Nine. He wrote *9/10* over his three-sevenths so hard the pencil tore the graph paper a little.

Then he did question eight by himself. It took him almost three minutes and he muttered the whole time, and when he was done, he pushed the paper at her without a word.

She checked it. It was right.

He looked at the paper for a while. Then, carefully, his tongue in the corner of his mouth, he drew a tiger face next to it. His tiger was much worse than Fang Hu's. One ear was bigger than the whole head.

In the other life, nobody ever explained anything to Big Bird twice. They said it once, loudly, and then they called him a rice bucket and did it themselves. I had been one of them. I'd been the worst of them, probably, because I was faster and I liked people to see it.

Xu Qing wrote in her notebook. I tried to read it upside down and this time I could: *Zhao Dapeng — fractions (in progress)*. Not pending. In progress.

"Fang Hu," she said. "Chinese."

He handed it over without his usual noise. The exercise book first, with a page of copied characters, each one written ten times in a column, the later ones leaning tiredly against the earlier ones. She checked them stroke by stroke and found two mistakes and showed him, and he fixed them with his eraser and his tongue out, just like Big Bird. Then she unfolded the composition paper.

It was the green-gridded kind, a box for every character, three hundred boxes to a page. The assignment was written across the top in a teacher's hand: *Essay: My Father. At least 300 characters.*

Xu Qing read it, and then she sat still, and then she slid it across the table, not to Fang Hu, but in front of me, without saying anything. I don't think she knew why she did it. I don't think I'd have known either.

Two lines, in the first row of boxes, in pencil pressed so hard it shone:

*My father is a policeman. He is at work.*

Below that, two hundred and eighty-something empty green squares, all the way to the bottom of the page.

I read it twice. The second time took longer than the first, though there was nothing more to read.

Fang Hu was watching me. Not the paper. Me. He had his hands tucked under his thighs on the carton and he was waiting for something, a joke, or a correction, or the word *short*, and I had all three ready, the way you always have them ready, the tired grown-up's little kit. I didn't open it.

I folded the paper back along its old creases, into quarters, and put it in front of him, squared to the edge of the table, the way Xu Qing squared things.

He took it and put it in his schoolbag. Nobody said anything about three hundred characters.

Xu Qing wrote one word in her notebook. I didn't try to read it.

At six, through the wall, I heard the main room's noise shift. Monk's shift had started; there was the clank of a mop bucket and the scrape of the counter stool. My free hour on machine 14 began right then, without me in it. Mei stuck her head through the doorway, cigarette first.

"Chopstick. Fourteen's free and empty. That's the most expensive kind of free there is."

"I'm busy."

"You're sitting." She looked over the table: the graph paper, the spiderweb circles, Big Bird drawing his second tiger with enormous care. "What is this, a school? Schools pay rent."

"It's a grade check," said Xu Qing.

"Seven o'clock, it's a grade check that pays for chairs." She looked at Fang Hu last, and a moment longer than at the rest of us, and then she was gone, and we heard her slap a tile down at the counter and say "Peng" to nobody, just to say it.

I didn't go out to fourteen. Big Bird did questions nine to twelve with Xu Qing beside him, saying "again" twice more, quieter each time, and she said each thing again without the word *tutoring* in it anywhere. Fang Hu fell asleep sitting up on the carton for about four minutes and woke up and pretended he hadn't. The fan turned its face from side to side and kept coming back to her.

At six-forty Big Bird stood up. "Bowl," he said. "Dad counts." He folded the graph paper into his apron pocket instead of leaving it, and went down the stairs at only two at a time, which for him was practically tiptoeing.

At six-fifty the light in the doorway changed. Out on the main street the lamps had come on, that thin orange that made everything look like an old photograph of itself.

"Hu Zi," I said. "Corner."

He didn't argue. That was new too.

The street was Saturday-evening loud: a skewer cart with its charcoal smoking, motorbikes nosing between people, a radio in the hardware shop playing opera to nobody. Fang Hu walked half a step ahead of me with his schoolbag thumping his back, talking about Big Bird's tigers, about how Big Bird's tigers looked like potatoes, about how he was going to charge Big Bird one jiao per tiger next week, with a receipt.

"You let him copy," I said.

"Only six." He kicked a bottle cap. "Seven was mine."

The rice shop had its shutter half down and a dog asleep in the gap. The corner was where the main street dropped off a high curb onto the lane that ran back toward his building. Every evening I stood here and watched him turn.

He stepped down off the curb, and stopped.

It was a second. Less. His hand came up and flattened against his chest, on the left, right where the inside pocket of the jacket was, where he kept the receipt. His other hand went out, toward nothing, and found my sleeve and held on. His face went a strange still color under the streetlight, like a picture somebody had stopped halfway through drawing.

"Hu Zi."

Then he laughed. It came out of him all at once, a little too high. "Stood up too fast."

"You were walking."

"Walked too fast, then." He let go of my sleeve. He patted the pocket, twice, flat, the same pat as last night, and grinned with the gap in his teeth. "Saw stars, Ge. Free ones. Mei can't charge for them."

"Did you eat?"

"I'm going to eat. That's where I'm going." He was already backing away down the lane, half-running, the oversized jacket flapping around his knees. "My dad left money. On the table. He's at—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. I'd read it.

He ran. Ten steps, fifteen, his shadow stretching and folding under the lamps. Halfway down the lane, by the bicycle repair stall, he stopped and turned around and threw his whole arm up over his head and waved it, back and forth, wide as a flag, the red scarf flying loose at his neck.

My hand was already up. I don't know when I'd lifted it. It was just there, over my head, going back and forth in the orange light on a street corner in Qingshui, and I left it there until he'd turned again and gone.
