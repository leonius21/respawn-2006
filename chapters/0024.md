# Chapter 24: Bowls

Xu Qing knocked on Grandma's door at seven forty on Wednesday morning. I knew the time because Grandma announced it.

"Seven forty," she said from her chair by the window, without turning her head. "Your monitor's on the stairs. What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"Then she's early."

She was. Xu Qing stood on the landing in a white blouse with the creases still in the sleeves, ponytail tight, notebook under one arm and the red envelope tucked inside its cover. It was golden week. Half the town was still in pajamas. She looked ready to inspect a factory.

"Good morning, Lin Nainai. I apologize for the hour." Then, to me, at a lower and much less apologetic volume: "Starlight is closed until Friday. The tournament is in eleven days. We are sixty-three yuan forty short. I have made inquiries."

"How do you know where I live?"

"The class address list. The monitor is entitled to it."

"For emergencies."

"This is an emergency of sixty-three yuan forty."

Her inquiries, it turned out, had been thorough. The tofu man had nephews home for the holiday and needed nobody. The recycling station paid by the kilo, and she had calculated how many kilos and then declined to say the number out loud. Zhao Beef Noodles, however, had lost its dishwasher, a woman everyone called Sister Pan, to her home village until the seventh, and the Party secretary's niece's wedding was tomorrow, which meant relatives arriving today, hungry, from three counties.

"Zhao Dapeng informed me on Monday," Xu Qing said. "He said, quote, 'Dad's dying.' I established that he meant busy."

"Lao Zhao pays?" Grandma asked.

"That is the subject of the negotiation."

Grandma made a noise through her nose that could have meant anything, then pointed her cane at me. "Go. Stall stays shut today."

"Your knee—"

"My knee is sitting. Your father calls at noon. I'm not shouting family business in front of Qiu Fang." She turned back to the window. "And tell Lao Zhao his soup is too salty."

---

Fang Hu was already on the step of Zhao Beef Noodles when we got there, sitting on his hands in his father's old jacket with the sleeves rolled three times.

"Ge! Big Ge said there's money, is it true there's money, how much, do I get some, I can carry four bowls, I practiced at home with the rice bowls but only two because the third one—"

"Broke."

"It didn't break. It's resting. Under the bed."

The shop was narrow, six tables deep, with a red sign over the door and one of its white characters faded to pink. Inside it smelled of star anise and beef fat and the chili oil that had soaked into the tables over twenty years, and at the back, through a square hatch, a pot the size of a bathtub had been breathing since before dawn.

I knew one thing about this shop that nobody in it did. In 2022 there had been a Zhao Beef Noodles at the Deyang bus station, same red sign with newer paint, and another one in Qingshui by the new senior school, with a plastic awning and a photo of a bowl in the window. I'd walked past both and never gone in to ask whose they were. Standing in the doorway now, I looked at the pink character and kept my mouth shut.

Lao Zhao, Zhao Guoqiang, was at the chopping board slicing brisket. He was a small man with forearms too big for the rest of him and a face that had stopped expecting much from anybody around 1990. He didn't look up.

Xu Qing went to the counter and stood very straight.

"Uncle Zhao. Good morning. I am Xu Qing, monitor of class three and treasurer of the Starlight extracurricular group. I'm here about Sister Pan."

"Went home."

"Yes. I'm offering two replacements. Lin Chen, washing. Fang Hu, orders. From ten until closing. Fourteen yuan."

The knife paused. Lao Zhao looked at Fang Hu.

"That one's eleven."

"Eleven and almost," said Fang Hu.

"Food," Lao Zhao said.

"Food is not wages."

"Ten."

"Twelve," said Xu Qing. "In cash. With a receipt."

"Receipt for what."

"For labor performed."

"A washed bowl doesn't get a receipt."

They looked at each other across the counter. Lao Zhao didn't lose. He just went back to the brisket.

"Twelve."

"Then I will write a receipt," Xu Qing said, "and you will not sign it."

He didn't answer. She opened her notebook and wrote: *Twelve. Cash. Receipt (disputed).*

---

Big Bird was at the pot.

I'd known Zhao Dapeng in two lives. I'd heard him shout across Starlight at midnight about spicy strips, shout at his monitor, shout at Streak's mother through Streak, shout in his sleep. When we came in, he looked through the hatch, nodded at me once, and went back to the noodles without a word.

I thought he might be sick.

The washing station was out the back door, under a corrugated roof: three red plastic basins, a cold faucet, a kettle for hot water, a bar of yellow soap worn into a saddle. The bowls came out in towers. For the first hour I washed them like a man who'd spent his thirties eating out of plastic boxes, because that's what I was. My fingers were slow and pruned within twenty minutes.

Lao Zhao came out with an empty tray and watched me rinse one bowl.

"Cold noodle," he said. "Move."

I moved.

By eleven the wedding relatives had found us. Cousins from Mianyang in good shoes, uncles who'd ridden motorbikes in from the hills with dust to their knees, a table of aunties who all ordered the same thing and then argued about who'd ordered first. Fang Hu ran orders at full volume.

"TWO BIG BEEF, ONE SMALL, NO CORIANDER, ONE—AUNTIE, WAS YOURS THE CORIANDER OR THE NO CORIANDER?"

He gave the wrong bowls to the wrong tables about half the time. It didn't matter. The customers passed them along hand to hand until everybody had the right one, and an uncle at the window table tipped him a five-jiao coin for the entertainment, which he showed me through the back door like a gold medal.

Every time I carried a stack in, I looked at the hatch.

Big Bird worked the pot with a long bamboo strainer. Noodles in. His lips moved, counting something. Noodles out, two shakes, into the bowl. Broth from the ladle, one pour, never a second. Beef, chili oil, scallions, the towel off his shoulder to wipe the rim, slide it to the counter. By the time Fang Hu had shouted the next order, there were five bowls lined up and the sixth was already in the pot. Lao Zhao moved behind him with the beef, and Big Bird stepped left without looking, and Lao Zhao passed through the space where he'd been. They did that all lunch, in a kitchen as wide as a bed, without touching once and without saying a word.

I'd watched this boy charge alone into five enemy heroes because one of them had typed something rude. Here there were thirty hungry people and a father behind him, and he didn't charge at anybody.

At ten past twelve I came in with a bowl in each hand and saw the clock on the wall, a beer distributor's freebie with a cracked second hand. Somewhere across town a Novia was ringing, or wasn't, and Grandma was holding it away from her ear, or wasn't, and my father was saying something with his voice shaking, or not saying it.

"Cold noodle," Lao Zhao said behind me.

I moved.

---

Fang Hu burned himself just before one. He reached past Big Bird's elbow for a bowl and put his hand flat on the rim of the pot to steady himself. He snatched it back and stuck his finger in his mouth.

"Stood up too fast," he said around it.

He hadn't sat down since ten. It was the same thing he'd said at the curb two Saturdays ago, word for word, and I watched his other hand go to his jacket and come away again.

Big Bird put the strainer down, took the boy's wrist without a word, walked him two steps to the faucet and held his hand under the cold water. His lips moved again. Counting. Fang Hu squirmed, and Big Bird's big hand didn't.

"It's fine, burns are good for you."

"Who says?"

"Me."

At the chopping board, Lao Zhao watched them over the brisket for a second. Then he went back to slicing.

---

The dead hour came at half past two. Lao Zhao went out the back to smoke on an upturned crate beside my basins, and the instant his back was through the door, his son came back to life.

"LIN CHEN. DID YOU SEE. NINE BOWLS. I HAD NINE BOWLS ON THE COUNTER AT ONCE."

"I saw."

"DAD DIDN'T SAY ANYTHING."

"Is that good?"

"THAT'S THE BEST ONE."

Xu Qing, who'd spent the morning at the corner table with her English book open and her notebook beside it, logging bowls in tally marks, looked up. "Volume, Zhao Dapeng."

"I'VE BEEN QUIET FOR FOUR HOURS."

"Then you have practice."

He ate two bowls standing up. Fang Hu fell asleep with his cheek on the table and his burned finger held up in the air like it had a question.

I went out back. Lao Zhao was looking at the blank wall of the next building.

"My grandmother says your soup is too salty," I said.

He took a drag. "Good."

That was the whole conversation. I washed the lunch bowls I'd missed.

---

Dinner was worse than lunch and better, because I'd learned where the bowls wanted to go, and my hands had stopped arguing with the soap. Fang Hu lost his voice at seven and kept shouting anyway, in a croak. By the time the last uncle had gone, wiping his mouth on his sleeve and promising to come back for breakfast, it was ten past eight and my fingers looked boiled.

Lao Zhao opened the till drawer and counted out our wages in coins. Aluminum jiao, brass five-jiao pieces, a few yuan coins, the whole day's change, slid across the counter one by one under his thumb.

Xu Qing took off her watch and laid it face up beside the coins, the cracked plastic strap flat against the wood. She counted in stacks of ten. Then she pushed the stacks together and counted them again.

"Eleven yuan ninety."

"Twelve," said Lao Zhao.

"Eleven yuan ninety. I counted twice."

Nobody said anything. The pot ticked as it cooled.

Big Bird came out from behind the hatch, wiping his hands on his apron. He dug in the apron pocket and came up with a jiao, and he put it on the counter next to the stacks without looking at anybody. His ears had gone red.

"It's mine. It's—I had it. It fell out of the—it's for the—"

He didn't finish. He stood there, enormous and scarlet, looking at the coin.

Lao Zhao looked at the coin for a long time. Then he looked at his son.

He nodded. Once.

Then he picked up Big Bird's jiao, put it back in the pocket of Big Bird's apron, took one of his own out of the drawer and set it on the end of the last stack.

"Twelve."

Xu Qing counted a third time. "Twelve."

She wrote out a receipt anyway, in full, with the date. Lao Zhao didn't take it, so she slid it under the chili-oil jar on the counter, where he'd see it every time he reached for the chili, and said nothing about it. Then she wrote a second one for Fang Hu, for six yuan, his half, because he asked, and he folded it into the inside left pocket of his jacket with the first one from September and patted the pocket twice.

The coins went into the red envelope. It was heavy now, and it clinked. Xu Qing opened her notebook on the counter and wrote, in her small square characters:

*Income: earned. Fund 23.60. Remaining 51.40.*

"Read it again," said Fang Hu.

"I have read it."

"Again, though."

She read it again.

Lao Zhao went to the pot, ladled something, and came back with a bowl. He put it in my hands without a word. It was from the bottom of the pot, broth gone dark, the last of the brisket on top. The bowl was almost too hot to hold.

He wiped his hands on the towel and didn't look at me.

"My rice bucket comes home every night," he said. "Not many do."

Behind the hatch, Big Bird picked up the big pot's lid. It was already clean. He scrubbed it very hard with his back to all of us.
