# Chapter 31: What the Book Said

Grandma gave me two yuan on Tuesday morning for lunch. She held the coins out of reach until I looked at her.

"Two buns. Not one. I've seen your wrists."

I bought none. Before she was up I'd packed last night's rice into the lunch box under a layer of her cabbage, and at noon I ate it cold on the steps behind the science building. The coins stayed in my left pocket by themselves, where nothing else could get at them. Once is a present, she'd told me at the stall. I decided this counted as once.

At six I put them on Starlight's counter. Mei swept them into the drawer with the side of her hand and didn't take her eyes off her tiles.

"Fourteen. Still three twenty."

"I know."

"Then sit down before somebody else does."

I didn't open Lianzhan. I opened the browser, waited for Baidoo's page to arrive from wherever Baidoo lived, and sat with my hands on the keys while the cursor blinked in the empty box.

In 2006 you couldn't just tell the internet what was wrong. You went to it with your cap in your hands and used whatever words it had. Child. Heartbeat. Fast. I typed *skips*, deleted it, typed *stops for one beat*, and hit enter.

The line thought about it. Results came down the screen in strips, top first. A question on Baidoo Knows from a mother in Henan whose son's heart "jumped like a frog" after PE, eleven answers, the best one marked with a little gold cup. A forum for traditional medicine. A health portal with a banner for a Chengdu men's hospital that flashed red and gold twice a second and promised results in three days or your money back. I clicked the portal, because it had a doctor's name at the bottom.

The paragraph was short. *In children, a rapid pulse is common after running, with fever, or with nervousness and excitement. Occasional skipped beats are usually harmless. If accompanied by dizziness, fainting or shortness of breath, see a doctor promptly.*

I read it three times. By the third time I was only reading *promptly*.

None of it was news. I'd read some version of that paragraph at two in the morning in a Shanghai hotel, about my own chest, after four cans of Red Ox and a loss I didn't want to think about. It had said the same thing then: probably nothing, go find an adult with a stethoscope. Every page on the subject ended there. It sent you to a parent and a doctor, and I was neither, and the parent in this case raided net cafes for a living.

"Mei, the stool's MINE, I sat on it Sunday—"

"You sat on it Sunday. It's Tuesday."

I clicked back to the search box and typed the first thing I'd rather be caught looking at: *stocks that go up*. Baidoo found me a forum thread six hundred posts deep. The first post said buy steel. The second said steel was finished and the first man was a liar. By post forty they had moved on to the second man's mother, and somebody in Wuhan was selling a book.

Fang Hu arrived at my shoulder, smelling of the cold. "Ge. Are you getting rich?"

"Looking."

"What's a stock?"

"Paper. Sometimes it goes up."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows. There's six hundred people on here and none of them know."

"Why, though?"

When nothing on the screen moved he lost interest and went back to the war over the stool. I typed BITCON, because I had the word and a feeling and nothing else. Baidoo told me politely that no pages containing it existed and asked if I'd like to check my spelling.

At twenty to seven I logged off and went to the counter.

"Eighteen minutes left on fourteen," Mei said.

"Give them to Hu Zi."

Fang Hu came off the stool so fast it rocked. Mei looked at me longer than eighteen minutes were worth.

"He's out at nine."

"It's twenty to seven."

"I can read my own clock."

He played on his knees, the way he always did. I stood behind him and didn't watch the screen. I watched the back of his neck and his ears, which were pink from the stairs and stayed pink, and his hands, which didn't stop. When the time ran out he turned around outraged.

"That was like five minutes. Mei's minutes are short."

"Mei's minutes are the same as everybody's."

"Then why do they feel short?"

Teacher Gao gave class 3 the last two periods on Wednesday for review. On Monday Xu Qing had filed a written request with the librarian for the long table by the window, "for the purposes of a study group," and the librarian, who I suspect had never had anything filed with her, said yes out of surprise.

The library was on the third floor of the old building: glass-fronted cabinets, a smell of paste and damp paper, a wall clock with a cracked face. In my first life I'd come up here once, in eighth grade, to sleep through a thunderstorm. When I came back in 2022 the old building was gone and the spot was a parking lot for teachers' electric scooters.

There were six of us. Xu Qing at the head with her watch flat on the table. Su Wen and me with math. Two girls from the front row who'd asked to join and then actually studied. And Big Bird, who'd walked in through the front gate because the gatekeeper still remembered him from June and waved. Xu Qing had brought him her own old ninth-grade Chinese book.

"The zhongkao comes around every June," she'd told him, "whether or not you are ready for it."

"Dad said go," he whispered as he sat down, at about the volume most people use to order across a restaurant. "It's the dead hour."

The librarian looked up. He put both hands over his mouth and nodded at her until she looked down again.

Su Wen tapped my paper. "Question six."

Question six was a quadratic that had done nothing to me personally. I did it wrong, and Su Wen pointed to the line where it went wrong without saying anything, and I did it again. At twenty-nine minutes past three Big Bird's head went down onto Du Fu. Su Wen glanced at the clock and wrote the time in his margin.

At twenty to four I went to the shelves. Medicine was the bottom row of the last cabinet, and the books there had been left alone since before I was born. A brown cloth spine said *Home Medical Guide*. Inside the cover was a stamp from the county education bureau and the date 1988. I carried it back and put it under my math.

The print was tiny and the index went by stroke count, and I lost my place twice. Su Wen watched me turn the same dozen pages back and forth.

"Su Wen. Find where it talks about a pulse that skips."

He took the book. He didn't ask. He found the index, ran his finger down it, and opened to page 214 in less time than it had taken me to fail, and then he read it to me in a library voice, flat, each sentence set down like a line of working.

"Premature beats. Common in children and adolescents. The majority are functional and are not caused by organic heart disease. They may be brought on by fatigue, emotional excitement, or strong tea." He didn't pause where a person would. He paused at the commas, exactly as long as each one asked. "Where premature beats are frequent, or are accompanied by—" He turned the page, because that was where the sentence went. "—dizziness, fainting, or chest pain on exertion, the patient should be taken promptly to hospital for examination. Listening to the heart and an electrocardiogram are advised."

Xu Qing checked everyone's work at the quarter hours. She leaned over at a quarter to four to check mine, and her eyes went to Su Wen's finger instead. It was resting under *fainting*. She read it. She sat back, opened the green notebook, wrote something short, and closed it with the pen inside. Nobody said anything. One of the front-row girls turned a page.

"What does it say to watch for?" I said.

Su Wen ran his finger back up a line. "Dizziness. Fainting. Chest pain on exertion."

"Exertion."

"Effort."

He waited a moment to see if there was another question. There wasn't. He closed the book, squared its corners with the table's edge, slid it to my side, and set my math on top of it.

"Question nine."

I did question nine. Somewhere in the middle of it I went back over what the book had given me. Tiredness, excitement, tea. A boy sitting on his knees at minute twenty-two of a game, gone pale, telling me he'd stood up too fast. The stars in September that he'd said were free. What was left on the page came down to three questions. *Has he ever fainted. Has anyone listened to his heart. Does anyone in the family.* They were questions for whoever signed his forms.

The book could tell me what to ask. It had nothing on how a ninth grader asks a policeman about his son without the policeman asking how the ninth grader knows to.

At ten to five Big Bird sat up with the edge of the page printed across his cheek in a red line. He looked down at the poem under him, blinked at it, and announced to the room, "HE'S SAD ABOUT THE WAR."

Su Wen looked at the clock. "Eighty-one minutes."

Big Bird was right about the poem. The librarian set her pen down very carefully. One of the front-row girls laughed into her sleeve.

"Correct," said Xu Qing. "Quieter."

At the gate she stopped me with the notebook held flat against her chest in both hands.

"Building two, fourth floor, seven o'clock. You will be paid before you open the case."

"I'd already decided that."

"Then it has been decided twice." She looked at me a moment longer, as though she were going to add something. Then she went the other way, toward the bridge, at her usual pace.

I walked home along the river. October had taken the water down to its stones; gravel bars stood up in the middle like the backs of animals, and a man had driven his motorbike into the shallows and was washing it with a bucket and a rag. The light over the hills went orange and then went gray without asking anyone. By the time I reached the rice shop on the corner the woman inside had the shutter half down and was sweeping spilled grains out of the doorway with a short broom. In the window hung the same calendar Grandma had on her wall, open to October, a red pagoda over the dates.

Then I knew I had sat here before.

I don't mean walking past, or waiting at this corner for Fang Hu to finish talking. I mean sitting, on this stretch of curb, with rain coming off the shop's awning and running down the back of my collar into my shirt, waiting for someone. And they hadn't come. That was all there was. No face. No year. I couldn't have said whether I'd waited an hour or all night. I only knew the waiting had ended without anyone arriving, and that I'd been cold, and that I'd kept watching the corner anyway.

I stopped walking. After a moment I sat down on the real curb.

It was dry. The concrete still held a little of the afternoon, and there was rice dust in the cracks. Nothing was falling off the awning. Across the street a boy pushed his bicycle past with a sack of coal on the rack, and a television in somebody's apartment was playing the weather.

Déjà vu, I told myself. The brain files a new thing in an old folder and then makes you feel stupid about it. I'd slept about five hours. Midterms started tomorrow. I'd spent the afternoon reading about hearts in a book older than me. In my first life I must have sat on fifty curbs in this town, some of them in the rain. None of that explained who I'd been waiting for.

The woman with the broom leaned out under the shutter. "You want rice?"

"No, Ayi. Just sitting."

She looked at me a second, then went back in and pulled the shutter the rest of the way down.

Friday, then. After the last exam, when there was nothing left to hide behind. I'd find the officer on the walk, or at the station if I had to, and ask him what the book said to ask. Has your son ever fainted. Has anyone listened to his heart. No story wrapped around it. Just the question, and then I'd stand there and let him look at me.
