# Chapter 15: Qingshui Laoren

The note had been in my shirt pocket for a day, and it was already going soft at the folds.

I'd found it at six on Wednesday, square in the middle of machine 14's mouse pad, folded so small it looked like somebody had tried to make it disappear and lost their nerve halfway. Pink receipt paper. No name. *Sundays, 8 p.m. Machine 38. One try a week. Don't be late. Bring your brain.* There was exactly one person in Qingshui who would order me to bring my brain, and she sat in a gray hood at 38 pretending her voice had broken.

So Sunday at eight I had a duel. Sunday at eight also cost two yuan. On Thursday afternoon, halfway up Starlight's stairs, I had ninety fen. I counted it on the landing out of pure habit, and from the top of the stairs Boss Mei said, "Ninety," without looking up from her tiles.

"Good afternoon to you too."

"It's not a good afternoon. You're poor in it."

I didn't argue. I went to the windowsill by the stairs, where Mei let magazines go to die. The pile was a year deep and swollen like bread. Most of it was *Game Frontline*, the Beijing monthly every net cafe in China read cover to cover, then read again, then used as a coaster. The October issue sat on top. It had been in the building three days. Somebody had already torn out the poster, and somebody else had already eaten noodles over the hero reviews.

I didn't want the reviews. I flipped to the back, past the readers' letters and an ad for a gaming mouse that promised to make you a great god for eighty-nine yuan, and found the box I'd forgotten I knew about.

*CALL FOR STRATEGY: AotA. We pay 100 yuan per thousand characters. Payment by postal money order after publication. Include real name, mailing address and postal code.*

In my other life I must have seen that box a hundred times. I'd been seventeen, nineteen, twenty-two, and busy being the best player in whatever room I was in. People who wrote for magazines were people who couldn't play. That was the whole of my thinking on the subject, for about fifteen years.

I did the sum standing up. Two thousand characters, two hundred yuan. Grandma's stall made a few hundred in a month of standing in the damp. And then I did the other sum, the one about time. If they liked it, it would go in December at the earliest. "After publication" meant weeks after that, an envelope crawling down from Beijing on a train.

So it wouldn't buy us a tournament. It might buy a winter.

I sat on the back-room sofa with the issue on my knee, moved my leg off the spring that bit, and dug the calendar pages out of my schoolbag. Seventeen of them, one for every morning since I'd started tearing the thing myself, held together with a rubber band. Map sketches on the backs, arrows, little circles where runes went. I turned them over to the clean parts and started writing.

At six, Mei leaned into the doorway. "Your free hour started."

"I'm not using it."

She looked at me as if I'd announced I was going to stop breathing to save air. "Free things are still things, chopstick." She left machine 14 empty anyway. Nobody else sat in it.

The writing went fast for about a page and a half. I knew this lane. A rune appears in the river on every even minute of the game clock, and if you're not standing on the spot when it does, someone else is. You don't have to kill every creep you can reach: hit only the last-hits and the fight stays near your own tower, where the other mid has to walk farther to bully you and you have farther to run. Simple things. The kind of simple that took me two years and a lot of dying to learn the first time.

Then I got to the word for ambush and my hand stopped.

I knew the word. I'd said it ten thousand times. But I'd spent sixteen years letting a keyboard remember the strokes for me, and the keyboard wasn't here. I had a ballpoint and half a character with a hole in the middle.

"That's not a character," Su Wen said behind me.

He'd come in through the green back door from the alley, quiet as a coat on a hook, his schoolbag held against his chest. My classmate, top of class 3, the family's gaokao hope, who was supposed to be somewhere else at all times.

"My father has evening duty on Thursdays," he said, before I could ask. "Until eight. I'm in the library."

"You're always in the library."

"Mostly I am." He sat down on the far end of the sofa, the end without the spring, and held out his hand. I gave him the pages. He read the first one. He read the second one. He took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirt, put them back on, and read the second one again.

"This isn't Chinese," he said.

"It's Sichuan."

"It isn't that either. 'Then you just kind of go.' Go where? When?"

"When it feels right."

"It can't feel right in Beijing. They can't see you." He pulled a pad of composition paper out of his bag, the green-gridded kind every kid in China filled with essays about their most unforgettable teacher. Four hundred squares a page, one character a square. "Readers like numbers," he said. "Numbers look true."

So he made them numbers. One: the rune clock. Two: why the wave should sit near your tower. Three: when to leave the lane. He wrote in small, upright characters that didn't lean on each other, and every few lines he asked me a question and wrote down the answer in a better shape than I'd said it. He fixed "ambush" without comment. He fixed two more characters I'd invented. His left hand tapped the sofa arm on a slow count, and at thirty it tapped harder, and I don't think he knew he was doing it.

At point three, he stopped.

"You say leave mid before the rune." He tapped the line. "You don't say how you know the other mid is going."

"No."

"On Sunday you knew."

"On Sunday I stood behind Streak's chair for three hours."

"Then write that."

"I did." I pointed. *Watch your opponent before you play him.* "That part they can have."

He looked at the line, then at me. "That's very little."

"It's what everybody in the country figures out in a year anyway."

That was true. It just wasn't the whole reason. Every boy in Thunder Net City read *Game Frontline*. Streak read it, moving his lips. And somewhere in Shanghai, Qin Shuo, the boy who would beat me in a final nine years from now, was fifteen too, and he would probably read it. I could give all of them the clock. I wasn't going to hand any of them my Sunday.

Su Wen wrote it down the way I'd said it. Then he counted squares. "One thousand five hundred and eighty-two," he said. "Including punctuation."

"Do they pay for punctuation?"

"It doesn't say." He looked troubled by this, as if Beijing had left a variable undefined.

The green door banged at a quarter past six.

"There is a man in that alley," Xu Qing said, "asleep on a bicycle."

The class monitor stood in the doorway with her school bag on both shoulders, ponytail exact, a red envelope pressed against her front. She'd come the back way. Qiu Fang watched the front stairs, and the class monitor of class 3 could not be reported to the tea house as a net cafe customer, for reasons of national security.

"You told her?" I said to Su Wen.

"I did not," Su Wen said.

"Nobody told me," said Xu Qing. "Su Wen bought three sheets of composition paper at the stationery shop at a quarter to five. The stationery shop is my aunt's. Rule three. Money is going to be spent."

"Has it been spent?" Xu Qing asked.

"Not yet."

"Good." She sat on an Uncle Kong box. It held. Then she looked out through the doorway, down the main room, to the corner by the window. Machine 38 was empty. The chair at 37 was pushed in, its monitor glowing faintly green.

"The hooded one," she said. "Typhoon. Does he come on Thursdays?"

"Typhoon comes when Typhoon comes."

"That's not a schedule." She opened the green notebook, wrote a line, and closed it before I could read it. I let the "he" stand. I'd watched what happened, the other time, when a whole cafe learned that Typhoon was a girl. I was in no hurry to see it again.

Su Wen explained the magazine. Xu Qing read the call for strategy twice, the second time with her finger under the words. "Paid when?"

"Weeks after it's printed."

"Printed when?"

"If they like it, December."

"So it does nothing for October fifteenth."

"Nothing."

She looked at the green-gridded pages, at the sum written in Su Wen's corner, at me. "Then it's an investment," she said, and wrote *Investment (pending)*. She counted the composition pages. "Four sheets. Five fen a sheet at Happy Student Stationery. Twenty fen." She took two jiao out of the red envelope and put them in Su Wen's hand.

"It's my paper," Su Wen said.

"Now it's the team's paper."

"It needs a name at the top," Su Wen said, folding the coins away. "An author."

"Not mine." I could see it already. *Lin Chen, Qingshui Junior Middle School, class 3, ranked thirty-fourth, explains the mid lane.* "Nobody takes advice from a ninth grader."

"They wouldn't believe it," Su Wen agreed.

"Exactly."

From behind the noodle boxes, where he'd been lying on his back and pretending not to listen, Fang Hu sat up. He was still in his primary school uniform, red scarf crooked, a scab on each knee. "Call it the old man."

"What?"

"You sound like an old man, Ge. You stand up like my grandpa. My grandpa's *dead*, and you still stand up worse." He tried it out. "Qingshui Laoren. The Old Man of Qingshui." He grinned with the gap in his teeth. "Old Man of Qingshui says go to the rune."

Su Wen tried it under his breath. Then he nodded, once, which for Su Wen was a standing ovation.

"That's a lie," Xu Qing said.

"It's a pen name."

"A pen name is a lie with a pen." She was looking at the four characters as if they'd cut in front of her in line. "He is fifteen. The readers will think he's seventy. They'll send him tea."

"I'd drink it."

I needed the stapler from the counter. I put my hands on my knees and stood up, and the noise came out of me before I could stop it, a long creak from somewhere around 2019.

Fang Hu pointed at me with both hands. "*That.*"

"Rule eleven," Xu Qing said.

"This isn't a meeting."

"Money is being spent. It's a meeting." She wrote something. She didn't cross out the name, though. Su Wen wrote *清水老人* at the top of the first page, very neatly, and the Old Man of Qingshui was born on an Uncle Kong box, in a back room that smelled of seasoning powder, over the objections of the class monitor.

Then there was the address.

I took the pages to the counter. Mei had a mahjong game going with the dried-goods woman from across the street and the man who sold lottery tickets, and she didn't look up.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked."

"You've got a letter and a face. No."

I explained anyway. A money order from Beijing to a ninth grader at Grandma's apartment: the mail carrier Old Wu's wife played mahjong with Grandma on Tuesdays and Fridays, so the whole street would know before lunch. And you needed an ID card to cash a money order. I wouldn't have one until I turned sixteen, in March, and by then the money would have gone back to Beijing out of boredom.

Mei laid down a tile. The lottery man swore at it.

"Whose name?" she said.

"Yours. Starlight's address."

"And when Beijing sends Mei Lan money for an old man?"

"You give it to the old man."

"Ten percent."

"Of what?"

"Of whatever comes. And if they send a rejection, I charge you to read it out." She finally looked at me. "What do I always tell you?"

"That you don't lie."

"And?"

"...You bill."

"Ten percent." She pulled an envelope out of the drawer under the counter, the one by the lock switch, wrote the cafe's address on the back without looking at anything, postal code and all, and slid it to me. "Envelope's one jiao."

Xu Qing had followed me out. She put one jiao on the counter. "Receipt."

Mei tore one off the pink pad without arguing, which I'd never seen her do for anyone, and scrawled *envelope 0.10* on it. The same pink paper as the note in my pocket. I didn't touch my pocket.

On the cover note, under *Author*, I wrote the pen name myself. My hand didn't stop once. Funny. I'd needed help with ambush, but these four went down like I'd written them a hundred times. Then again, *old* and *person* are easy characters. Even I can write *person*. It's two strokes.

Mei read it upside down. "Old man," she said, and looked at me a beat longer than the joke needed. "Suits you." Then she turned and shouted at Fang Hu to get out from behind her noodles before she shipped him to Beijing in the envelope.

The envelope wouldn't stick. They never did, back then. Monk, Tang Yi, the night-shift wangguan, was eating his dinner out of a steel box at the end of the counter, rice and pickled cabbage. Without a word he held out his chopsticks with one grain of rice balanced on the tips.

"Rice," he said.

I smeared it along the flap and pressed. It held better than glue.

The streetlights came on at seven. Su Wen went down the back way to be in the library before eight. Fang Hu came with me because I was walking him anyway, and Xu Qing came because stamps cost money and money needed a witness.

The postal newsstand sat at the corner of the market, painted the same green as the mailbox beside it. The woman inside wore green sleeve protectors and had reading glasses on a string. She weighed the envelope in her palm like a melon.

"Over," she said. "Two stamps."

"One yuan sixty," Xu Qing said, and paid it, and asked for a receipt. The woman looked at her for a long moment, then wrote one on the back of a delivery slip. Xu Qing folded it into the red envelope with the others. "Four yuan sixty left," she announced to the market. "We are further from the tournament than we were this morning."

"We're further from everything than we were this morning," I said. "That's how mornings work."

She almost wrote that down.

I licked the stamps and put them on crooked. Then I stood at the mailbox with the envelope halfway into the slot and didn't let go.

Fifteen hundred and eighty-two characters, punctuation included. Everything I'd ever known about the game had lived in my head, and the only way it ever got out was through my hands, on a screen, for me. I'd never given away any of it. Not to the teams I played on, not to the kids who asked. Now it was going to Beijing on a train, under an old man's name, where anybody with nine yuan for a magazine could have it. Streak. Thunder Net City. A fifteen-year-old in Shanghai.

Not the Sunday part, though. I'd kept that.

"Ge," Fang Hu said. "It's not going to jump in by itself."

I let go. It dropped onto whatever else was in there with a soft, paper sound, and the slot flap clicked shut.

Fang Hu stood up straight in his oversized jacket, heels together, and gave the mailbox a full Young Pioneer salute, right hand flat above his head, red scarf crooked at his throat.

"That salute is for the flag," Xu Qing said.

"It's for the old man," said Fang Hu, and held it until the woman at the newsstand laughed.
