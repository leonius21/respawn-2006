# Chapter 5: Zhao Beef Noodles

I didn't follow him in.

I knew what was behind that door. Every kid under eighteen in Qingshui learned it eventually: a narrow storeroom behind Starlight's counter, five machines older than the ones out front, a sofa with a spring that bit, Uncle Kong noodle boxes stacked to the ceiling, and a bare bulb Boss Mei never switched off. When the police came up the front stairs, she herded the minors into it and out the green door into the alley like ducks. On quiet nights she let a few regulars play back there with the door shut. These were people who didn't want to be seen: a married teacher from the senior school, a man dodging his brother-in-law's loan, once a girl who cried at her screen every Thursday and never explained why. At fifteen, the first time around, I'd wanted badly to be important enough for the back room.

What I didn't know was that Su Wen had ever been in it. I'd spent two years at Starlight and never once seen him there. Either he'd been very careful, or I'd been the kind of kid who only looked at screens. Probably both.

The thirty-one-year-old in me wanted to knock twice, sit down beside him and say, "Your rune grid is ten seconds off," then watch the best student in class 3 turn the color of exam paper. I'd known a manager in Shanghai who recruited like that, collecting his players' secrets. By spring every one of them hated him. You don't catch a boy like Su Wen. You let him find out you know, at a time he picks, and you act like it's weather.

Also, the alley smelled of beef bones, and I was fifteen, which meant I was hungry in a way that felt medical.

Grandma had mahjong at Auntie Pan's on Monday nights. That morning she'd unfolded three yuan from the handkerchief in her apron and pressed them into my hand. "Eat something with meat in it. Not those red strips. They're made of shoe." I put the bag of spicy strips in my pocket, out of sight, which wouldn't matter. She would know anyway.

That left three yuan for dinner and eight-ninety from a biscuit tin that had told me, in my own handwriting, not to touch it. My two-hours-a-day plan cost four yuan a day, so it was a two-day plan. I cut it to one hour. Adults call that managing expectations. At fifteen it's called being broke.

---

I went the long way, out of the alley and onto the main street, because I wanted to look.

That was the strange part of the week, stranger than my knees. Every face on that street came with a second face behind it. Old Tan from the hardware shop sat on his step filing a key, and I could see the phone-repair counter his daughter would put where his padlocks hung, with a pink neon sign that flickered on the R. Across the road, the barber was sweeping hair into the gutter with the broom he'd still be using in 2022, when he'd sweep sitting down. A boy from the vocational school went past on a bicycle with no brakes. Deng Wei. He'd get fat, sell insurance, and call me three times in the autumn of 2022 about a policy for a man with no income. I'd hung up on him twice.

It was like walking through a photo album somebody kept turning the wrong way.

Zhao Beef Noodles sat two doors down from the hardware shop. In 2022 it had a lightbox sign. Now it was a painted board, the character for "beef" fading into the wood, six folding tables, red plastic stools, a menu on the wall, and a TV in the corner playing a costume drama with the sound off. Steam rolled out of the kitchen hatch like weather.

I heard him before I saw him. I'd known I would.

"THREE SMALL BEEF! ONE NO CILANTRO! AUNTIE YANG WANTS HER CHILI ON THE SIDE BECAUSE OF HER PILES!"

At the table by the door, Auntie Yang from the cloth stall quietly died. Her son would move to Guangzhou and send her a massage chair, and she would use it to dry socks for a decade.

Zhao Dapeng, Big Bird to everyone, came through the bead curtain with three bowls along one forearm, chewing. A dapeng is the giant bird in the old stories, the one whose wings cover the sky, and he flapped his arms when he got excited, so the name had stuck from both ends. He was sixteen and built like a wardrobe somebody had taught to walk: a round face, tan, flushed ruddy from the pots, a buzz cut, a stained apron over a red basketball jersey that said WANG FEIHU 23. He put the bowls down with one hand and took a slice of beef off the cutting board with the other.

Hearing him at sixteen was like hearing an old song played by a school band. Everything was the same, and much too loud.

The last time I'd seen him, he'd been thirty-two and wider. His hairline was going, and he had a little girl's plastic butterfly clip pinned to his apron for reasons he never explained. I'd come in with one suitcase and the look of a man whose career had ended in a word other people used for choking. He'd stood up behind that counter and shouted, "SECOND! You came home!" as if I'd been gone a week and not fourteen years. He put extra beef in my bowl and wouldn't take my money. I let him think I was doing fine. I never asked how he was.

The first time around, I'd decided inside a week that he was a feeder, the kind of player who dies so often he's basically paying the enemy, and then I spent two years using him as a wall and screaming at him for being a bad one. When I left for Chengdu at seventeen, I didn't tell him. He heard it from somebody else.

"DAPENG." The voice from the kitchen was much smaller and much more frightening. "That beef is for customers."

"I'M TASTING IT."

"You've tasted three jin today."

He arrived at my table and looked down at me from a great height. "You're Grandma Lin's grandson. The pickle one."

"Small beef. Three yuan."

"She feeds the whole market and you look like a bean sprout. Is she doing it on purpose?"

At the next table Deng Wei, back from wherever he'd been on his brakeless bike, snorted. Big Bird swung round like a gun turret, and I knew exactly what was coming, because I'd heard it two hundred times.

"I'm telling you. Streak. Thursday. I got him eleven times."

"You play Tidecaller," Deng Wei said. "Tidecaller doesn't have a hook."

"So I tentacled him. Same thing."

"Streak didn't cry."

"He cried on the inside," I said into my noodles, half a second early.

"HE CRIED ON THE INSIDE," Big Bird announced.

Deng Wei looked at me. I ate a piece of beef with great innocence.

The bowl was heavier than three yuan's worth. Big Bird hovered, the way people do when they've caught you listening, and squinted.

"You play? You look like you play Frost Maiden. You look like you stand at the back."

Word for word. The first time, I'd said, "And you look like you play a fridge," and that had been the foundation of our friendship, if you could call two years of insults a friendship. My mouth had the line ready. I put it down.

"I play whatever the team needs."

"Nobody plays everything."

"When do you use the tentacles?"

"When I'm in the middle of them."

"How do you get into the middle of them?"

He looked at me as if I'd asked how you get into a bowl of noodles. "I walk."

"DAPENG. Table four wants vinegar, not a speech."

He grabbed the vinegar without looking. "My dad sends Boss Mei her bowl at seven. I take it up. Machine fifteen. One game. I'll show you walking."

He invited everybody. I knew that too. It was what I did with the invitation that would be new.

---

Boss Mei was at the counter with her perm pinned up, a cigarette going, and a hand of mahjong tiles laid out against nobody. She was losing to herself.

"The chopstick," she said, without turning around.

I put two yuan on the counter. "I want fourteen. Every day after school, from—"

"Six to seven." She turned a tile over. "Like a hotel."

School let out at 5:40. Anyone could have worked out six. I decided someone had.

"Like a hotel," I said.

"Hotels have towels." The coins went into her drawer. "Ten minutes late and I sell it to a fatty."

The back-room door was shut, with a strip of yellow light under it. Through it I could hear a single keyboard, quick and very tidy, nothing like the hammering out front. I didn't ask. Asking Mei Lan about the back room was how your borrowed ID number went missing. My eyes went to the door anyway. Hers followed mine, then came back, and she blew smoke at the ceiling as if I'd said something boring.

I drilled last-hits on an empty map until the clock over the counter crept toward seven. It was better than Sunday. My fingers were starting to feel the little hitch before a creep dies, the way you know the step on a staircase you always trip on. I still missed one in four. On a stage in Shanghai, that would have been career suicide. In Qingshui it was a Monday.

At 7:04 the bead curtain exploded.

"BOSS MEI! YOUR NOODLES!"

The bowl came in a plastic bag, steam leaking from the lid. Mei tore a pink slip off her receipt pad, wrote *15 — 7:04* in neat, angry numbers, and held it out. Big Bird stuffed it into his apron's front pocket and dropped into the chair beside me. The chair complained.

"Your dad's going to come up at half past," I said.

"It's the dinner rush. He never comes up in the rush."

In my first life, Lao Zhao had come up in the rush about twice a week, and Big Bird had believed every single time that he wouldn't. I let it go.

We joined "Qingshui 1-100 No Feeders" and got three random teammates and five random enemies. One of the enemies was a Fudge with a bleached-stripe avatar.

"STREAK!" Big Bird told the entire cafe.

He picked Tidecaller, the sea giant whose tentacles stun everyone around him. I picked Lyon the Hexer: spikes that stun in a line, a spell that turns an enemy into a frog, and a finger of death for one unlucky person. It's a hero for people who stand at the back and choose. He gave me a look that said he'd told me so.

At six minutes he walked into the bottom lane alone, where two enemies waited under their tower.

"There's a Watcher Eye in those trees," I said. "They've watched you coming for half a minute."

"LET THEM WATCH."

They watched, and then they killed him. He said a word that would have gotten him slapped by any grandmother in Sichuan, then added, for my benefit, "Unlucky."

That was the moment. Last time I'd started screaming right there and kept it up for two years. It had made him worse and me hoarse. So I said it once and shut up, and from then on I watched how he went in instead of where.

He never hesitated. Every other boy at Starlight stood at the edge of a fight and took a breath first. Big Bird never took the breath. For a feeder, that was a death sentence. For a frontline, it was the whole job, if somebody behind him knew when to aim it.

At seventeen minutes all five of them came to push our middle tower, bunched over the creep wave the way kids bunch round a cake.

"Wait. Their Frost Maiden still has her freeze. When she uses it—"

Our Phantom Blade wandered in from the jungle like a lost sheep, and the Frost Maiden froze him. That freeze was the one spell that could have stopped Tidecaller on the way in, and it's slow to come back. Big Bird walked into the gap it left before I'd finished the sentence.

Tentacles burst from the ground. Four enemy heroes stuck fast, and the fifth spun away at the edge.

My fingers had a plan: spikes through the two in a line, frog the one who escaped, finger of death on the Frost Maiden. The spikes went out clean. The frog landed on Streak's Fudge, who was already stunned and helpless and now became a stunned, helpless frog, which achieved nothing but comedy. The finger found the Frost Maiden. Our teammates poured in.

Three of theirs died, and one of ours: Big Bird, under their tower, shouting "AVENGE ME" to a room that hadn't asked.

"YOU SAW? I WENT WHEN SHE USED IT."

"You went before I said."

"You were TALKING."

"Why that second?"

He thought about it, which visibly cost him. "I was hungry."

But I'd watched him. Half a second before he moved, his eyes had flicked to the Frost Maiden. He didn't know why he'd gone. He'd just gone at the right moment. In fifteen years I'd met maybe four players who did that, and I'd spent a fortune and a lot of screaming trying to teach it to the rest. I'd had one sitting beside me for two years, and I'd called it feeding.

At twenty-two minutes, our five gathered for the last push, and the bead curtain clacked.

Zhao Guoqiang, Lao Zhao to the whole street, was half his son's size: wiry, bald on top, a cigarette behind one ear and an apron that had seen things. He didn't shout. He just stood there until the front row felt him.

"Lao Zhao," Mei said. "My noodles went cold waiting for your boy to finish feeding."

"Ba! I just got here. I'm going right—"

His father plucked the pink slip from the apron pocket and read it. He looked at the clock, which said 7:31.

"Fifteen. Seven-oh-four." He held it up. "You write very neat lies for him."

"I don't lie," Mei said. "I bill."

Lao Zhao took his son by the ear. Big Bird had to fold sideways to keep it attached, and his enormous foot caught the power strip under the desk. Fourteen and fifteen died together with a pop, the CRTs whining down to black.

"WE WERE WINNING!" Big Bird bellowed, going down the stairs at an angle.

"Winning?" His father's voice came back up the stairwell, small and terrible. "You've eaten the shop, you rice bucket, and now you want to eat my evenings too."

"Your hour was up at seven," Mei told me. "Consider the rest a donation."

---

I went downstairs only because it was on the way home. That's my story.

Lao Zhao was wiping a table that was already clean. He pointed at a stool with the rag. "Sit."

A bowl came, large, beef piled up the side like a landslide.

"I already ate here."

"Students are always hungry." He squinted at me. "That idiot dragged you up there, I'll bet."

"He's very persuasive."

"He's very loud." He went back into the kitchen. Sixteen years from now, the same squint would be watching his son from a stool by the hatch, his knees finished, saying nothing about the butterfly clip.

My thirty-one-year-old stomach knew there was no room. My fifteen-year-old stomach had already started. Through the front window I watched Su Wen come out of the alley, schoolbag on both shoulders, walking fast toward the teachers' housing. Under the streetlight he stopped and counted what was left in his hand. It took him less time than before.

The kitchen hatch banged open. A head came through the steam, one ear still bright red.

"HEY. SKINNY."

Auntie Yang, back for a second bowl and a second humiliation, flinched.

"You're not bad," Big Bird told the whole street. "For a skinny kid. Saturday. Rematch. Real players, five of us, not those vegetable birds." That was cainiao, Starlight's word for beginners.

"You don't know any real players."

"I KNOW EVERYBODY."

He did, in his way. He knew everybody's mother and what they ordered and who wanted chili on the side. I knew everybody's next fifteen years. Between the two of us, that was most of Qingshui.

A dishrag hit him in the side of the head from somewhere behind, and the hatch banged shut.

I finished the bowl, broth too, and walked home past the dark market to fail at factoring for an hour. Under the quadratics, in the back of my notebook, I wrote one name and put no question mark after it.

Big Bird thought he'd just challenged me. I was happy to let him think so.
