# Chapter 4: Class 3

Grandma tore three pages off the calendar at once that morning, and for once it showed the right date. She stood back and frowned at it like it had pulled something on her.

"Monday," she said. "School. Wash your face, you look like a dried bean."

I washed my face. I looked, in the speckled mirror over the basin, like a dried bean that had been told to go to school, and I didn't look long. She pushed a steamed bun stuffed with pickled greens into my hand at the door, then a second one, then took the second one back, thought about it, and gave it to me again.

"Your father will ask about the ranking on Sunday," she said. "Don't make me lie for you."

So the notebook story hadn't fully sold. I'd suspected as much.

Qingshui Junior Middle School sat at the end of the main street, past the hardware shop and the noodle places, behind a gate painted the red of every school gate in China. Kids streamed in on bikes three to a frame, all of them in the same blue-and-white tracksuit, so the street looked like a river of cheap pajamas. A loudspeaker on a pole was playing the march they play before morning exercises. I had not heard it in sixteen years, and my feet fell into step before I could stop them.

The rankings were on the wall by the stairs, where they'd always been. Eighth grade final exams, class 3, fifty-one names in black brush on red paper, everyone's business. I found mine on the way up.

Thirty-fourth.

Not a disaster. Not anything. The exact middle of nowhere, which was where I'd been at fifteen, give or take, with a head full of hero names and no room for anything else. At the top, in neat calligraphy, was SU WEN, and under him, a nose behind, XU QING.

I stood there long enough that a girl with two thermoses had to go around me. She looked back to see what was so interesting. It was a sheet of red paper.

---

Here is a thing I'd forgotten about ninth grade: morning reading starts at seven-twenty. Forty kids read Chinese texts out loud, all at different speeds, a sound like a beehive reciting poetry. It's the most effective sleeping pill ever made. I'd spent half the night with my eyes open, looking at the dog on the ceiling and thinking about last-hits. I sat down, and when my knees didn't complain my body took that as a sign that everything was fine. I opened my book to a poem about a man missing his hometown, and then I was asleep.

I woke because someone was standing very close to my desk.

"Lin Chen."

Xu Qing was the class monitor, and she looked like one. Her tracksuit was zipped exactly to the chin, the only zip in the room that went all the way. Her ponytail was so straight and tight it looked installed rather than tied. Light skin with a warm undertone, a small sharp face, and eyes that stared at you as if you were a question she had already marked wrong. In her left hand was a small notebook with a green plastic cover. I remembered that notebook. Everybody in class 3 remembered that notebook.

"Sleeping during morning reading," she said, and wrote it down. "Seven thirty-one."

"I was resting my eyes."

"Your eyes were open for none of it." She wrote again. "Also. When you sat down, you made a noise."

"What noise?"

"Like this." She produced, with total seriousness, a perfect imitation of me lowering myself into a chair: a long, wheezing *hhhaaah*, the sound of a man of sixty easing into a bath. Two boys in the row in front turned around, delighted.

"I don't do that."

"You did it three times this morning in the register line. And you sit like this." She hunched her shoulders and put her hands on her knees. It was, to be fair, a very good grandfather. "That is not correct posture for a student."

"Is that going in the book?"

"It is already in the book." She turned it so I could see. Under my name, in tiny, ruthless handwriting: *Slept (7:31). Sighs like old man (×3+). Sits like grandfather.* Below that was an empty line, waiting for whatever I did next.

Then she held out her hand. "Math homework."

I gave her my math homework. She looked at it. It was a sheet with all six questions copied out very neatly. Under each one there was nothing at all.

"You have copied the questions," she said.

"It's a start."

"It is not a start. It is the questions." Her pen hovered. "This goes to Teacher Gao. If you finish it tonight, you can give it to me before seven-twenty tomorrow, and I will note that it was late."

"Can't I just send it to you on WeTalk—"

The pen stopped. "On what?"

"QiQi. I meant QiQi."

"You said 'we talk.'"

"I meant, we talk about it. Later." I was a grown man. I had done post-match interviews in front of cameras. "Old habit."

She looked at me a while longer. Then she wrote something down that I couldn't see, and walked back to her desk. Her ponytail swung exactly twice, and then it stopped.

---

Teacher Gao taught math and was our banzhuren, the homeroom teacher, which made him the most powerful man in any fifteen-year-old's world, just under God and well above the police. He was a heavy man with chalk dust permanently ground into the knees of his pants. He carried a glass jar of green tea so big it could have held a goldfish. He read Xu Qing's report at the start of second period, then looked up over his reading glasses and found me.

"Lin Chen. Board."

There's a particular silence when a classmate is about to be executed. It's a happy silence. I got up. I did not make the noise. I felt the whole room listen for it anyway.

He wrote the problem himself, his chalk squeaking on every stroke: *A county pig farm sold 200 pigs in 2004 and 242 pigs in 2006. Find the average annual growth rate.*

He was being kind, I think. It was the textbook's warm-up, the one even the back row could get with a push. He expected me to stand there for a minute, sweat, and get the push.

The trouble was that I'd spent fifteen years of my other life staring at prize pools, sponsor percentages and my own falling salary, and you don't do that without learning what 242 over 200 looks like. The answer surfaced in my head before I'd even read the second sentence.

"Ten percent," I said.

Teacher Gao's tea stopped on its way to his mouth.

"Working," he said.

So I wrote the working. Two hundred times one plus x, squared, equals two hundred and forty-two. One plus x squared is one point two one. Square root, one point one. Ten percent. My handwriting was a fifteen-year-old's, all loops, but the speed was not, and I heard the beehive behind me go very still.

"And the other root?" he said.

"Negative two point one." I shrugged. "But pigs don't shrink by two hundred and ten percent. I'd throw it out."

Someone in the back laughed, then swallowed it. Teacher Gao didn't laugh. He was not a stupid man; he'd been a teacher in this county for twenty years and had seen every kind of cheating invented by children. He looked at the board, and then at my empty homework sheet on his desk. Then he wrote a second problem underneath, the kind you solve by factoring: x squared minus five x plus six equals zero.

It was the easy one. Seventh-graders could do it. I stared at it and absolutely nothing happened in my head. Somewhere in the last sixteen years, factoring had gone the same way as my phone number from 2009 and the name of the girl who'd sold me my first mouse.

"Mm," said Teacher Gao, and I could hear him tidying the world back into order. The boy couldn't factor. The first answer was a fluke, or something he'd copied off somebody's older brother. That was fine. That he could understand.

I should have left it there. Instead, being an idiot, I tried to help.

"I guessed the first one," I said. "Lucky. My grandma sells pickles. She does sums like that with. Brine."

There was a pause in which forty kids tried to picture how brine came into it.

"With brine," Teacher Gao repeated.

"Prices. Of the brine. Going up." I put the chalk down very carefully. "Every year. About ten percent."

"Sit down, Lin Chen."

I sat down. I did make the noise this time. I couldn't help it.

Two rows over, Xu Qing's pen moved, one word, firm, with a period you could hear. I leaned an inch and read it upside down across the aisle.

*Suspicious.*

---

During eye exercises I watched Su Wen.

I'd been meaning to all morning. That was half of why I'd come to school without a fight, apart from Grandma and the face thing. Last time around, Su Wen had been a name on top of a red sheet, a thin boy who walked the edge of every room and went off to a good university, after which I never thought about him again. He sat by the window in the second row. He had the narrow, careful look of a boy who'd been told to sit up straight once and never forgot it. His skin was pale with pink undertones, he wore heavy black glasses that were too big for his face, and there was an ink callus on his middle finger the size of a grain of rice. When the teachers asked him something, he answered in as few words as the question allowed and then went back inside himself and shut the door.

Eye exercises meant five minutes of the loudspeaker counting in a woman's sweet, tinny voice, *one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight,* while forty kids pressed their knuckles into the corners of their eyes and peeked at each other. Su Wen's eyes were shut properly. His lips were moving.

Not with the loudspeaker. Against it. The speaker counted in eights. He was counting something else, very small, so his breath barely fogged the glass beside him. I watched his mouth go round the numbers and his finger tap his desk once at the top, and then he started again. I counted with him without meaning to, and my thumb twitched where my hotkey used to be.

Thirty. He was counting to thirty and starting over.

AotA has a clock in the corner of the screen, and everybody can read it. What the clock won't tell you is the things a support actually needs: how long your Watcher Eye has before it goes blind, when the enemy's big stun comes back, how many seconds you have before their Blink Stone works again. And in a fight you can't take your eyes off the map to check anything. So the good ones keep a beat in their heads, thirty seconds a beat, a tap at the top, and count how many beats each timer has left. You count that in your sleep. I had known exactly one player who counted it out loud, in my original life, a Korean support who did it through a whole tournament in Shanghai, until his teammates made him stop because it was driving them insane.

I told myself it was a coincidence. Lots of boys count things. Then, in the last period before lunch, Su Wen took out his Chinese textbook, and a folded corner of paper stuck out of it near the back, and I saw a grid in tiny, square handwriting. Hero names down one side, numbers down the other. *Tidecaller 160. Earthbreaker 150. Eye 6:00.* And above them, half hidden under the fold, a word I'd have recognized in any handwriting on earth.

*Cooldowns.*

He felt me looking. Su Wen had the instincts of a boy who'd been caught before. The book closed so fast it barely made a sound.

"Chinese notes?" I said.

He looked at me through the big glasses. Up close, his eyes were tired in a way I recognized.

"Yes."

"In a grid?"

A pause. "Chinese is very organized."

Then he turned back to the window and did not speak to me again for the rest of the day. I'd spent sixteen years having arguments in voice chat with grown men. I had never been so thoroughly dismissed.

---

The bell went at five-forty. I let the river of pajamas take me out the gate, and then I stepped out of it and waited by the snack cart where a woman sold spicy strips in plastic bags. I bought one bag with money I didn't have, mostly so I'd have a reason to be standing there.

Su Wen came out alone, his bag on both shoulders, straps pulled tight. He turned left, toward his family's apartment in the teachers' housing behind the school. I knew where it was because Teacher Su had once caught me throwing a shoe onto their balcony in fourth grade, and nobody forgets the address of their first real crime.

He went about fifty steps toward home. Then he stopped, looked back once at the school gate and once at the street, and turned the other way.

I followed him. I'm not proud of it, but I'm not that sorry either. He walked fast and kept to the edge of the sidewalk, past the noodle shops and a man frying skewers over a trough of coals, past the mahjong parlor with its clatter coming through the door. At the hardware shop he didn't go up the dark staircase with its bead curtain and its sign about minors and crying. He ducked into the alley beside it. It was narrow and wet, full of crates and the drip of somebody's air conditioner, and the whole place smelled of cooking oil and rust.

I stopped at the mouth of it, behind a stack of empty beer crates, and watched.

Halfway down, set into the back wall of the building, was a metal door painted a tired green. Su Wen stood in front of it and took something out of his trouser pocket: a short stack of one-yuan coins, held together so carefully they didn't clink. He counted them with his thumb. Once. Then again, like the number might change. He looked over his shoulder, straight past my beer crates, without seeing me, and knocked twice.

The door opened a crack. A slice of yellow light fell across the wet ground, along with a breath of cigarette smoke, and I heard, faint and unmistakable, the clatter of forty keyboards and somebody upstairs shouting at a monitor.

Su Wen, first in class 3, Teacher Su's boy, the boy every parent in the county held up as "other people's child," slipped inside with his coins. The door shut behind him.

I stood in the alley holding a bag of spicy strips I couldn't afford.
