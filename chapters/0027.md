# Chapter 27: The Price of a Review

Streak caught me at the counter at two o'clock, while I was explaining to Boss Mei that eighty fen was a kind of money.

"It's a kind of eighty fen," she said, and turned over a tile.

Streak leaned on the counter next to me. He ran the holiday crowd, the boys who came up the stairs in packs all week because school was shut and their mothers were at the mahjong tables, and he had spent five days treating Starlight like his own living room. Now he looked at the ceiling, then the fridge, then me.

"Second." He said my ID as if it had gone off. "We lost twice this morning. Lianzhan. Some Mianzhu kids who type in all capitals. And yesterday we beat you, and then we didn't." He scratched his neck. "How do we keep losing?"

In my first life I would have told him. Loudly, for free, from across the room, with his whole group listening. Telling people what they did wrong was the one thing I'd always been generous with.

"That's a lesson," I said. "Lessons cost money."

"How much?"

"Twelve," said Xu Qing, from the top of the stairs.

She had her notebook against her chest and her school bag on both shoulders, though there was no school until Monday. Her watch said whatever her watch said. She looked at Streak and didn't blink.

"Grade check is at four," she told me. "I came at two, because by four you would have done something."

"I haven't done anything."

"You are about to sell something. I can hear it."

Streak's group had drifted over from the machines by the window: Deng Wei, Liu Bo, and Deng Wei's cousin from Deyang, a tall boy in a borrowed jacket with the sleeves pushed up past his elbows.

"What are we buying?" Liu Bo asked.

"Him," said Deng Wei.

"Three," Streak said to me, not to her. "For all four of us."

"Three each," said Xu Qing. "Twelve."

"Three's for all of us."

"Then you misheard the offer."

"You didn't make an offer, you just said twelve."

"Twelve is the offer."

Streak looked at me for help. That was a mistake. I was enjoying it.

"Ten," I said. "One hour. Your hour, your machine, in the back room. You bring your last game. If nobody learns anything, you get it back."

Xu Qing's pen stopped. "There is no refund procedure."

"There is now."

She wrote it down, slowly, and I could tell from the slant of the strokes that she was going to bring it up later.

"One more thing," I said, before Streak could start haggling with the ten. "I talk about what you did. I don't talk about what we do."

"What do you do?"

"Lose to you on holidays."

Deng Wei laughed. Streak didn't, and I liked him a little more for it. He'd beaten us yesterday morning and he knew exactly how much that was worth.

The truth was simpler and less funny. Somewhere in Beijing, a print shop had my three rules for the middle lane in a stack waiting for November, and in a month every boy in every cafe in China who could find nine yuan for a magazine would own them. Hammer would own them. Whatever I said out loud stopped being mine the moment it left. That was fine for the middle lane. It was not fine for the things on the folded calendar page in my shirt pocket.

Mei pointed her tile at Streak. "Back room's on your hour. Door stays open. Smoke stays out here."

She said it with a cigarette in her mouth.

---

Streak sat at the middle of the five machines. The other three crowded behind him. Fang Hu came in under Deng Wei's elbow and sat cross-legged on the Uncle Kong boxes as if he had a ticket.

"He's not paying," said Streak.

"He is staff," said Xu Qing.

"I'm staff!"

"You're out at five, staff!" Mei called from the counter.

"Pull up the VOD," I said.

Four faces turned.

"The what?"

"The... replay. The file. The last one."

"Where'd you learn VOD?" said Deng Wei.

"It's English."

"For what?"

"For replay."

Liu Bo nodded slowly, a boy filing away an English word he would use wrong for a year.

Peacecraft saved your last game whether you wanted it or not. Streak found it, the loss to the Mianzhu capital-letter kids, and I took the mouse to run it. My fingers went for the speed key, hit the wrong one, and for two seconds the whole game ran at eight times speed. Four boys watched their heroes walk out, fight, die and come back in a blur.

"That's how it felt," said the cousin.

I slowed it down and stopped it at four minutes.

"Where are you, Streak?"

"Getting the rune."

I let it run to six. "Where are you?"

"Rune."

Eight minutes. His hero was in the river again, picking up a Haste rune and then using it to walk back to his lane slightly faster than usual.

"It was a good rune," Streak said.

"Ten."

At ten minutes the enemy mid was already standing in the trees beside the rune spot. He'd been waiting about twenty seconds. Streak's hero walked into him at exactly ten, and died at ten-oh-three.

Nobody said anything.

"He didn't guess," I said. "You told him. Four, six, eight. Anybody can count to ten."

"Runes are free."

"Watch your lane while you're getting it."

I ran the four-minute trip again and pointed at the bottom of the screen, where Streak's creeps were walking into the enemy tower with nobody to collect anything off them.

"Hu Zi, count."

Fang Hu counted out loud, too loud, the way he did everything. "One, two... seven, eight... nine. Ge, that's nine. Is that a lot?"

"That's a lot of gold," I said. "He traded it for a rune he used to walk."

Streak glared at the screen. Then he said, quieter, "Somebody told me runes win games."

"Who?"

"A guy."

I waited. He didn't offer a better answer, so I filed it next to the other things in this town I didn't know yet.

At seventeen minutes, an enemy Drow Archer with a thin red sliver of health ran out of a fight and into the fog by the jungle. Streak's four went after her, and their random fifth followed. I stopped the replay with them in a line, chasing, nearly at the trees.

"Deng Wei. How many enemies can you see?"

"One. She's almost dead."

"How many are on their team?"

He thought about it. "Five."

"Where are the other four?"

"Shopping," said Deng Wei.

Liu Bo laughed. Fang Hu laughed because Liu Bo did. I let it run. The other four came out of the trees and Streak's team died in roughly the order they'd arrived, Liu Bo last, and Deng Wei swore at the replay. It had already happened three hours ago and did not care.

"She was almost dead," he said again, to the monitor.

"She was running toward her friends. Chase her to the edge of what you can see. Then stop. If she's still alive after that, she earned it."

"That's boring," said the cousin.

"Losing's boring. You lose for forty minutes."

We went through it twice more. Streak sat with his elbows on the desk and his jaw out, and he didn't say much. He didn't need to. At twenty-six minutes, when his hero turned around in the replay to go to the river again, his hand twitched on the desk, as if he wanted to reach in and stop himself.

With ten minutes of the hour left, the cousin said, "Your ghost. The one on thirty-eight. Typhoon."

"What about him?"

"Can he look at mine? I'll pay him. I'll pay him more than this."

"He doesn't do lessons."

"When's he here?"

"When he's here."

"What's his name? His real one."

"Typhoon."

The cousin looked at Deng Wei. Deng Wei shrugged. Behind me I heard Xu Qing close her notebook. She only did that when she'd decided not to write something down.

---

They paid at the counter. Streak put down a five. Deng Wei put down two ones, the cousin two more, and Liu Bo counted out a yuan in jiao coins while everyone waited.

Xu Qing picked up each note in turn and walked it to the window by machine forty, where the light came through the crack with masking tape across it. She held the ones up. She held the five up for longer.

"This one has no flower," she said.

"What flower?" said Streak.

"There is supposed to be a narcissus. In the white part. There is nothing in the white part."

"It's money, not a garden."

"It is not money. That is my point."

Streak snatched it back and held it up himself. His face went red from the collar. "The skewer cart. On Bridge Street. Last night. That turtle's egg gave me change for a ten—"

"Skewer carts give fakes after dark," said Mei, without looking up from her tiles. "Everybody knows."

"I didn't know!"

"Now you're everybody."

Xu Qing held out her hand, palm up, and waited until Streak, for reasons I don't think he understood himself, gave the note back to her.

"Counterfeit currency is not returned to circulation," she said.

"That's my five yuan."

"It is nobody's five yuan."

She opened the notebook to the inside of the back cover, taped the note down at all four corners, ruled a neat box around it, and wrote underneath: *Counterfeit. 5. Bridge Street skewer cart (per Streak). Retained.*

Deng Wei paid the missing five in coins and a crumpled one, and told Streak, loud enough for the whole row to hear, that Streak now owed him a skewer cart.

"Ten," Xu Qing said, after counting it twice. "Thirty-three sixty. Remaining, forty-one forty." She looked at me over the notebook. "Income from instruction is team income. That is rule thirteen."

"When did you make rule thirteen?"

"While you were saying VOD."

Streak stopped at the top of the stairs. He didn't turn all the way around.

"Next time I want the second hour," he said.

"Next time's ten again."

"For the second hour." He went down. His group went down after him, the cousin last, looking back once toward machine thirty-eight, which was empty.

---

Zhao Dapeng arrived at half past three, filling the doorway with a dish towel still over his shoulder. Sister Pan was back at the sink, and Lao Zhao had told him to go without telling him where. In the Zhao family, that counted as a holiday.

Xu Qing held out her hand. He gave her a fractions worksheet folded into eighths.

She unfolded it, read it, and marked it in pencil while he stood over her with his hands on his knees.

"Seven and a half," she said.

"SEVEN AND A HALF."

"Question nine has the right answer and the wrong working."

"THE ANSWER'S RIGHT."

"By accident."

"I'LL TAKE ACCIDENT." He turned to me. "Lin Chen. Last week it was four."

"I know."

"FOUR," he told the room, and two holiday boys at the window machines looked over and decided not to ask.

"Fang Hu," said Xu Qing. "Essay."

Hu Zi stood up on the Uncle Kong boxes, which wasn't necessary. "A hundred and forty."

"Characters?"

"About. I counted twice and got two. A hundred and thirty-eight and a hundred and forty-four. So about."

"It must be five hundred."

"Nainai says it's done when it's done."

"That is not a deadline."

"She says it is."

"Can I read it?" I asked.

He grinned with the gap in his teeth. "When she says."

"It's five," said Mei. "Staff. Out."

I didn't argue for fourteen. Eighty fen was twenty-four minutes, and Mei didn't sell minutes. Xu Qing went home with her notebook under her arm and a fake five-yuan note taped inside it, and Big Bird went back to the shop before his father could change his mind about the holiday.

---

At the bottom of the stairs, Old Tan had a bicycle upside down on its seat and handlebars on the stretch of sidewalk he rented outside the hardware shop. He was spinning the back wheel with one hand and watching it wobble.

"You," he said. "Lin Guifen's boy."

"Grandson."

"You fixed Mei Lan's computers."

"One."

"One's what I heard." He stopped the wheel with his palm. "Man whose chain this is. Works at the tax office. His son's computer's got a virus. Words come up by themselves, in English. Boy says the computer's talking to America. The shop by the bus station says bring it in, a week, forty yuan to look."

"And you said you know a boy."

"I said I know a boy." He spun the wheel again. "Three yuan. Four if it works. I'm only asking."

He said it in the same voice he used for buying radishes.

"Monday," I said. "After school. Tell him not to turn it on till then."

"He turns it on to see if it's still talking."

"Tell him anyway."

"Ge fixes everything," Fang Hu informed Old Tan.

"I'm only asking," said Old Tan, and bent back to his chain.

I walked Hu Zi toward the rice-shop corner. He talked about the skewer cart, whether you could tell a fake by biting it, whether Streak would really buy Deng Wei a whole cart and where you'd keep one. I said yes and no in mostly the right places.

Streak would stop going to every rune by Tuesday. He'd been embarrassed in front of his own group, and embarrassment teaches fast. The chasing would take longer. It always does; a dying hero running away is the hardest thing in the game to leave alone. Two hours, maybe three. Then the cousin would bring his games, then the Mianzhu kids, if word got out, and after some number of hours there would be nothing left of their mistakes worth paying for. What would be left was the folded page in my pocket.

At the corner Fang Hu stopped, looked both ways twice with his whole head, and crossed slowly.

The second hour would be twelve.
