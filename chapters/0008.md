# Chapter 8: Raid

The tofu man paid me three yuan for a Sunday morning of soybean sacks, plus a block of tofu. Grandma accepted the tofu like a general accepting a defeated enemy's sword, and then cooked it with so much chili that the defeat felt personal.

So on Wednesday I walked up the Starlight stairs with three yuan ninety in my pocket. The Sunday call was still sitting in my chest. My father had asked about the ranking. I'd said thirty-four. The silence after that was long enough to cost him money. Then he said, "Study hard," and my mother came on to ask whether I was eating meat.

Boss Mei was already in her cardigan at the counter, keys in hand, a cigarette parked unlit in the corner of her mouth. She took my two coins without looking at them.

"Fourteen. If anyone asks, I'm at the bank."

The bank on the main street closed at five. It was six. I didn't say so.

She went down the stairs in her slippers, and the stool behind the counter was taken by somebody I hadn't seen in fourteen years.

Tang Yi. Everybody called him Monk, because he shaved his own head with a disposable razor every Monday and it never quite grew back between shaves. He was tall and long-boned, and he folded onto the stool in sections. The stubble left a blue shadow on his scalp, and his skin was warm tan from a summer he must have spent outdoors doing something he never mentioned. His eyes were always half shut, like the world was a television someone had left on in the next room. Army-green jacket, plastic sandals, a cheap digital watch with a cracked face.

In my first life I'd known him for two years. In those two years he said maybe a hundred words to me, and most of them were "Restart it." Once, at four in the morning, he'd fixed machine 14 for me without being asked, then gone back to sleep on the sofa in the back room as if the sofa weren't trying to bite him. When I left for Chengdu I didn't tell him either. When I came home in 2022, nobody at the noodle shop knew where he'd gone, and I hadn't asked twice.

Tonight he had a bicycle chain spread out on a sheet of the *Deyang Daily*: small, rusty, black oil soaking through the newsprint. Next to it lay a pink plastic bell with a peeling rabbit sticker. He was working the links with a screwdriver, black to the knuckles, and he didn't look up.

"Machine fourteen," I said, for something to say.

"Mm."

That was one of the hundred.

---

Big Bird was at 15, already logged in, a bag of spicy strips open on the keyboard. Zhao Dapeng had told his father he was at the market buying chilies. "There's a lot of market," he explained, which was true, and also not an alibi.

Under the desk behind him, cross-legged on the concrete, was Fang Hu.

"Ge!" He scrambled up, the red scarf hanging out of his jacket pocket. "You're alive! I thought you'd quit."

"I was poor."

"Me too. My dad was on days." He said it in the voice other kids used for *it rained all week*. "So I had to go to school *and* go home. Both. Every day."

"Tragic."

"I nearly died."

At the corner, Xiaoyu had her hood up and her knees on the chair at 38. As I sat down she glanced over once, mouthed *thirty-four*, and went back to her screen. Fair enough. I hadn't earned more yet.

We played one ugly public game. Big Bird charged into five people because one of them "looked at him." I spent forty minutes of my hour making my fingers do the same thing a hundred times, and they did it about seventy times. Better than Saturday. Worse than I needed. Fang Hu narrated every one of my last-hits from behind my chair like a horse-racing commentator who had money on the horse.

At 6:41, the bell rang.

It was an old brass doorbell screwed to the wall above the counter. Its wire ran down the stairwell to a button behind Old Tan's till in the hardware shop. One short ring meant Mei's mahjong friends were coming up. Two long rings meant uniforms. I'd heard it maybe a dozen times in my first life, and I still knew the second sound in my stomach before my head caught up.

It rang twice. Long.

Starlight changed in about a second. Thirty kids stopped clicking at once, and the room went quiet.

Monk didn't stand up right away. He reached under the counter and pressed something, and every screen in the room flipped to a gray star logo with LOCKED underneath. Then he said, not loudly, "Back room."

The kids knew the drill better than they knew their times tables. Chairs scraped. A river of school tracksuits poured toward the narrow door behind the counter. Through it, for a second, I saw the bare bulb, the boxes of Uncle Kong stacked to the ceiling, and a pair of neat glasses turning toward the commotion. Su Wen, already inside. His lips were moving. Counting. Of course he was.

"Alley," said a low voice by the window.

Xiaoyu was standing at 38, looking down through the glass, hood still up, fake voice and all. "There's a guy in a windbreaker going into the alley. He's got a clipboard."

So no green door tonight. Monk took that in with his half-shut eyes and changed the plan in three words.

"Behind the boxes."

Xiaoyu walked past me toward the back room without hurrying, a boy-shaped hoodie among boy-shaped hoodies. She bumped my shoulder on purpose. "Move, thirty-four." Then she was gone into the bulb light.

Big Bird tried the desk first. He got about two-thirds of himself under machine 15. The desk rose on his back, the monitor tilted, and a spicy strip slid off the keyboard and onto his head.

"Out," I said. "Out, out."

He crawled out. The back room door was already a wall of elbows and knees, and Big Bird in that doorway would have been a cork in a bottle. I could hear feet on the stairs. Not many. Two people, one of them heavy and patient.

"I'm too big," Big Bird whispered. His whisper was louder than most people's talking. "Lin Chen. I'm too big for crime."

"You're not doing crime. You're a customer." I pushed him toward the counter with both hands. It was like pushing a fridge. "You came to buy noodles. You're buying noodles."

"I'm a what?"

"Noodles!"

Monk already had a cup of Uncle Kong out on the counter and was peeling back the lid as if he'd thought of it first. Maybe he had. When he reached for the thermos, his elbow caught the edge of the newspaper. The chain slid off the counter and I caught it. Oil on my palm. The little pink bell went *ting*.

I put it back in front of him, and my mouth said, "Tell Ling I'll fix her bike. Second link's bent."

There was no time to wonder what I'd said. The bead curtain at the top of the stairs clicked against itself. It did that when somebody tall stood just outside it.

Fang Hu shot across the room.

He went between the rows low and fast and slid under machine 14. I dropped in after him. We ended up wedged between the computer case and the wall, my knees at my ears and his elbow in my ribs, just as the curtain parted.

From under the desk all I could see were shoes. Black leather, polished to a shine, one lace broken and knotted back together near the top. They stopped just inside the doorway.

Next to me, Fang Hu sagged against the case. He was breathing short and fast, and his hand was pressed flat on the front of his jacket. I put my palm on his back. Under the denim, his heart was hammering against my hand like a fist on a door.

He turned his head and grinned at me, gap-toothed, sweaty, and mouthed: *Exciting.*

Then, very quietly, he breathed: "If he finds me, he kills me. If he finds you, he kills you, and then he kills me again for knowing you."

"Shh."

He sniffed. "Ge. Your feet smell like pickles."

They probably did. I'd spent Sunday in soybeans and my whole childhood in Grandma's front room. I said nothing.

"Where's Mei Lan?"

The voice from above the shoes was dry and level, the voice of a man reading a form aloud. I knew it. Officer Fang Jianguo, county police. In my first life he'd raided Starlight four times in two years and never once raised his voice. He'd never caught me either. I'd always assumed that was luck.

"Bank," said Monk.

A pause.

"The bank closes at five."

"She likes to be early."

The shoes turned toward the counter, and stopped again.

"Zhao Dapeng."

"Fang Shushu!" Big Bird roared the greeting at favorite-uncle-at-a-wedding volume. "Uncle Fang! Good evening! Have you eaten?"

"What are you doing here?"

"Buying noodles."

Another pause, longer.

"Your father sells noodles."

I closed my eyes under the desk.

"Beef," said Big Bird. You could hear him searching. "Ours have beef. Every day, beef. I wanted noodles with no beef in them. Just for one night. Uncle, I'm so tired of beef."

"Two yuan fifty," said Monk, and poured hot water into the cup, and the smell of artificial pork filled the room like an alibi.

The shoes didn't move for a while. Then: "Eat it at home."

"Yes, Uncle."

The shoes started down the rows. Slowly. They walked at one speed, the speed it takes to do a thing properly, and I suspected their owner did everything at it. They passed machine 12, machine 13, and stopped in front of 14, a foot from my knee. Above us the chair creaked as a hand rested on it. The seat would still be warm.

"Whose?"

"Mine," said Monk from the counter. "Testing it. Key sticks."

It was 27 whose key stuck, but nobody had told the police that.

Fang Hu had stopped breathing. Really stopped, cheeks puffed, hand still pressed to his chest, eyes on the knotted lace. I could feel the hammering through his back. I counted the way Su Wen counted. At nine the shoes moved on.

"Open that."

The back room. I couldn't see it, only hear the door, the little sigh its hinge always gave, the hum of the bare bulb. There were maybe fifteen kids behind those boxes, holding their breath and their knees, and none of them made a sound. I was proud of them, which was ridiculous.

The shoes stood in the doorway for a long time.

"You stacked these yourself?"

"Yes."

"They're crooked."

"I'm tired."

Heavier steps came up the stairs, wheezing. A second man came through the curtain. All I saw were brown shoes with a plastic sole flapping loose at one toe. "Nothing in the alley, Lao Fang. One cat. Some kid's bicycle chained to a pipe."

The black shoes stayed where they were a moment longer. I have thought about that moment since. Fang Jianguo was not a stupid man. He had stood in front of a wall of noodle boxes with a warm chair at his back and a sixteen-year-old buying instant noodles two doors from his own father's noodle shop. Whatever he decided in that doorway, he decided it on purpose.

The back room door closed.

"Tell Mei Lan I came."

"She'll be sorry she missed you," said Monk.

The curtain clicked. Two sets of shoes went down the stairs, one heavy and patient, one flapping, and the brass bell over the counter stayed quiet.

---

Nobody moved for a full minute. Then the back room door opened and Starlight came back out of it, blinking, laughing too loudly, punching each other's arms. Someone shouted that he'd been sitting on a box of noodles the whole time and his ass was now flavored. Monk pressed the thing under the counter and forty screens woke up at once.

Xiaoyu came out with her hood up, walked straight to 38, and sat down. Su Wen came out last. He stopped when he saw me crawling out from under 14. He looked at me, and I looked at him, and then he went back into the back room and shut the door, and a few seconds later I heard the green door clang in the alley.

That was a conversation for another day.

Fang Hu unfolded himself from under the desk, still pink and damp, and laughed loudly enough for both of us.

"Did you *see* his shoes? He was this close. This close, Ge. My heart was going like—" he slapped his chest with his palm, fast, *bam bam bam bam*, "—like a drum at a wedding."

"You okay?"

"I'm amazing." He was already backing toward the stairs. "I have to get home before he does. If he gets home first, I was never here, so I was home, so I'm fine."

"That's not how—"

He was gone. The bead curtain swung behind him.

At the counter, Big Bird was eating the Uncle Kong with the plastic fork, steam fogging the edges of his face. "These are worse than ours," he announced. "Way worse. I've wronged my father." He chewed. "Uncle Fang eats at our place on Fridays."

"Will he tell?"

Big Bird considered this, chewing. "He doesn't talk much. Mostly he eats."

The clock over the counter said 7:02. I had paid for an hour and spent nineteen minutes of it under a desk beside an eleven-year-old who'd insulted my feet. No refunds at Starlight. I wiped my palm on my tracksuit. The bicycle oil didn't come off. It just spread.

Monk had the chain back on the newspaper. He wasn't working on it. He was holding the pink bell between two black fingers, turning it so the rabbit faced me, then away.

Ling. I went back through my first life the way you go through your pockets for keys. Two years. A hundred words. "Restart it." The sofa, the razor, the four a.m. reboot. Nothing about a girl, a bike, a family. Nothing about anything, because I'd never asked. And a pink bell was a pink bell. Half the girls in the county were called Ling-something.

So it was a guess. A lucky one.

"The bell," I said, before he could say anything. "Pink, rabbit on it. Figured it belonged to a little girl, and every little girl in this county's called Ling-something. Lucky guess."

Monk put the bell down very carefully on the newspaper. Then he looked up at me, and for the first time in two lives I saw his eyes all the way open. They were darker than I'd thought.

"How do you know my sister's name?"
