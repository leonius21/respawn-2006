# Chapter 12: One Game

By three o'clock Boss Mei had started looking at my feet.

I'd been standing behind the same four chairs in the middle row since a quarter past one. My arms were folded, and the ninety fen in my pocket were still in my pocket. I watched Streak's crowd lose to a team of bus-station boys, then beat them, then lose to them again. Standing behind somebody's chair at Starlight was free. Not much else in the room was.

"You," Mei said when I passed the counter on my way to the bathroom. She didn't look up from her tiles. "Two hours on my floor. I'm working out a price."

"For standing?"

"For shoe. You're wearing a path in my floor and you haven't bought so much as a Cola. Lao Wang drinks four a night, and he's only got the one lung to spend." She slapped a tile down. "Peng."

I'd filled the backs of four calendar pages by then. Not with builds. With people. Streak played mid, and he walked his hero to the river every time the clock hit an even minute, every single rune, like a man checking his mailbox. Deng Wei, the vocational-school boy who would one day sell me insurance I couldn't afford, sat with one sneaker on the chair and swore at the screen before anything bad had even happened. Liu Bo, pudgy and squinting through his new glasses, still played whatever died last, except that today, three games running, it had died first.

And whenever any of them saw an enemy hero bleeding, all five of them came. Every time. Like flies to a dropped bun.

I wrote that one down twice.

At four I went back to the counter. "My hour," I said. "Can I have it at half past four today instead of six?"

Mei did look up then, briefly, over the cigarette. "Then at six you're a customer."

"At six I'm broke."

"Then at six you're standing. You're good at it." She went back to the tiles. "Back room. Five machines. The fifth one's got a boy in it. Take it up with the boy."

---

The boy in it was asleep.

Fang Hu had been playing on the fifth machine since two, against the rules and in plain sight, and somewhere in the third hour he'd lain down on the biting sofa "to rest my eyes like Dad," and the sofa had taken him. He lay with his mouth open, his father's old jacket pulled up to his chin, one sneaker hanging off by its heel. The fan was still pointed at the noodle boxes.

Big Bird arrived at twenty past four. He smelled of beef broth and wet stairwell, and he came through the door sideways. It was the only way he fit through any of Starlight's doors.

"I told Dad I was making a delivery," he announced. "And I am. Me. I'm delivering me." He looked at the sleeping kid, then at the empty chair. "Is that chair free?"

"It's Hu Zi's."

"Hu Zi's asleep. When you're asleep, you don't have a chair. That's the law." He sat down. The chair sighed. "It's warm. It's like sitting in a hen's spot."

Monk came at twenty-six minutes past, up the front stairs in his plastic sandals and his army-green jacket. He put two coins on Mei's counter without being asked, and she swept them into the drawer without comment. Then he sat down at the middle machine of the five and looked at the login screen the way other people look at weather. His shift started at six. It was the only thing I'd ever seen Tang Yi hurry for, and he didn't hurry for that either.

Two knocks at the green door at four twenty-eight. Su Wen came in with rain on his shoulders and an English textbook pressed to his chest. A cassette was rubber-banded to the cover.

"Sorry," he said. "Present perfect."

"Is that an excuse?"

"It's a tense. It ran over." He set the textbook face down on the floor next to his chair, carefully, as if it might be watching him. "I'm here." Then, without noticing, he slipped into English, in the careful voice of the cassette tape: *"I have been here. I have been being..."* He stopped and switched back to Chinese. "Give me a second."

Through the wall, Streak was already hosting. The lobby filled up: Streak, Deng Wei, Liu Bo, Deng Wei's cousin, and one of the bus-station boys who had stayed on out of spite. Our five slots filled after them. Then a name dropped into the observer slot that nobody had invited.

*Typhoon (Observer)*

Big Bird saw it and threw his whole voice at the wall. "BROTHER TYPHOON! HI, BROTHER TYPHOON!"

I leaned back far enough to see through the gap in the back-room door. In the corner by the window, machine 38: gray hood up, headphones on over it, knees on the chair. A ladder game was open on her screen, very visibly. She didn't turn around. She was the least interested person in the building, and she was making sure we could all see it.

"Picks," I said. "Monk, Phantom Blade."

"Fine."

"Bird, Tidecaller."

"MY BOY." He was already clicking.

"Su Wen, Lyon. Stuns and the frog. You place the Eyes, you call the timers. I'm on Soul Fiend, mid."

Through the wall, Streak took Storm Sprite. Of course he did. A hero that flies around the map as lightning is perfect for a boy who goes to every rune.

"Before we load." I kept my voice down, because the wall between us and them was thinner than Grandma's wall between our two rooms, and that was one brick. "When they see one of us bleeding, all five come. Every time. I watched them do it for three hours. So at some point I'm going to bleed on purpose. When I say 'now,' Bird, you go. Not before."

"How long before now?"

"Until I say now."

"That's not a time," Big Bird said.

---

The first seven minutes were Su Wen's worst since I'd known him in either life.

"Our Eye on the top rune runs out in forty," he said.

"It's gone."

"It can't be gone. It's been..." He counted under his breath, tapped the desk, frowned. "Five minutes and twenty."

"It's been six. Look at the map."

He looked at the dark patch where the Eye had been. He looked at the textbook on the floor. Somewhere in the last few minutes a whole verb tense had got into his head and taken up room, and his beat had gone slow. He took his glasses off, wiped them on his shirt, put them back on, and started his count again, the tip of his finger tapping the desk at thirty.

From the next Eye on, he was exact. I didn't hear him miss again.

My hands were better than they'd been two weeks ago, and nobody who hadn't been watching me drill on machine 14 every evening would have noticed. Soul Fiend's razes are three blasts at three fixed distances, short, middle, long, and the whole hero is about guessing where the other boy will be standing when the ground explodes. My head guessed right almost every time. My fingers were right more often than they'd been in the first week. I took most of the creeps I meant to take. Streak took fewer than he should have. Every even minute he flew off to the river and left his lane, and every time he came back it cost him a little more.

Bottom lane was another thing entirely.

I'd known Tang Yi played well. "Number one on the night list" was a joke, but it was also a list. I hadn't known what he looked like doing it. He didn't watch the creeps. He watched the little health bars over them, and when a bar was down to its last sliver his hero's sword came down on it, thump, like a post-office stamp on an envelope. Thump. Next. His mouse barely moved. His face didn't move at all. When Deng Wei's Fudge threw his chain hook out of the trees and dragged Su Wen's Lyon into a mouthful of trouble, Monk didn't swear or slam the desk. He took a step back, waited one beat for Su Wen to pop his stun, walked back in and finished the job. Fudge died. Monk ate a creep on the way past.

"That's," Su Wen said, "very calm."

"Mm," said Monk.

Big Bird was alone at the top against two. That was his job, and he was doing it loudly. "I'M FINE," he told us at minute seven. "I'M ONLY A LITTLE DEAD." At minute nine he was completely dead. That was what woke Fang Hu up.

He sat up on the sofa with his hair flattened on one side, saw Big Bird's back where his own back should have been, and his face went through a whole short opera.

"That's my machine."

"You were asleep," Big Bird said.

"I was resting my eyes."

"Your eyes were snoring."

Fang Hu folded his arms and sat back down hard, and the sofa's spring found him at once, and he yelped and didn't move, out of pure pride. He sulked at the noodle boxes for almost a full minute.

"Hu Zi," I said, without looking away from the screen. "I need a drinks man."

"I'm not a drinks man. I'm a player."

"Players get thirsty. Drinks men decide which players get to drink."

That landed. He was on his feet before I'd finished. Big Bird dug coins out of his apron pocket without taking his eyes off his respawn timer, and Fang Hu went out to the fridge. We heard Mei shout, "Which fatty is this for? I'm writing it down!" Then he was back with a Coco Cola for Big Bird, and also a paper cup of water for Monk that nobody had ordered.

He put it down next to Monk's keyboard very carefully. Monk glanced at it.

"Thanks," Monk said.

Fang Hu took up a post behind my chair with his arms folded, the way I'd stood behind Streak's crowd all afternoon. I couldn't see him, but I could feel him copying me.

---

The fight came at sixteen minutes, because the rune came at sixteen minutes.

"Eye on the river ledge," Su Wen said at fifteen-forty. "Good for another three minutes. Fudge's hook is back up."

I walked Soul Fiend to the rune spot with a third of his health and almost no mana. In AotA that is the same as standing in the street holding a sign that says *free*. I'd taken the damage on purpose, from creeps, which felt stupid while I was doing it.

"Bird. The trees above the river. Go now. Stop before the edge."

"Going." His Tidecaller lumbered up into the trees, an enormous sea giant tiptoeing through a forest, if a sea giant can be said to tiptoe. In real life Big Bird had stopped breathing through his nose.

"Storm Sprite on the Eye," said Su Wen. "Coming." A pause. "Two behind him. Three. Fudge in the trees on the far side. Four."

"Liu Bo?"

"Five. Liu Bo's last."

Through the wall, somebody said "He's low, he's low, go go go," with total sincerity.

They came. Streak's lightning first, Deng Wei's Fudge right behind, the other three spreading out in the river to cut off every way home. It was exactly what I'd written twice on the back of a calendar page. I let them have about two seconds of believing it. Then I walked backward, uphill, into the trees.

"Not yet," I said.

Big Bird made a small strangled noise.

"Not yet."

They followed me into the treeline, all five, crowding into a narrow path between trunks, and Big Bird's giant was standing right in the middle of the forest they were all walking into.

"Now."

The Tidecaller rose out of the trees, and tentacles burst from the ground all around him, and four enemy heroes were thrown into the air and held there, helpless. On our side of the wall, Big Bird roared. On the other side of the wall, a chair scraped.

Then it was my turn, and I knew exactly how it was supposed to go. Short raze, middle raze, long. The first one hit the Storm Sprite square. The second caught Deng Wei and the bus-station boy together. The third went where my finger slipped, not where I'd aimed: a beautiful long blast that blew up a patch of empty river behind everybody.

Monk was already there. Phantom Blade blinked onto the Storm Sprite's back and came down with one of those enormous critical strikes, and Streak was dead before the tentacles let go. Then she blinked onto Deng Wei. Then the bus-station boy. Monk's hand moved about three centimeters in total. Deng Wei's Fudge got free, turned to run, and Su Wen turned him into a frog. The frog hopped two steps and died.

Four of them. Only Liu Bo got away, because Liu Bo had been last, the way Liu Bo was always last.

Through the wall, Deng Wei said something about his monitor's whole family. Mei answered at once, louder and much worse, about kicked desks and paying for them out of his skin. She did not say "language."

"BROTHER TYPHOON, DID YOU SEE THAT?" Big Bird yelled at the wall.

I looked through the gap in the door. The observer slot in the corner of my screen was empty. She'd left right after the fight. Over at 38 the hood didn't turn. One hand came up off the mouse, held up three fingers, and slowly folded one of them down.

Two out of three. She'd seen the miss.

---

After that it was only walking. Su Wen's Eyes kept us out of the dark, the runes came every two minutes and we were at every one of them, and Streak's crowd stopped coming as a group, which meant they stopped coming. At thirty-one minutes their Ancient went up in a slow, orange, very satisfying cloud, and somebody in the main room typed gg.

The scoreboard came up. Monk had the most gold on the map, by a lot. I had the most deaths on our team, by one, and all of them had been on purpose.

Big Bird drank the whole Coco Cola in one go and burped the team name.

Fang Hu said, "Ge, can I play the next one," and I said, "There isn't a next one," and he said, "There's always a next one," which was the most frightening thing anybody had said all day.

Monk looked at his cracked watch. Five-oh-nine. Fifty-one minutes until his shift. He drank the paper cup of water in one swallow and put it down next to his keyboard, exactly where Fang Hu had put it. Then he stood up.

He didn't look at any of us. He looked at the floor between his sandals, as if the words were printed there and he was reading them off.

"Next Sunday. Same time."

Then he walked out to the counter, and Mei pushed the ledger toward him without a word, and he started reading it.

I sat with my hands on the sticky keyboard and did the arithmetic. In the other life, Tang Yi had said "Restart it" to me more than anything else, and that was an instruction to a machine. On Thursday I'd bargained "One game" out of him like a sack of soybeans. Nobody had asked him for this one.

I didn't say anything back. I was afraid he'd want it returned.
