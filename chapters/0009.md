# Chapter 9: Five Chairs

The bell saved me.

One short ring above the counter, the brass one wired down to Old Tan's till. One short meant friends, not uniforms. Three seconds later Boss Mei came through the bead curtain with her cardigan over her arm and her slippers slapping each stair. She looked at the room: Big Bird holding an empty noodle cup, Xiaoyu's hood back in the corner, me at the counter with bike oil on my hand. It was the look a woman gives a kitchen after her nephews have cooked in it.

"Bank was closed," she said.

Monk put the pink bell in his jacket pocket and didn't look at me again. I said goodnight to nobody in particular and went home.

I had a whole night and two periods of Chinese to build a lie. I was thirty-one inside. I'd lied to sponsors, to coaches, to my mother every Sunday for ten years about whether I was eating. By the end of second period, what I'd built was: small town, everybody talks.

It was a terrible lie. It was the only one I had, apart from one yuan ninety.

---

Starlight at ten to six on a Thursday was half asleep. Two vocational-school boys were arguing about a hero neither of them could play, and an old man in the back row was playing cards against the computer and losing out loud. Mei sat behind the counter sorting mahjong tiles into a cookie tray by suit, cigarette parked in the corner of her mouth. Somebody had tacked a new red poster next to the NO CREDIT sign.

"Fourteen's at six," she said without looking up.

"I want to talk business."

"You have one yuan ninety."

I stopped. "How do you—"

"You count it on the stairs. The stairs echo." She set down a bamboo tile. "Business."

So I gave her the speech I'd put together during the eye exercises. Her machines were falling apart. Twenty-seven still had a Coco Cola key. Nine had a worm that hid on people's USB sticks and woke up every time somebody plugged one in, which was why nine played a pop song at full volume every afternoon and everyone blamed the boy sitting at it. Half the window row froze if you opened two programs, and her customers had started hitting the monitors like they were vending machines. I would clean, fix keyboards, wipe the viruses and bring the crashed ones back up before anyone threw a chair. In exchange, machine 14 from six to seven, free.

I'd learned all of it in team houses later on, in crowded apartments where the only adult was a coach who thought computers ran on fear. I didn't mention that part.

"Fine," Mei said.

I'd prepared counterarguments. I'd prepared a fallback where I also mopped. "Fine?"

"You want me to argue? I'm very good at arguing."

"No."

"One condition." She pointed her cigarette at the stairs. "The little turtle's egg. Fang Hu. If he's up here when the streetlights come on, you take him down. To the corner by the rice shop. You watch him turn."

"Why me?"

"Because he doesn't listen to me." She went back to her tiles. "And because he'll go if it's you."

I didn't ask how she knew that. You don't haggle with a woman who's just said yes.

"Start with twenty-seven," she said.

---

I pried the keys off twenty-seven with a coin and soaked them in a bowl of hot water from the noodle kettle. I scrubbed them with a toothbrush Mei handed me from under the counter, and I decided not to ask whose it was. My fingers were slow on a keyboard and fine with a coin. It was the first time in two weeks my hands had done exactly what I told them.

At machine nine, a boy in a Nyke cap watched me open the task list, kill the worm, and dig its hidden file out of the stick like a splinter. Then he looked at me like I'd pulled a coin from his ear.

"Where'd you learn that?" Mei asked.

"Videos."

"Videos of what?"

"People. Fixing computers."

She took the cigarette out of her mouth. "Who would film that?"

It was a fair question for 2006. In sixteen years it would be half the internet. "Nobody," I said. "Forget it."

Monk came up the stairs at six exactly, army-green jacket, plastic sandals, a plastic bag knotted around something that clinked. Tang Yi had his weekly shave on Mondays, so by Thursday his head was a blue shadow. He nodded at Mei, went behind the counter and set the bag down in front of me.

The chain. The pink rabbit bell was on top of it.

Neither of us said anything. Mei's toolbox lived under the counter between the lock switch and a crate of Red Ox, and I found the pliers by feel. The second link was bent in the usual place, from a kid stamping on the pedal while she stood up. I eased it back a little at a time. Yesterday's oil had faded to a gray smear in the creases of my palm. Today I gave it company.

"You didn't answer," Monk said.

"Small town," I said. "Everybody talks."

He let that sit for exactly as long as it deserved. "Who."

"People. The market. My grandma's friends, they—"

"Nobody talks about Ling." He said it flatly. "She's nine. She goes to school and comes home."

I worked the link. It straightened with a small click I felt more than heard.

He could have pushed. He was seventeen, a head taller than me, and he had every reason. He watched me thread the link back, test the chain over my knuckles and hand it to him. He ran it through his fingers once, link by link, the way you'd check a string of beads for a crack.

"What do you want," he said.

"One game. You, me, three others. Against Streak's lot."

"I don't play with people."

"Yesterday a policeman walked through this room and you lied to him about a sticky key without blinking. Wrong machine, too. I need somebody who doesn't blink."

"I'm tired."

"That's the one you use when you're lying."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. It might have been a smile in a man who used them more. He put the chain in his pocket next to the bell.

"One game," he said.

---

By five past seven I had all of them in the back room. My free hour had ended at seven. I told myself I was still on the clock in case something broke.

The bare bulb buzzed. Uncle Kong boxes rose to the ceiling on three sides. Su Wen sat on the sofa that bites, at the oldest of the five machines, in the exact posture of a boy caught by his own father. His glasses were pushed up and his coins were stacked by the keyboard in a neat column of seven. He'd been playing when I opened the door. He wasn't playing now.

"I'm not here to tell anybody," I said.

"You were under fourteen yesterday," he said. "I could hear the little one breathing through the boxes." Then, as if it cost him a coin: "I wasn't going to tell anybody either."

That was the whole conversation. It was enough.

Big Bird filled the doorway. Zhao Dapeng had come up with Mei's seven o'clock bowl of beef noodles, because his father had decided that if his son was going to climb those stairs anyway, he could be useful while doing it. He still held the empty tray against his chest like a shield.

"Dad gave me six minutes," he announced. "Four are for the stairs."

"It's twenty steps."

"I go slow so he hears me coming back."

Xiaoyu leaned on the boxes by the door with her hood up and her headphones round her neck. When I'd gone to 38 to ask her, she'd said, "I'm not joining," and I'd said, "Then come say no in person," and she'd come. Mostly, I thought, to watch me fail with a good view. She spoke in Typhoon's voice, the low scratchy one. Big Bird had stared at her for a full five seconds when I said the name, then put his hand out and said, "Brother Typhoon. Big fan." She'd shaken it. Su Wen had looked at her wrist for one second too long and then at nothing. He was good at keeping things.

Monk stood in the doorway to the counter so he could hear the bell. He was holding a mop because Mei had walked past.

"Mid lane goes to Typhoon," I said.

"I'm not on your team," Xiaoyu said. "But if I were, mid's mine, and that's not a vote."

"Big Bird, offlane. Alone against two, all game."

"Alone?" He looked betrayed. "Who do I talk to?"

"Them. Loudly. It's the best part."

He thought about it, and you could watch it go down like a meal. "Okay."

"Su Wen, support."

Su Wen pushed his glasses down. "Support gets the least gold."

"Support decides when everybody else gets theirs. You'll be counting anyway. Count for five people."

He was quiet. His thumb moved against the desk, one tap, as it did at the top of the thirty. "...Acceptable."

"Monk carries."

"Monk plays?" Big Bird said. "Since when does Monk play?"

"Night list," said Monk. "Number one."

"The night list doesn't count," Xiaoyu said. "Everybody on it is asleep."

"Counts at night."

"And thirty-four does what," she said. "Sits in the middle and talks?"

"I fill whatever's missing, and I call."

"You call." She tilted her head. Under the hood I could see the ragged edge of her hair, shorter on the left. "You lost to me twice in a row."

"I lost with my hands. I'm calling with my head."

"Your head's attached to your hands."

"Loosely."

Big Bird snorted, and then looked guilty for snorting on my side.

I pointed through the doorway at the poster on the counter wall. I'd read it while the keys dried. QINGSHUI FIRST NET CAFE AotA TOURNAMENT. Sunday, October 15, at Thunder Net City on West Street. Teams of five. Fifty yuan entry. First prize six hundred yuan and a hundred free hours. At the bottom, in smaller characters, as if whoever wrote it hoped nobody would read that far: Players under 18 require a guardian's signature and ID number.

"Fifty," Su Wen said at once. "Ten each." He looked at his column of seven coins. "I have seven."

"I have one ninety," I said.

"I have a beef," said Big Bird. "I mean I can get beef. Not money. Beef."

"We'll get it," I said. "Sunday afternoon, three o'clock, one game against Streak, the third-building kid and whoever they drag in. If we're garbage, we don't enter. If we're not, we do."

"Sunday I've got extra English till four," Su Wen said.

"Sunday's our busiest lunch," said Big Bird. "Dad counts the bowls."

"I sleep till two," said Monk.

"Four-thirty, then." Somewhere in the future, I thought, there was a person whose whole job was this, and I'd never needed one more.

"We need a name," Big Bird said. "For the poster. They write it on the poster."

"They don't—"

"Flying Beef Brothers."

Silence. The bulb buzzed.

"No," said Xiaoyu, in the low voice.

"Typhoon's a brother," Big Bird counted on his fingers. "Monk's a brother. Su Wen's a—"

"Please don't," said Su Wen.

"Thirty-four and Four Mistakes," Xiaoyu offered.

"Qingshui Junior Middle Extracurricular Group," said Su Wen, and then, hearing it out loud: "No. Forget I said that."

"Starlight."

All five of us turned. Fang Hu was sitting on top of the Uncle Kong boxes in the far corner, knees up under his huge denim jacket, sleeves rolled four times, grinning down at us with the gap in his teeth. He'd been there long enough to look comfortable.

"Since when are you—" I started.

"Since the beef." He swung his scabbed knees. "It's Starlight. That's where you play. Stupid if you call it something else."

It was a kid's reason, and it was better than anything we'd come up with.

The bead curtain rattled. Mei stood in the doorway behind Monk with a cigarette halfway to her mouth, come to tell Big Bird his six minutes were up. She looked at the boy on the boxes, then at the rest of us, and for one second she didn't move. The ash on her cigarette got longer. I'd seen that stillness on players, the half-second after a fight goes wrong, when the hands stop and the head keeps going.

"Don't be late for anything," she said, and left.

Big Bird turned to me. "Was that a yes?"

"From her," said Monk, "that's a speech."

"Starlight," Su Wen said quietly, trying it out. He nodded once.

"Fine." Xiaoyu pulled her hood lower. "I'll play the tournament. For the six hundred. That's business, not listening to your speech, so don't get excited. I'm not on your team until you beat me. You've got till the fifteenth, thirty-four."

"I'll beat you before then."

"You'll try."

Big Bird had wandered out to the counter to read the poster himself. He read it the way he ate, slowly and with his whole face. Then he came back in.

"Guardian signature," he said. "What's a guardian? Like a parent?"

"Like an adult," Su Wen said.

We went round the room in silence, and I watched each of them do the math. Big Bird's father would sign it with Big Bird's ear. Su Wen just shook his head, very small. Xiaoyu said, "Mine aren't around," fast and flat, and nobody touched it. Monk said, "I'm seventeen," which was true and didn't help. And my grandmother would sign it, and then pickle me in the big jar by the door, the one that had burped since before I was born.

Five kids, one poster, no adults.

"My dad's an adult," said Fang Hu helpfully, from the top of the boxes.

Big Bird dropped the tray.

Outside the little back window, the streetlights had come on along the main street, a line of tired orange all the way to the rice shop. I stood up. No groan. I noticed and didn't comment. Then I held out my hand to the boy on the boxes.

"Come on," I said. "Corner. I watch you turn."
