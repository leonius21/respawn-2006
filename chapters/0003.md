# Chapter 3: Sunday Call

By Sunday my fever had officially broken, because Grandma said so.

She'd checked at noon on Saturday, as she always had. Between a customer and the tofu man's dog, she grabbed my head and pressed her forehead to mine, and she declared me "lukewarm, like bad tea." Fang Hu had abandoned me at the edge of the market the moment he saw which stall I was walking toward, as if he could smell grandmothers. That night I gave her back the money with the peppercorns. She counted the coins twice and sniffed the bag. She told me the bridge man had cheated me on the weight. He had. Then she asked where the rest had gone.

"A notebook. For school."

"Show me."

I showed her a notebook. It came from my desk drawer, where fifteen-year-old me had kept it untouched and pristine. She looked at it, then at me, and said "Hn," which in her language covers everything from *I believe you* to *I will remember this on my deathbed*.

So on Sunday morning I was broke. I knew it for certain because I'd turned my room upside down looking for my younger self's savings. I found them in a cookie tin under the bed: eleven yuan and forty fen, a Peacecraft sticker, and a note in my own handwriting that said DON'T TOUCH, LIN CHEN. That told me the kid had known exactly who the thief in this house was.

At ten past twelve the Novia rang.

Grandma had been at the table pinching the ends off green beans. She reached the TV faster than her knee should have let her. The phone lived up there between the plastic peonies and the lace doily, wrapped in a handkerchief like something you'd smuggle across a border. She unfolded the cracked hinge with both thumbs, very slowly.

"Wei! WEI! Can you hear? It's me. Who else would it be, it's my phone."

The pickle jars along the wall gave one of their slow wet burps.

"Eaten. Yes. Eaten. The knee's fine." She said this while standing on the other leg. "He's here. He had a fever. No, it's gone. He's strange, though. I'll give him to you."

She didn't give me the phone. She came around the table and held it to my ear herself, with both hands, bent over me, close enough that I could smell the vinegar on her fingers. In her mind the hinge was one bad conversation away from coming apart, and she wasn't going to let a melon-brained wa be the one holding it when it did.

The line hissed. Under the hiss, a voice cleared its throat.

"Chen Chen."

My father. He was forty-one. I'd forgotten that he'd ever been forty-one. The last voice I had for him was older, rougher, and said things like "your mother worries" in place of saying anything himself. This one was younger than I'd expected and just as stiff. It was a man reading his own lines off a card.

"Ba," I said, and then had to stop for a second, because my throat had other plans.

"School's started?"

"Friday."

"Your ranking. From last term."

I did not know my ranking from last semester. I hadn't known it for sixteen years. Somewhere in 2006 that number existed and mattered enormously to at least three people, and I had let it fall out of my head along with my old QiQi password and the capital of Mongolia.

"Middle," I said.

"Middle where?"

"Middle of the... middle."

Static. Then: "Teacher Su's boy is first again, I suppose."

"Yes, Ba." I didn't actually know that either. But I knew Su Wen, and Su Wen had been first since birth.

"Hn." It was Grandma's noise, handed down. Then there was a muffled exchange on his end, the sound of the phone changing hands, and my mother arrived like a gust of wind.

"Chen Chen? Your Nainai says you had a fever. Are you eating? You sound thin."

"How can somebody sound thin?"

"You do. Are you eating?"

"I'm eating. She makes me eat two eggs a day. Ma—" And here is where I made my mistake. I was sitting at my grandmother's folding table with her alive and breathing vinegar on me, and on the line was my mother at thirty-nine. Her voice was tired in a way I'd only understood after I'd been tired like that myself. All the soft things came out of me at once. "Ma, how are you two? Is it hot down there? Are they making you do overtime? You should sit down when you can. Tell Ba to look after his back, he lifts things wrong, he always—"

Silence, not static. Real silence, the expensive kind.

"Chen Chen," my mother said carefully. "Did you break something?"

"What?"

"Is it the TV? Did something happen at school? Do you need money? Tell me how much. Don't tell your father."

"I don't need money."

"How much?"

Grandma leaned down to the phone, still holding it to my ear, so now we were three heads around one Novia. "He's been strange since the fever," she announced. "Yesterday he thanked me for the porridge. Twice."

"Twice?" My mother's voice went up an octave. "Ma, take him to the clinic—"

"I told him. He bought peppercorns instead."

"Why would he buy *peppercorns*—"

There was a scuffle on the Dongguan end, and my father came back on. Somewhere behind him a loudspeaker was saying something about shifts.

"That's seven minutes," he said, as if reading a meter. "This line costs."

"Ba—"

"Study hard. Don't end up like us."

Click. Then three flat beeps, then nothing. Grandma took the phone away from my ear, looked at it suspiciously as if it had hung up on its own, folded it shut with both thumbs and wrapped it back in its handkerchief.

In the other life, I'd spent these calls saying "en." En, I'm eating. En, school's fine. En, bye. Then I'd hand the phone back and go out. Later, when I was in Chengdu and then Shanghai, Sunday became scrim day, and I stopped picking up at all, and they stopped asking why. Don't end up like us. I'd heard it all through my childhood like the sound of a fridge, and then I'd gone out and ended up worse. They'd at least had each other in that factory dorm. I'd had a heater I was too cheap to plug in.

Grandma put the phone back between the peonies.

"They work hard," she said, not looking at me. Then she went back to her green beans, and after a while she added, "Your mother cried a bit. She does that. Eat something."

---

At six that evening I climbed the dark staircase beside the hardware shop with my red free-hour card and two yuan from the cookie tin. I felt mildly guilty about the tin. The note had been very clear.

Boss Mei was at the counter in her cardigan and slippers, losing at mahjong to three women who were all also smoking under the NO SMOKING sign. She had already written WANG JIANJUN in her ledger before I opened my mouth. She took the card and my two yuan, stamped the card with something, and pointed her cigarette at the room. Two hours.

"Not 27."

"Did somebody fix the key?"

"No. Sit somewhere else." She went back to her tiles. "You look like you slept. Good. Chopsticks should sleep."

I took machine 14, in the middle row, between a boy asleep with his mouth open and a man in a vest playing cards against the computer. I didn't open Lianzhan. I'd decided on the walk over that I wasn't going to lose another game to Streak. That didn't mean I'd win. It meant I wasn't going to play him until my hands deserved it.

So I did what I'd made rookies do in 2017, and what every rookie had hated. I opened an empty AotA map with no enemies and no teammates. Just me, a hero, and the creeps marching down the middle lane. Then I last-hit them. Wave after wave. Only the killing blow gives you gold, so the whole art is hitting at the exact moment a creep's health drops low enough to die, not a hair earlier and not a hair later. My head knew the moment. My finger arrived at it the way a bus arrives in Qingshui, sometime that day.

The first ten minutes, I got about half. Half is what bad players get. I knew that because I'd spent years being disgusted with players who got half.

Every hero's attack has a wind-up, the little swing before the blow lands, and good players don't click when the creep is low. They click when the creep will be low by the time the swing lands. You're always hitting the future. Which, given my situation, should have come more naturally to me than it did.

Somewhere in the second wave of misses, my father's voice got back into my head. Don't end up like us. And the part of my brain that wasn't busy watching creep health bars started doing sums.

Grandma's stall: a few hundred a month. My parents: eight hundred a month sent home, and seven minutes of phone call counted like gold coins. Me: nine yuan and change of stolen savings. The bus ticket to Chengdu was thirty. The cafe tournament I half-remembered from this fall would pay a few hundred, maybe, if it paid in money at all instead of free hours. The world championship would one day pay a million US dollars to the winners, and that day was five years and about four thousand of these missed last-hits away.

But I was a man from the future. Men from the future get rich. It was practically a rule. I set my hero to stand still, sat back, and began to plan my fortune.

Stocks. Stocks had gone up. I was sure of that. There'd been a year when everyone's uncle bought stocks, and Grandma's mahjong friends talked about stocks instead of their daughters-in-law, and then there'd been a year when nobody mentioned stocks again. Which year? Some year. Which stocks? The ones that went up. My entire understanding of the Chinese economy from 2006 to 2022 was that a man at Starlight had once lost his wedding money and cried on machine 9.

Houses. Houses in Chengdu had gone up. Everyone knew that. You needed a house to buy a house, though, and I had a cookie tin.

And then it came to me, and I nearly said it out loud.

Bitcon. Everyone had gotten rich on Bitcon. Kids younger than me, teammates' cousins, a support player I knew who'd retired at twenty-four and bought his mother a restaurant. You only had to buy it early. I was as early as a person could possibly be.

I opened the browser. The page loaded the way pages did in 2006, in horizontal stripes, while the ADSL modem behind the counter thought about its life. I typed into Baidoo with real hope in my heart.

*No results found for "bitcon". Did you mean: bitcom?*

I tried it in Chinese. I got a computer-science glossary entry about bits and a company in Shenzhen that sold modem cables.

I sat there and tried to remember when it had started. 2008, 2009, 2010? I had exactly one Bitcon fact. Some guy had once bought two pizzas with ten thousand of them, which became the funniest and saddest story on the internet. I didn't know the year, or the guy. I didn't know what a person did to get one, or where you kept it, or whether you needed a computer that wasn't held together by Coco Cola. Sixteen years of the twenty-first century had passed in front of my face, and I'd spent all of it looking at creep health bars.

"Bit-coin," said a voice at my elbow. "What's that? Is it a game?"

Fang Hu was standing between my chair and the sleeping boy's. His jacket sleeves were rolled four times, and the red scarf hung half out of one pocket. Nobody had seen him come in, which I would learn was his main talent.

"It's money," I said.

"Game money?"

"Kind of."

"Can I have some?"

"There isn't any. Yet."

He frowned at the screen, then at me. "Then how do you know about it?"

"I dreamed it."

"My dad says dreaming is just being lazy with your eyes shut." He leaned over to look at the map, where my hero stood alone among the slaughtered creeps of an empty lane. "Who are you playing against?"

"Nobody."

"Who's winning?"

The man in the vest laughed so hard he lost his card game. Across the room, Boss Mei looked up from her tiles.

"Fang Hu! You little turtle's egg. Out! Do you want me to call your father? Do you want him to come up those stairs?"

"He's on nights," Fang Hu said, but he went. At the bead curtain he turned around and pointed at me. "Still a maybe!" Then he was gone down the stairs, two at a time, with a cough at the bottom that turned into a laugh.

I closed the browser. I had forty minutes left. I used every one of them on the creeps, and in the last wave I got most of them. Not all. Most.

---

That night, at the folding table, I opened my math homework, discovered that I could no longer factor a quadratic, and closed it again with real fear. Then I turned the notebook over and opened it from the back, where teachers don't look.

I wrote:

*STOCKS — up. 2007?? Which ones?? (ask who)*

*HOUSES — Chengdu — buy (with what)*

*PEAR — something big? (can't buy, am 15)*

And under that, in bigger letters, because it was my best idea:

*BITCON?? (watch for it)*

I looked at the list for a long time. Four questions, seven question marks, and not one answer. It was the financial plan of a man who had once mistaken a tournament for a life.

Then I wrote the only thing on earth I actually knew. *AotA.* And under it: *2 hours a day. Every day. Hands.*

And under that, smaller, because it felt stupid to write down and I wrote it anyway: *Sundays. Ask Ma what they eat down there. Let Ba finish a sentence.*

From the other room, Grandma's voice came through the curtain.

"Chen-wa. Light off. Electricity isn't free either."

I turned it off. In the dark, the sleeping dog on the ceiling was just a stain again, and the pickle jars burped, and I lay there thinking that the one thing I truly knew was also, as luck would have it, the one thing that was going to pay.

But I didn't sleep.

Part of me was still waiting. For two nights I'd gone to bed half sure I would wake up in the other room: the rented one, December, the heater unplugged, my feet sticking out of the blanket. Maybe all this was a dream at the end of dying, the brain's last screensaver. I lay on my back with my eyes open and waited for the cold to come back and take it all away.

It didn't come. What came instead was a snore.

The wall between my room and Grandma's was one brick and some tired plaster, and through it came Lin Guifen, sixty-eight, snoring like a small engine that didn't quite trust its fuel. In, a long rattle. A pause, long enough that my heart stopped with it. Then a snort, and a mutter in her sleep that sounded like haggling. Then in again.

For eight years she had been a photo and a name on a stone. I'd dreamed about her often enough, after. In those dreams she never snored. She stood at the stove with her back to me, and I always woke up before she turned around.

I put my palm flat on the wall. The plaster was cool and a little damp. Through it I could feel nothing at all and hear everything.

Then, to be sure, I did the stupid thing. I put the side of my hand in my mouth and bit it, hard, then harder, until my eyes watered.

It hurt. It hurt exactly like a fifteen-year-old's hand that has been bitten by a thirty-one-year-old idiot.

"I'm really here," I whispered to the dog on the ceiling. The dog had no comment.

On the other side of the wall, Grandma snored, stopped, and muttered something that might have been "too much chili."

I laughed into the pillow, very quietly, and then I stopped, because it wasn't really laughing anymore.

All right. No dream. No screensaver. The calendar on its nail, the Novia with the glued hinge, my father counting minutes like coins, the radish, the eggs. All of it was 2006, and all of it was mine again, along with sixteen years of knowing exactly how it went wrong.

Fine. If this is real, then I'm going to do it right.

I left my hand on the wall until her breathing went slow and even, and for a while after that.
