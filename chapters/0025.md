# Chapter 25: Thunder Net City

That morning Fang Hu had turned up at Grandma's door at eight with his composition paper rolled in his fist, and the two of them spent an hour at her folding table with their heads almost touching. Whenever I came near, Grandma laid her hand flat over the page and told me the bowls in the sink were waiting, since I was a professional now.

So I no longer knew what his father did in that essay. At half past two I met the others at the bridge.

Monk had sent word through Big Bird that he was asleep. For Monk, that was a long message.

Thunder Net City was on West Street, in the part of Qingshui that had grown up around the bus station while nobody was watching. In my first life I'd been up its stairs maybe twice. It was where the boys with real pocket money played, and I'd told myself I didn't want to go. The street had tiled shop fronts, a bank with a guard who mostly sat, and three phone shops selling the same Novia at three different prices. Outside the Golden Bridge Hotel the sidewalk was red with spent firecracker paper, and a woman was sweeping it into a pile the size of a dog. Somewhere inside, the Party secretary's niece was getting married over a fish, and under the fish was my grandmother's cabbage. I didn't mention it. Fang Hu would have told the woman with the broom.

Thunder took the second and third floors above a phone shop. Its yellow lightning-bolt sign was lit at three in the afternoon. In Qingshui that counted as showing off. A board at the bottom of the stairs said 3 YUAN/HOUR, MEMBERS 2.5, NO MINORS, and somebody had pasted a mooncake ad over half of NO MINORS.

Fang Hu ran up ahead of us. When I reached the landing he was standing at the top with his mouth open and one hand pressed flat to the left side of his jacket, over the pocket where he kept his receipts.

"I won," he said.

"Nobody raced you."

"I won anyway."

Big Bird came up behind me, puffing loud enough for both of them. Xu Qing said "Volume" without looking up from her notebook, and nobody else looked at Fang Hu. I did. Big Bird had his breath back before he finished wiping his forehead. Fang Hu took longer. He grinned the whole time, gap tooth and all, and when he saw me watching he moved the hand off his chest and pointed it at the door.

"Ge, they've got a curtain."

It was a strip door of thick plastic, the kind butchers hang to keep the cold in. We pushed through, and the air conditioning hit all of us at once.

Big Bird stopped in the doorway and shut his eyes. A boy trying to get out behind him said "excuse me" twice, then squeezed around through the gap by the wall.

"It works," Big Bird said to nobody. "Their air works."

"Move, Zhao Dapeng."

"I'm moving. I'm just. It works."

It was big, sixty machines at least, in straight rows under fluorescent tubes that didn't buzz. The monitors had flat faces. The chairs had armrests, and every one of them matched. Smoking was allowed only in a glass room at the back, so the front rows smelled of floor cleaner and somebody's oranges. A girl of about twenty sat behind the counter, cracking sunflower seeds and dropping the shells into a cone of folded newspaper. She kept a neater pile than most people keep anything.

In the far corner by the window, at the last machine in the row, a hood was up.

Fang Hu opened his mouth. I put my hand on top of his head and turned him toward the counter. The hood didn't turn. Either Typhoon hadn't seen us, or Typhoon had watched us come up West Street from that window and had had a full minute to decide not to.

Xu Qing looked at the hood, and her pen made one short stroke. I didn't ask.

She had come for the owner. "Rule eight," she'd said on the bridge, and I'd nodded as if I remembered which one rule eight was. The owner came out of an office behind the counter when the girl called him. He was somewhere in his thirties, in a white shirt tucked into pressed pants, with a Motocoola clipped to his belt and hair combed back with water. Everybody on West Street called him Boss Luo, the girl told us, as if there were any other way to call him. I remembered the name and the shirt. He'd run Thunder for years, the first time around. Past that I knew nothing about him.

"The tournament," said Xu Qing. "I am the treasurer of a team. I have questions."

Boss Luo looked us over: a girl with a notebook, a boy with glasses holding a library bag, a boy the size of a wardrobe, me, and Fang Hu, who waved. "A team."

She took the form out of its plastic sleeve and gave it to him. He read it, then went back and read the sponsor line again, and laughed once through his nose.

"Mei Lan's sponsoring."

"That is what the line says."

"Does Mei Lan know what sponsoring means?"

"It is defined on the back," said Xu Qing. "Paragraph two."

He turned the page over and actually read paragraph two. I liked him a little for that.

"Form's fine," he said. "Seventy-five. Fifty to enter, five a head deposit. You get the deposit back after your last game. Cash, at check-in, on the day."

"Not before?"

"I don't hold children's money for ten days. Somebody's mother comes asking." He said it without any edge. He'd had a mother come asking before. "Check-in's at nine. First games at ten."

"Nine by which clock?"

He pointed at the clock above the counter. Xu Qing held up her wrist, the cracked plastic strap facing us, compared the two, and wrote something down.

"Yours is one minute slow," she said.

"Then come at nine by yours."

"How many teams?"

"Eight slots. Five gone." He took a clipboard from under the counter. "Mine. Two from Deyang. One from Mianzhu. Number One Senior High, if their teacher doesn't find out." He uncapped a pen, stopped, put it back in his pocket and picked up a pencil instead. "Starlight, six. In pencil. Ink when I see the money."

Xu Qing watched the pencil through every stroke. When he finished she gave him a single short nod. Starlight was in pencil. So was Typhoon.

"You want to see who you're playing?" Boss Luo tipped his chin toward the window. "My boys. They're there every day."

There were five machines by the front window with a hand-lettered card on the first one: RESERVED. The boys at them wore matching black T-shirts with the yellow bolt printed on the back, so somebody had paid for shirts. They were older than us, seventeen to nineteen, and one of them had a mustache he was clearly proud of. Each had a bowl of Uncle Kong noodles beside his keyboard with two ham sausages in it, plus a can of Red Ox. Nobody went to the counter to pay. The girl brought the food over and went back to her seeds.

We stood behind them the way we'd stood behind Streak's lot three weeks ago, and they didn't care any more than Streak had. The difference was everything else.

They were in a Lianzhan room against a team from somewhere with a slower connection. I could read the score off their shoulders. The captain sat in the middle seat, the biggest of them, with a buzz cut and a white Nyke wristband, playing Earthbreaker under the ID *TNC.Hammer*. He didn't shout. Nobody shouted. When the enemy mid drifted down into the river for a rune, their support shifted first, a half-step into the trees, and Hammer said, in an ordinary voice, "Three. Two. Go."

All five came out of the fog on the word. Earthbreaker split the ground behind the enemy mid and the wall went up across the river. A stun, fire, a frost arrow, and the hero was dead in about two seconds. Then the five of them walked to the tower and took it without a word. The boy with the mustache ate a sausage left-handed while they hit it.

"Oh," said Big Bird, quietly. He didn't say anything else for a while.

I watched two more fights. Each time it was the same count in the same flat voice, "three, two, go," and all five moved on "go." That was why they were good. Five boys who'd sat in these chairs every afternoon for a year could move on one word, and nothing I'd built in four weeks moved like that.

It was also something I could use. At the tournament we'd be in this room, fifteen steps away, and Hammer would be telling everyone within earshot exactly when his team was coming. He wasn't stupid, though. The first time it cost him, he'd stop. So it could only be used once, and late, and not in the first game.

On the third "go," across the room, the hood turned toward the window for a second, then turned back to its own screen.

Su Wen hadn't been watching the game. He stood at an empty machine at the end of the row with his hands behind his back and his face close to the monitor, the way you'd look at a fish in a tank.

"The mouse is lit," he said.

He tipped it up. There was a red glow underneath and no ball.

"Ours stick," he said. "Every afternoon at about four. You push harder." He set the mouse down as if it might be fragile. "So our hands have learned to push harder. And this screen doesn't shimmer. I can't see the lines."

Starlight's old monitors redrew the picture sixty times a second. That was why they shivered at the corner of your eye and sent you home with a headache. These were set higher, so everything on them reached your eyes a hair sooner. I told him that.

Su Wen looked at the screen for a long time.

"So we've been practicing on the wrong thing," he said.

"We've been practicing on the thing we have."

"Yes," he said, which wasn't agreement.

"One hour," said Big Bird. "Three yuan. We all get a turn."

"No," said Xu Qing.

"THREE YUAN."

"Three yuan of the fund in a competitor's register. No. Under rule—" She turned back a page, then another. "There is no rule. There will be one tonight."

I checked my pockets anyway. They didn't have three yuan in them, and they hadn't since Monday.

Big Bird looked at the counter. Then he walked over to it.

"Can I test a chair?"

The girl looked up at him. "They're chairs."

"I'm heavy. The tournament's here. If I break one in the final, it's your chair."

"So don't break it."

"I have to know before." He leaned on the counter, and the counter creaked. "You eat at Zhao's. Tuesdays. Big bowl, extra chili, no coriander."

She stopped with a seed halfway to her teeth. "How do you know that?"

"I make it."

She looked at the office door, then at the empty machine nearest the counter, then back at him. "Five minutes. Don't log in to anything."

Big Bird sat down. The chair said something about it. He bounced twice, listened, and stood up again. "It's good," he announced. "Lin Chen. Sit."

Peacecraft was on the desktop, and the AotA map was in its folder where it always is. I started a game alone, took Soul Fiend, and walked him to the middle lane as the first creeps came down. Five minutes was barely enough time to find out anything. It turned out to be plenty.

I moved the mouse the distance my hand had learned at Starlight, and the cursor shot straight past the creeps into the trees. I pulled it back and it shot past the other way. The button gave as soon as I touched it, so I clicked before I meant to, and Soul Fiend swung at a creep with a sliver of health left a moment early, and the tower took the gold.

"MISSED," said Big Bird. "MISSED. GOT IT. MISSED."

Xu Qing tapped her pen twice on the notebook.

"Two of nine," said Su Wen, very quietly, just beside my ear.

At Starlight, on the sticky ball mouse with the scratched pad, I'd have had six of those, maybe seven. My fingers were fifteen and still slow, but they were slow on that mouse in particular, and in four weeks they had learned it like a bad bicycle. By the fourth minute I'd made my hand smaller. I took three in a row, and on the third, something in my wrist relaxed that I hadn't known was tense.

The girl tapped the desk with one finger. "Chair's tested."

I didn't argue. I closed the game and stood up. My right hand had a cramp in the web of the thumb, and I shook it out under the desk where nobody could see.

Big Bird left at twenty to five at a jog, because his father wanted him back early to start the dinner prep. The rest of us stayed until Su Wen's library would have closed, standing behind Thunder's boys and learning nothing that made us feel better. They were better than us. I didn't mean in some future I could remember. I meant today, on these chairs, against any five I could have sat down; they'd have taken us apart in half an hour and eaten during it.

On the way out Fang Hu waved at the hood. The hood didn't move.

The sky over the river was going gray-orange by the time we reached the bridge, with the moon already up above the hill road, one day short of full. Tomorrow was Mid-Autumn. Every window on the far bank would have mooncakes on a plate in it.

Xu Qing stopped halfway across. She didn't open the notebook. She knew the numbers by heart.

"Ten days," she said. "Fifty-one yuan forty still missing. Four days without practice. And the best team plays at home, on their own machines, with their own food." She looked at me. "That is the situation."

Fang Hu kicked a stone through the railings into the water and didn't say anything, which for him was a statement.

I could have told them about Hammer's countdown. I could have told them a lot of things. Most of them would have sounded like a man in a suit at a podium, and none of them would have changed the mouse.

"Starlight opens tomorrow," I said. "We start again tomorrow." I turned to Su Wen. "Count how many days of practice we really have."

He didn't use his fingers. He looked at the water for about as long as it took the current to carry Fang Hu's ripple under the bridge.

"Nine," said Su Wen. "If nobody is late."

Xu Qing wrote that down after all.

At the end of the bridge she went left and Su Wen went right with his library bag, and I walked Fang Hu to the rice-shop corner as I did every night. He talked the whole way, about the air conditioning, the strip door, whether the counter girl ate her sunflower seeds shells and all when nobody was looking. He walked, though. He didn't run once.

At the corner he stopped and stood on one foot.

"Nainai says you can read it when it's done," he said.

"When's it done?"

"When she says." He switched feet. "That's her rule. She's got more rules than the monitor. She just doesn't write them down."

Then he went off up the alley toward the dark windows, and I stood there until his footsteps stopped.
