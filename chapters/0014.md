# Chapter 14: The Hood

Nobody was home, which was normal, so He Xiaoyu didn't say good morning to anyone.

She said it in her head to the rice cooker, which was warm, and to the note under the thermos, which was written on the torn flap of a detergent box in her mother's square, careful characters. *Rice in the cooker. Ten yuan for the week. Cut your hair properly.* The ten yuan was folded into quarters underneath, soft as cloth, the way money came home from the Golden Bridge Hotel by the bus station. Her mother washed the hotel's sheets from half past six at night until half past eight in the morning, in a basement where the steam came off the presses so thick you could lose a hand in it. Xiaoyu left for school at seven. Some Sundays they overlapped.

She put the money in her shoe, ate the rice standing up, and took the gray hoodie off the hook by the door.

It had been He Lei's. Her brother had left it there the morning after New Year when he got on the bus to Shenzhen, where he now soldered the insides of phone chargers twelve hours a day and called on the neighbor's phone once a month to say the food was bad and the money was good. The sleeves came past her fingertips. She'd cut thumbholes in the cuffs with the kitchen scissors, the same scissors she'd used on her hair, and the hoodie no longer smelled like him. It smelled like her, and faintly like Starlight's smoke, and she wore it anyway. Inside it nobody could see how thin her wrists were. With the hood up, nobody could see anything.

---

Class 1 sat on the third floor of the new building, where the windows actually opened and the fans actually turned, and the people who sat there knew it. It was the class with the best teachers and the best rankings and the worst manners.

At the morning break Zeng Haoran leaned across the aisle. He had bangs gelled so stiff it stuck out over his forehead like the eave of a roof, and he was always looking for rain.

"He Xiaoyu. Did you cut it in the dark?"

His friends turned around for it. The left side of her hair stopped above her ear; the right side reached her jaw. The left was always the side where the scissors went too far. She'd cut it herself in August and trimmed it again last Thursday night in the bathroom mirror, with the stairwell light buzzing through the frosted glass, because the hair kept falling in her eyes during laning and a haircut on the main street cost three yuan, which was an hour and a half of machine 38.

"Yes," she said. She took the compass out of her geometry box and opened it so the point caught the light. "Want me to do yours? I'm better in daylight."

The friends laughed at him instead. Zeng Haoran said something about crazy girls and sat back down, his bangs very still. That was the end of it, and she'd won it, and she spent the whole of morning reading with her left hand covering her left ear anyway.

It was thirty degrees by lunchtime. September in Qingshui was still summer, it just had less to say. She took the hoodie off and hung it over her chair back, because the alternative was fainting into a bowl of school-canteen cabbage, and she was eating that cabbage at her desk, alone, with the English book propped against her lunch box, when someone stopped at the back door of the classroom.

Xu Qing.

Xiaoyu didn't need to turn around to know. She knew it because the noise in the room dropped by a third. Class 3 had the hygiene inspection this week, and its monitor was doing the rounds as she did everything: ponytail straight down the middle of her back as straight as a plumb line, uniform zipped to the exact top, and in her hand the green notebook every ninth grader had learned to fear. She stood in the doorway and looked at the room.

Then she looked at the chair.

At the gray hoodie hanging over it, hood dangling, thumbholes and all. She looked at it the way she looked at a stain.

Xiaoyu kept her eyes on her cabbage. Under the desk she found the hem with her foot and slid the hoodie down off the chair back, slowly, an inch at a time, until the whole thing lay in a heap behind her ankles. She had last-hit creeps with less concentration.

On Saturday, in Starlight's back room, Xu Qing had sat on the biting sofa and asked the hooded boy at 38 where he came from, and the hooded boy had said, in a voice like a gravel truck, "I'm from Deyang." Xu Qing had said "Hm." Xiaoyu had been thinking about that "Hm" for three days.

Xu Qing's pen moved. Something short.

"Class 1," she said to the room. "There is chalk dust in the blackboard trough. And there is a mop standing in a bucket. Mops do not stand in buckets. They hang. Minus two."

Someone near the window said, "Who cares about a mop?"

"The school," said Xu Qing, "and therefore me."

She looked at Xiaoyu for exactly as long as it took to write one more word. Then she turned on her heel and was gone down the corridor to class 2, where someone could be heard hiding a comic.

Xiaoyu finished the cabbage. She didn't taste any of it.

---

After school she went home and ate the rest of the rice. The apartment was what it always was at that hour: the rice cooker clicking as it cooled, the television under its embroidered cloth like a covered birdcage, He Lei's bed folded up against the wall with a sheet over it. Her mother's slippers stood by the door, pointing out. She did the English homework at the kitchen table, all of it, fast, because nobody would check and she'd rather have it done than have it hanging over her. At half past six she heard the neighbor's radio start the news. At a quarter to seven she went out.

She didn't go up Starlight's stairs. She stood in the bus shelter across the main street with her hood up and her hands inside her sleeves and watched the stairwell beside the hardware shop. Old Tan's wife, Qiu Fang, was at her stool by the hardware till, watching everything, as she watched everyone, so Xiaoyu stood behind the bus schedule.

At five past seven the streetlights came on. A minute after that, the thin boy came down the stairs with the small one.

Lin Chen. Thirty-four on the class-3 wall. The small one was Fang Hu, the sixth-grader with the scabby knees who talked at Starlight like a radio somebody had sat on, and he was talking now, walking backward in front of the older boy, arms going. Lin Chen walked with his hands in his pockets and nodded at intervals, slightly bent, like a retired man listening to his grandson explain a cartoon. At the rice-shop corner he stopped and stood there while the kid ran off down the side street, and he didn't move until the kid had turned. Then he went the other way toward the market.

Xiaoyu crossed the street.

Halfway up the stairs she met Big Bird coming down with an empty tray, which filled the stairwell completely, the way weather fills a valley. Zhao Dapeng, the noodle shop's son, in his red jersey. He saw the hood and lit up.

"Brother Typhoon! You missed him! He was here! He killed a virus on 22, it was making the screen go purple—"

"Hn," said Xiaoyu, in the gravel voice.

"Your voice is getting lower." Big Bird nodded, impressed. "Mine did that. It was terrible. For a year I sounded like a door." He turned sideways to let her past, which almost worked. "I have two minutes. Bye!"

He took the rest of the stairs two at a time.

---

Upstairs, Monk took her two coins and another two without looking at her face. Tang Yi had the night shift and a head shaved down to blue, and he moved through the cafe as if each step had been budgeted. Boss Mei was at the counter with a cigarette and three mahjong friends and didn't look up from her tiles.

"Two hours, Deyang," Mei said.

Xiaoyu's neck went hot inside the hood. Mei played a tile. That was all.

Machine 38 was in the corner by the window, where the light from the street came through the grime. Machine 37 next to it still showed everything in green. Nobody sat at 37. Over by the aisle, machine 14's chair was pushed back at an angle, the way somebody leaves a chair when they mean to come back tomorrow.

She took the MP3 player out of her pocket and set it beside the keyboard like a lucky coin.

It had been her brother's too, a gray plastic thing the size of a lighter with a wobbly screen and a hundred and twenty-eight megabytes of Cantonese pop he'd never come back for. On Sunday evening, after the trial game, before she'd played anything else, she'd dug the replay out of the Peacecraft folder on 38 and copied it onto the player, between two love songs. Until last week, plugging anything into a Starlight machine meant catching the worm off number 9, the one that made every computer in the room play the same pop song at full volume and got whoever was sitting nearest blamed for it. The song had stopped on Thursday. She knew who'd stopped it.

The file was called *34*.

Peacecraft replays did not rewind. You could speed them up or pause, but to see something twice you had to start over at minute zero and sit through the boys buying their first items again. She watched it at full speed first, through the Starlight side's eyes. Then she started it again.

On Sunday, at the time, it had looked like what Big Bird would call luck. Streak's five had seen a bleeding Soul Fiend in the river, all five had come, and Big Bird's Tidecaller had come out of the trees and thrown the whole lot of them in the air. She'd raised three fingers and folded one down: two razes out of three. She'd been generous.

Now she watched the minute before.

At fifteen-thirty, the Soul Fiend left the middle lane with a third of his health and walked to the river. Not ran: walked. He didn't buy anything. He didn't go home to heal, which was what anybody with a third of his health would do. He stood on the rune spot and waited, and at sixteen exactly the rune came up under his feet, and he didn't take it.

She paused it and sat back.

He'd left it there. For Streak. Who left mid for every rune, every two minutes, every game. Xiaoyu had played against Streak forty times and knew that as well as she knew her own mouse. She'd never once thought about it. It wasn't the kind of thing her hands needed to know. Her hands saw an opening and went, and by the time she understood why, the other hero was dead.

The Soul Fiend had been in the river before the opening existed. He'd made it.

She started the replay over and watched only Streak. Rune, rune, rune. At every even minute the Storm Sprite went to the river like a man who goes to the bathroom at the same hour every day. Then she started it over again and watched only Su Wen's support, which had lost its first Watcher Eye early, but after that had put every eye down about where it needed to be. Then once more, at quarter speed, through the fight itself.

The first raze landed. The second landed. The third went off a hair late and blew up a patch of empty water, because his fingers had been a quarter of a second behind his head. They always were. She'd beaten him twice on Day 8 in the mirror on exactly that quarter-second. Head attached to his hands, she'd told him. Loosely.

None of it had mattered. He'd been right about every single thing that happened before his hands were needed.

It irritated her so much that she took the hood down. The window fan blew on her neck. She put it back up.

She thought about how he'd said her name. The Saturday before last, at this machine, he'd said "He Xiaoyu" quietly, as though he'd known it for years, when nobody in the cafe knew it, when even the rumors about Typhoon put her in a Deyang college or the Riverside cafe. And then he'd taken her terms, no tricks, no using her name, and as far as she could tell he had never said it again, not to Big Bird, not to the class monitor, not even to the noodle boxes. She'd been listening.

She put the player back in her pocket and logged into Lianzhan.

The ladder room was half full. She opened a solo-mid challenge with her usual name on it, and somebody from Mianzhu took it before she'd finished cracking her knuckles. She beat him by four minutes; he left without typing anything. A boy from Deyang (an actual one) took the second and lasted six minutes, and typed *lag* as he died. The third was polite and good, with careful hands, and she killed him twice before seven minutes, and he typed *gg wp* and *how* and she didn't answer.

Three in a row. Her name sat at the top of the room, as usual.

She waited for it to feel like something. Usually it felt like a door shutting on cold air. Tonight it felt like the rice cooker clicking in an empty kitchen. All three had been over by minute three, she just hadn't bothered to finish them. None of them had stood in a river on purpose. None of them had made her think about anything at all.

She looked over at machine 14.

At the counter she said, in the gravel voice, "Paper."

Monk tore a pink slip off Mei's receipt pad without asking what it was for and laid a pen on top of it. Mei went on playing tiles. Xiaoyu took both back to 38 and wrote standing up, the hood hanging over the paper like a lamp shade.

She counted Sundays on the calendar in her head first. The twenty-fourth. The first of October, golden week. The eighth. On the fifteenth it would be too late. Three chances. If he couldn't do it in three, he couldn't do it.

*Sundays, 8 p.m. Machine 38. One try a week. Don't be late. Bring your brain.*

She read it over. She thought about signing it and didn't. He'd know. He'd known everything else.

She folded it in four, then once more, and walked down the aisle to machine 14 and slid it under the mouse, square in the middle of the pad, where nobody who sat down there could possibly miss it. The chair was still pushed back at its angle. She left it that way.

When she turned around Monk was looking at her from the counter, half-lidded.

"You didn't see that," she said.

"Didn't."

She went down the stairs with her hands inside her sleeves. Qiu Fang had gone in. The main street smelled of skewers and motorbike exhaust, and the apartment would be empty when she got there, the rice cooker cold, her mother's slippers pointing at the door. She walked home with the hood down the whole way, and her ear was cold, and she let it be.
