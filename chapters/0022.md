# Chapter 22: Takings

The stall key lived on a nail by the door, on a loop of string that had been red some years before I was born. At five-thirty the window was still black. Grandma was asleep in the back room with her left leg propped on a rolled quilt and her mouth a little open. Last night she'd taken a white pill from the drawer she says is for buttons and told me it was for her teeth.

I lifted the key off the nail. It ticked once against the wall. I stood still and listened. Nothing.

Under the salt jar I left a note: AT THE STALL. ATE. HOME AT DARK. Two of the three were true.

At that hour the market was mostly sounds. Shutters rattling up. A rooster that had no business being in town. Steam coming off the tofu man's cart before I could see the cart. Qiu Fang was already crouched over the coal stove at the tea-egg stall next to Grandma's. She had a scarf knotted over her hair, and her hands were stained brown to the wrist from twenty years of soy and tea leaves. She looked at me, then at the key, then at the empty air behind me where Grandma should have been.

"She knows you're here?"

"She'll find out."

Qiu Fang laughed through her nose and hooked a bamboo stool over to me with her foot. "Then sit now. You won't get to later."

I unlocked the plank cabinet and dragged the jars onto the trestle one by one: chili cabbage, white radish, ginger, long beans, the mustard greens Monk had taken home yesterday without arguing. Big brown glazed bellies with a lip of water around each lid to keep the air out, so the brine sighed at you when you lifted one. At the back sat a smaller jar, nearly black, with a cloth tied over its mouth and a chalk mark on the lid I didn't bother to read. I tasted every jar with a clean chopstick. The black one made me close my eyes. So the black one went in front.

I'd built the plan lying awake at two in the morning, so naturally it was a very good plan. The factory people were home from Guangdong for golden week, and by midweek they'd start dreading the cafeteria food in Dongguan and buy pickles to carry south in plastic tubs wrapped in three bags each. Mid-Autumn was Friday, and anybody who couldn't afford decent mooncakes for the in-laws would bring a jar instead. And on a holiday in Qingshui, everyone walks the market slowly with their whole family, buying nothing until they buy everything.

I knew them, too. Old Tan from the bicycle repair, who would sell the shop in 2011 and put up a pink sign for phones, bought radish, never cabbage, and always said he was only looking. The Liang sisters shopped for their mother, who was going to live to a hundred and two out of spite. I'd grown up watching these people walk past this stall. I'd just never stood on this side of the jars.

By seven I had a cup of toothpicks and a saucer of black-jar cabbage at the front of the trestle, the oldest trick in any market, and I had a line. Four deep. People ate free pickle off toothpicks, made faces of private happiness, and bought radish because it was cheaper and the free thing had put them in a buying mood.

Then Postman Wu's wife came by with her string bag. She'd been losing to Grandma at mahjong twice a week for longer than I'd been alive and had never forgiven her.

"Guifen's boy. Where's Guifen?"

"Resting. What can I get you, Ayi?"

"Chili cabbage. One jin."

"Five."

Qiu Fang stopped peeling an egg.

Postman Wu's wife looked at the jar, at me, and at the jar again, checking which of us had changed. "Five what?"

"...Yuan?"

"For five yuan the cabbage carries itself home. It's two. It's been two since the year the river came up. Is your grandmother dead, or did you sell her?"

The number had come from 2022. That last winter I'd bought chili cabbage at the supermarket by the bus station, in a plastic tub with a printed label, because by then there was no stall to buy it from. Whatever it cost there had stuck in my head as what chili cabbage costs. It was the price of the wrong year.

"Two," I said. "I was testing the market."

"Test your own mother." She held out the bag. "Two, and Guifen gives me the end of the radish free. You couldn't know that, so I'm telling you."

I put the radish end in. My hands were fine. The rest of me took a second.

At eight, Boss Mei walked through the market.

I almost didn't know her. Shoes, real ones, black with a small heel that clicked on the wet concrete. Her perm pinned flat. No cigarette. A brown handbag clamped under one arm. Starlight was shut; I'd passed the cardboard on its street door at twenty to six. In two lives I had never once seen Mei Lan below her own stairs before noon.

She saw me behind the jars and slowed by one step.

"Chopstick."

"Morning, Boss Mei."

Her eyes went to my sleeves. "Dry. Look at that." And she kept walking, clicking, toward the bridge and the county offices on the far side.

Qiu Fang watched her go. "Mei Lan in shoes," she said. "Somebody's crying today."

Grandma arrived at ten.

She was four and a half hours late to her own stall and looked at me like I was the one who'd wandered in. She had the broom upside down under her right arm, bristles in her armpit, and her own stool hooked over her left elbow. She crossed the market one careful step at a time while people called "Lin Nainai!" and "Aiya, your leg!" and she answered "What leg?" to every one of them.

I stood. "Nainai, you're supposed to—"

"I heard the key." She set her stool behind mine, lowered herself onto it with the bad leg straight out, and let her breath go. "Forty years I hear that key. I wanted to see what you'd do with it. Move your elbow. You're hiding the radish."

So for an hour I sold, and she sat behind me and told me how.

"Not her. Look at her bag. Already full of Gou's cabbage. She came to look at us and feel rich."

"Don't smile at Old Tan. You smile, he thinks you want something, he buys nothing. Look at the beans, Chen-wa. Look at the beans."

I looked at the beans. Old Tan said he was only looking, then bought two radishes in a voice that dared me to notice.

"Him," she said about a big man in a Dongguan factory jacket with a little girl on his shoulders. "Talk to the girl."

I talked to the girl. He bought three jin and a jar to carry them in.

She was doing what I did at machine 14 when a game was won before the first fight: watching the other side until it told her what it wanted. I'd written an article about that. Beijing was going to print it. She'd been doing it on this stool since before Beijing had heard of AotA.

Around eleven the market thinned out for lunch, and I tried.

"Nainai. I've been thinking."

"Since five. I know."

"From now on I'll do the stall. You rest the leg. I'll come before school and—"

"Before school." She nodded slowly. "Jars out at six. School at seven-thirty. Who sells from seven-thirty? Qiu Fang?"

"I'm busy," said Qiu Fang, without turning around.

"After school, then. And Saturdays—"

"You promised me." She said it quietly, which with her was the loud version. "Yesterday. With me on your back. Not one day of school for my leg. Did you promise me, or did a crow?"

I had nothing. There was no version of the math that worked, and she'd done it faster than I had.

She was quiet too, for a while. She fished a long bean out of the brine with her fingers, ate it, and wiped her fingers on her good knee.

"Chen-wa. You come here once and sell my cabbage, that's a present. I'll tell Qiu Fang about it till she's sick of me."

"Already am," said Qiu Fang.

"Every day, it's not a present anymore. You're taking it." She tapped the cloth on the black jar. "This. Them." A small tip of her head took in the market: the shutters, the steam, Old Tan pretending to examine someone else's beans. "You'd take my whole market and give me a bed for it. When I'm done, I'll tell you. You don't tell me."

I wanted to tell her what I knew. That last time, as far as I ever knew, nobody had told anyone anything, me least of all. She must have stood on that knee for years, until standing wasn't the problem anymore, and I'd heard how it ended over a phone, between games.

There was no version of that that worked either.

"Okay," I said.

She looked at me long enough to check what kind of okay it was. Then she said, "Good," and reached over to straighten the black jar's cloth, lifted it, and looked inside.

It was half empty. Less than half. For the first time I read the chalk on the lid: LIU.

"Aiya," said Grandma. Only that.

"Who's Liu?"

"Golden Bridge Hotel. The kitchen. She comes on the second day of every holiday." She set the cloth back very gently. "Since the hotel had one floor."

"When does she—"

Grandma looked past my shoulder. "Now."

Ayi Liu was a broad woman with warm tan skin and a white kitchen jacket under a man's raincoat. She had a gold tooth on the left that showed when she was pleased, and a plastic tub the size of a drum. The tooth was out when she arrived.

"Guifen! I heard you fell. I heard you broke your leg. I heard you broke both legs and a jar." She leaned on the trestle. "My jar's fine?"

Grandma lifted the cloth.

The gold tooth disappeared.

"Guifen."

"I know."

"It's half."

"Less," I said, because some part of me is still an idiot.

"My chef is cooking fish with that cabbage on Thursday for the Party secretary's niece's wedding. I told him, the old woman's black jar. Those words." Her eyes went to the toothpick cup, then the empty saucer, and I watched her put it together. Then her eyes came to me and stayed. "The whole market ate my wedding."

"There's always Gou," offered Qiu Fang, which didn't help.

Grandma laughed, not loudly. She flicked two fingers at me, which meant help her move up, and I did. She sat in front of the jars with her bad leg out to the side like a gate.

"Liu Shuzhen. Come here. Taste."

"I've tasted it. Every holiday I've tasted it."

"Not the top. The top was for the street." She took a clean chopstick and went straight down past the brine to the bottom of the jar, and came up with a fold of cabbage gone nearly gold. "The bottom's been sleeping. The bottom was always yours."

Ayi Liu tasted. She closed her eyes, same as I had at six.

"My grandson's melon-brained," Grandma said, conversationally, "but he isn't stupid. He gave the street the top. Now the whole street knows what the Golden Bridge cooks with. Tomorrow they'll come asking for it by name. Your chef should raise his prices. Tell him I said."

"You're a terrible woman."

"Full price," said Grandma. "For the bottom half. Because it's the bottom."

The gold tooth came back. Ayi Liu paid full price for less than half a jar, thanked Grandma for it, gave me a smack on the back of the head that didn't hurt and wasn't meant to, and left hugging her drum.

I sat down on Qiu Fang's stool. "Half price would've been fair."

"Fair's for people who don't see each other on Thursday." Grandma ate another bean. "No more free things in front of my good jar."

Fang Hu turned up at two. Qiu Fang's radio was giving the time signal, and he arrived on the last beep. He held a dented steel thermos in both arms, the big one from Zhao Beef Noodles with ZHAO painted on the side in red, and he stopped two meters from the stall and came no closer.

"It's not me," he said. "It's Lao Zhao's soup. I asked him myself. Big Bird can't come, there's a line out the door. I'm not in it. I'm only the legs."

Yesterday at Starlight he'd offered to carry that soup, and I'd said, You're a kid. Stay out of it. I'd said it in his father's voice. Six words and a door shut. I'd heard it come out of me and kept going anyway.

"Fang Hu. Come here."

He came one meter.

"What I said yesterday. I was wrong to say it. I'm sorry."

He looked at the thermos. He looked at Grandma, who had become very busy with the ginger. Then he grinned with the gap in his teeth, and the whole thing fell off him at once, something kids can do and I'd forgotten.

"You were really wrong," he said. "Like, a lot."

"I know."

"Okay." He set the thermos on the trestle. "Nainai, it's beef with the bone in. Lao Zhao says drink the bottom, the bottom's the good part."

"Everybody knows that," said Grandma, and looked at me.

She drank from the cup with both hands. Fang Hu took in the jars, the crowd, the toothpick cup now empty by order of the management, and his face lit with the look he got when Mei handed him a drinks tray: a job.

"GRANDMA LIN'S PICKLES!" he bellowed, loud enough that the tofu man's deaf mother turned around. "BEST IN QINGSHUI! THE OLD MAN OF QINGSHUI EATS THESE EVERY DAY!"

I put my head down and counted beans very hard.

"Who's the old man?" Grandma asked.

"A great master," Fang Hu told her, then, to the whole market: "A GREAT MASTER EATS THESE!"

A grandmother in a blue padded vest stopped. "Which master? Which temple?"

"He doesn't do temples," said Fang Hu. "He writes."

She bought two jin of radish out of respect.

By four he'd outsold my whole afternoon without knowing a single price. He pointed at a jar, shouted, and looked back at Grandma, and she held up fingers. Around half past four he stopped. He sat on the cabinet door laid flat and was quiet for most of a minute, his hand pressed to the front of his jacket over the inside pocket.

"Throat," he said when he saw me looking. He coughed to prove it, and drank the rest of the soup when Grandma pushed the cup at him.

Grandma watched him the whole minute and said nothing about it. To me she said, low, "That one's worth wages." At five he left for home, waving his whole arm over his head. Grandma, who doesn't wave at anyone, raised two fingers.

When dusk came, the market lights came on in strings, and she counted.

She did it on her stool with the cash box on her good knee, by hand. Every note flattened and turned the same way, her thumb licked for the jiao. I'd kept the total in my head all day. She counted twice anyway, lips moving, and came out where I had. Sixty-one yuan, four jiao.

"Not since before your father went south," she said. That was all.

She took out some of the tens, rolled them tight, tied them into a handkerchief, and put it in her cardigan's inside pocket. "Tomorrow. The doctor."

I'd done the other arithmetic too, because I can't not. Sixty-one forty was two yuan short of what the team still needed. I didn't say it.

She took two yuan from the box, put them in my palm and folded my fingers over them.

"Wages."

"Nainai, I don't need—"

Then I thought of Fang Hu's toffee tin, the rabbit worn off the lid, a fen for a small bottle and two for a big one since third grade. Nobody had given him that money. Keeping it was half of what earning it meant. If I handed this back, today became something I'd done for her. If I kept it, it was something we'd done.

I closed my hand. "Thank you, Nainai."

"Mm." She shut the cash box on whatever her face was doing.

Qiu Fang helped me lock the jars away. The black jar went to the back of the cabinet with almost nothing in it and the chalk still on its lid. I hooked Grandma's stool over my elbow, gave her my shoulder, and she took the broom in her other hand, upside down, and we went home.

It took a long time. She didn't hurry and I didn't make her. On the main street we passed Starlight, dark upstairs, the cardboard on its door gone soft at the corners. The two coins sat in my pocket against my leg, warm from her hand and then from mine. At the curb she stopped, put more of her weight on me than she had all day, and stepped down.
