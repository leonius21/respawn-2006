# Chapter 16: Sixty-Seven Forty

Xu Qing had brought an agenda.

It was written on the back of a returned math quiz. Hers: ninety-eight, with the two missing points circled by Teacher Gao in red ink, as if they had been caught somewhere they shouldn't be. She had propped it against a carton of Uncle Kong at the head of the back room. For the first time since I'd known that room, in either life, the fan was not aimed at the noodles. She had turned it toward her own chair.

**AGENDA**
**1. Present position.**
**2. Proposals.**
**3. Contributions.**
**4. Any other business.**

"There's four things," Big Bird said, reading it over my shoulder in a voice they could hear at the bus station. "Is that long?"

"It is as long as it needs to be," said Xu Qing.

Boss Mei appeared in the doorway at six exactly, slippers flapping, a mahjong tile in her fingers like a cigarette she'd forgotten to light. She looked at me, then at the five plastic stools around the box.

"Your free hour is for machine fourteen," she said. "Show me fourteen."

"It's a meeting."

"A meeting is people sitting in my chairs for no money." She clicked the tile against the door frame. "At seven I charge for the chairs. Him I charge double." She pointed the tile at Big Bird and went back to her table.

I'd woken up with ninety fen. The tofu man had needed his cart unloaded at six that morning, before school, and his back was worse than mine had ever been, even the first time around; he paid me two yuan. Now I had ninety fen again, and a secret in my shirt pocket, behind Xiaoyu's note. I could feel the edge of it every time I breathed.

"Roll call," Xu Qing said.

Su Wen had come up the back stairs with a library book under his arm, held at the angle of evidence. Big Bird filled two stools and part of a third. Fang Hu sat on a noodle carton with his jacket zipped to the chin over something lumpy and square. He hadn't said a word since he came in, which for Fang Hu amounted to a fire alarm. On the fifth machine, half out of the circle, sat Typhoon: gray hood up, sleeves over the thumbs, back to the wall. Out front, Monk was on shift. Through the gap in the curtain I could see him mopping the same square of floor slowly enough that it had to be on purpose.

"Rule five," said Xu Qing. "Lies to parents are reported before the meeting. Su Wen."

Su Wen held up the book. "My mother thinks I'm returning this."

"Are you?"

"At six forty. On the way home." He pushed his glasses up. "So it's only early."

She wrote. I read it upside down: *Su Wen, small, scheduled.*

"Zhao Dapeng."

"My dad thinks I'm in the kitchen," Big Bird said. "It's Friday, so the policeman's in. Dad stands at the counter and watches him eat the whole time. He can't watch two people."

Xu Qing's pen stopped. "That is not a lie. That is neglect."

"It's Friday," Big Bird said, as if that settled it.

She wrote something anyway.

---

"Item one." She opened the red envelope and laid the fund on the carton: two one-yuan notes, a five-jiao coin, the rest in jiao, flattened, faces up, all pointing the same way. "Four yuan six jiao. Required: seventy-five. Remaining: seventy yuan four jiao. Days to the tournament: twenty-three."

"Twenty-three," Su Wen agreed quietly, like a witness signing.

Big Bird squinted at the ceiling. "That's three yuan a day."

"Three point oh six," said Su Wen.

"Three. I'm rounding for everybody."

"Item two. Proposals."

Big Bird's hand was already up. It had been up since the word *agenda*.

"Coupons." He said it the way other people say *gold*. "I write FREE BOWL on nice paper. Red paper. I sell them out there for two yuan each. Everybody here eats, everybody here's hungry, it's the fastest. Forty coupons and we're done by Sunday."

"Thirty-six," said Su Wen.

"Forty, to be safe."

"Who makes the forty bowls?" Xu Qing asked.

"The kitchen."

"Who buys the beef for the kitchen?"

"The kitchen."

"Who," said Xu Qing, "is the kitchen?"

I watched it travel across Big Bird's face, slowly, like a bus coming up the main street on market day.

"...My dad."

"Rejected. And minus one for volume."

"MINUS ONE WHAT?"

"Minus two."

He looked at me for help. I looked at the agenda. There was nothing on it about what the points were, which, I suspected, was the point.

"Then what's fastest?" Big Bird said, lower, but not much. "I want whatever's fastest."

"Fastest is rob a bank," Boss Mei called from the counter without looking up. "Go now. It closes at five."

Out front somebody laughed and somebody else said *peng*, and Big Bird sat back on his stools, betrayed by a woman he had personally fed every night for two weeks.

"Other people have money," he muttered. "Su Wen's dad's a teacher. Teachers have a salary. Every month. Su Wen's rich."

Xu Qing turned. "Su Wen. Can you contribute further?"

Su Wen's finger had been tapping the edge of the carton, the slow count he kept for everything. It stopped. He didn't answer. He didn't look at anyone. He looked at the library book on his knees as if it might help, and the fan turned its face toward him and away again, and nobody in the back room said anything at all for longer than was comfortable.

I remembered seven one-yuan coins in his hand on the stairs, eight days ago. I'd never asked where seven yuan came from in a house where games were forbidden. I hadn't asked in my first life either, about anything, about anyone. It was the main reason I'd had so few people to ask.

"He's already in for three," I said. "Next."

Su Wen's eyes came up and found mine for half a second. Then the tapping started again, from one.

Xu Qing looked from him to me and wrote something short. She angled the notebook so I couldn't read it.

"Typhoon," she said. "Contribution."

From inside the hood, in the low voice from Deyang: "I'm not on the team."

"You are on my list of players."

"In pencil."

Xu Qing looked down. It was in pencil. She'd written it that way herself, because of the deadline, because she wrote *pending* next to everything she hadn't won yet. A small muscle moved in her jaw.

"When he beats me," Typhoon said, with a tilt of the hood in my direction, "I pay. Until then I'm watching. Nobody buys a ticket to watch around here."

"Brother Typhoon's got principles," Big Bird said, with deep respect.

"Then rule ten," said Xu Qing. "The hood comes down at meetings."

"Rule ten's for the team."

There was a silence in which you could hear Xu Qing understand that she had been beaten with her own paperwork. She wrote something. She pressed hard enough that I heard the nib go into the next page.

"Lin Chen. Proposal."

I took the card out of my pocket and laid it on the carton next to the fund.

It was a welfare lottery scratch card, two yuan, from the kiosk by the bus stop, where the woman sold single cigarettes from an opened pack and watched the street with the face of somebody who already knew how everything ended. Three silver panels. Match three gold ingots, win up to five hundred.

"What is that," said Xu Qing, without a question mark.

Here is what I had. In my first life, at about two in the morning on some overnight package, one of the old regulars used to tell a story about a kid who'd won five hundred yuan on a scratch card from the kiosk by the bus stop, the fall I was fifteen. Or sixteen. The third card off a fresh roll; the man always said that part twice, tapping the desk. It was a story you heard after midnight, from a man who also claimed he had once shaken hands with a deputy provincial governor and that the hand had been soft.

That morning, with two yuan from the tofu man warm in my pocket, I'd walked past the kiosk and seen the woman tearing the plastic off a new roll.

"I think the third one wins," I said.

"You think," Xu Qing said.

"I'm fairly sure."

"Whose money?"

"Mine. Tofu man."

"How much?"

"Two."

Under the table, my right foot did the arithmetic for Sunday at eight, machine 38, without asking me first. I let it.

Fang Hu leaned in from his carton, silent but unable to stay out of it. Big Bird leaned in. Even the hood turned a few degrees. Su Wen did not lean in, but his tapping got faster.

I reached for one of the fund's jiao coins to scratch with, and Xu Qing put her hand flat over the money.

"Fund money does not gamble."

I used one of my own. The first panel came away in a little curl of silver: a gold ingot. Big Bird made a sound like a pressure cooker. Second panel: gold ingot. Big Bird stood up. The stools didn't.

"Ge," whispered Fang Hu. "Ge. Ge."

Third panel. I scratched it slowly, because I was thirty-one and this was the closest I had come in two lives to understanding money, and under the silver there was a peach. A fat pink cartoon peach with a leaf, smiling. Beneath it, in small red print: *THANK YOU FOR YOUR PARTICIPATION.*

Big Bird howled. Out front, every head turned.

"Who died?" Mei shouted.

"Two yuan!" Big Bird shouted back.

"Whose?"

"GE'S!"

"Then nobody important," said Mei, and discarded a tile.

I let out a breath through my nose, a long one, from somewhere in my thirties.

"Rule eleven," said Xu Qing, writing.

"It wasn't fund money."

"It was money that could have been fund money." She turned the notebook around so I could read it. She had drawn a new column. At the top it said *Losses*. Under it: *Lin Chen, 2.00 (fined).*

"Fined by who?"

She looked at the card. "By the peach."

"The peach ruined it," Fang Hu said, with real grief, and that was nearly the end of him being quiet. Nearly. Then he looked at the agenda and closed his mouth again.

Big Bird checked the plastic clock over the door, said, "The BOWL," and left at a run. I heard him take the stairs three at a time, the whole building agreeing with each step.

---

"Item three," said Xu Qing. "Contributions."

She glanced at the gap in the curtain. "Tang Yi."

Monk did not stop mopping. "On shift."

She wrote *On shift (pending)* and moved on, which from Xu Qing was something close to tenderness.

Fang Hu unzipped his jacket.

The lump was a tin. It had held toffees once. A rabbit on the lid had been rubbed down to a ghost of a rabbit, and one corner was chipped through the paint to brown rust. He pried it open with his thumbnail, which took three tries, and tipped it out onto the carton.

Coins rolled. Su Wen stopped one with a fingertip before it went over the edge. Folded small among them, softened like cloth, were jiao notes. Fang Hu flattened them one by one with the side of his hand.

"Bottles," he said. "And New Year. Since third grade. The recycling granny gives one fen for little ones and two for the big ones, and I take them out of Starlight's trash before she has to." He pushed the whole pile across the carton with both hands. "All of it. For the form."

Su Wen was counting without moving his lips. I could see it in his eyes, going left to right. Fourteen coins. The notes.

"No kid money," I said.

It came out fast and flat, before I'd thought it, and I knew the voice. It was a man in Dongguan saying *That's seven minutes. This line costs.* It was a man ending a thing because ending it was easier than holding it.

Fang Hu's grin went first. Then his hands, which had been spread over the coins like a dealer's at a table, drew back into the sleeves of the too-big jacket until there was nothing left of them.

"I'm on the team," he said. Not loud. He wasn't talking to me. He was telling the carton.

Nobody answered him. Xu Qing's pen had stopped. The fan went on turning, toward the noodles out of old habit, then back.

What I'd meant was: keep it, you're eleven, buy something with a rabbit on it. What he'd heard was the other thing. I knew which one he'd heard, because at eleven I had heard it from a phone every Sunday.

"Three," I said.

He looked up.

"Three yuan. The rest goes back in the tin."

"Five."

"Three."

"Four and a receipt."

"Receipts," said Xu Qing, "are issued to all contributors."

Fang Hu thought about that as he thought about item builds when he was copying mine: lips pushed out, eyes on the ceiling.

"Three and a receipt," he said. "A real one."

"There is no other kind."

She counted out three yuan from the pile herself, coin by coin, and put the rest back in the toffee tin with the same care, the notes folded the way he'd folded them. Then she closed the lid on the ghost rabbit and slid it back to him. From the red envelope she took a small pad of receipt slips, the ones she'd been keeping since the stamps, and wrote with her back straight and her elbow off the table:

*Received from Fang Hu: three yuan (3.00), for the registration of Team Starlight. Treasurer: Xu Qing. September 22, 2006.*

She added the three to the fund. Under the column of numbers in her notebook she drew a line and wrote *7.60*. Under that she wrote *Remaining: 67.40*, then paused, and added *(pending)*. She did not underline it. She didn't need to.

Su Wen stood, tucked the library book under his arm and said, "I'm late for my book," and went out the back.

Xu Qing tore the receipt along its perforations, a clean, slow sound, and slid it across the carton to Fang Hu.

He read it. His mouth moved on his own name. He folded it once, then again, unzipped the jacket halfway and put it in the inside pocket on the left, and patted it flat, twice. Then he zipped the jacket up to his chin and sat on the noodle carton straighter than I had ever seen him sit on anything, the tin on his knees, as if somebody had just pinned something to him in front of the whole school.

Out on the main street, the lights were coming on.
