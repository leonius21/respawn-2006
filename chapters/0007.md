# Chapter 7: Typhoon

Saturday is the market's big day, and all of Qingshui turns out to argue about the price of things.

I came through the red school gate at 5:40 with two yuan ninety in my pocket. The main street had become one long wall of baskets, motorbikes and old women weighing tomatoes in their palms as if they could feel lies in them. Somewhere back in the market Grandma was shouting at a customer about radish. I took the long way, behind the tofu man's stall, and hoped her eyes were on her scales.

I'd skipped Starlight on Thursday and Friday. Even a boy who'd needed three days to factor x²−5x+6 could do that sum: two yuan a night, two yuan ninety in total, and one Saturday I couldn't miss. So for two evenings I drilled on paper, drawing creep waves on the backs of calendar pages. It was like learning to swim from a restaurant menu. My fingers knew they'd been cheated. They'd been twitching on the desk all day, and Xu Qing had probably written something down about it.

Starlight at six on a Saturday sounded like a train station with the roof on fire. Every machine was taken, the air was blue, and somebody in the back row was losing loudly to his own little brother.

"Fourteen," Boss Mei said without looking up from her tiles. "You skipped two days. I gave it to a fatty."

"Did he cry?"

"He paid. Same thing." She held out her palm and counted my coins by feel. "One hour. After seven you pay or you stand." "Your noodle boy's been here since five. Two packets and a ham sausage, and he talks about you like you owe him money."

"SECOND!"

Zhao Dapeng, Big Bird to everyone, was standing up in the third row with both arms in the air, red WANG FEIHU 23 jersey stretched over his chest like a sail. Half the cafe turned. The last time he'd shouted my name across a room like that, he'd been thirty-two, with a child's butterfly clip on his apron, and I'd been a man with nowhere else to go. I had to look at the floor for a second.

"My dad's at a wedding in Deyang," he announced when I reached him, as if he were reading the weather. "Back at nine. Until nine I'm free. I brought real players."

He had brought three.

First there was Streak, with the bleached stripe in his fringe and the face of a boy who thought he should be in charge. Next to him was Deng Wei from the vocational school. He was thin now, smelled of motor oil, and had no idea that in fifteen years he'd be selling insurance to his own aunties with a belly like a rice sack. The last was a pudgy boy with glasses so new that the frames still had the shop shine.

"This is Liu Bo," Big Bird said. "Third building."

The Liu boy from the third building. Grandma's cautionary tale, in the flesh: in with good marks, out with glasses. I almost asked him how his marks were doing. I shook his hand instead. It was damp.

"I play whatever doesn't die," Liu Bo said.

"Everything dies," I said.

"Then I play whatever dies last."

Streak wanted mid, so I gave him mid. That surprised him, and he spent the first five minutes waiting for the catch. I took Frost Maiden, which meant support: wards, freezes, and giving my gold to people who would forget to thank me. Somebody had to see the whole map, and it wasn't going to be Big Bird. He looked at a minimap the way a dog looks at a calculator.

The game itself was ordinary. A team from the Riverside cafe across the bridge, five boys in a room called "Qingshui 1-100 No Feeders," none of them stupid. Runes appear in the river every two minutes, and almost nobody in 2006 kept count. At 5:50 on the game clock I told Streak to go stand by the top rune spot. He asked why. I said, "Trust me, it's a feeling." At six minutes exactly a Fury rune popped up under his hero's feet: double damage for a little while. Their mid died twice before he understood what had happened.

Streak stared at me over his monitor. He didn't say thank you. Kids don't. He did start asking where the next one would spawn.

My hands were still my hands. In the twentieth minute I had a perfect freeze lined up on their carry, a clean read on which way he'd turn. My finger came down on the key a quarter-second after he'd already turned. Deng Wei died for it. He told his monitor exactly what it could do with itself, and Mei shouted "Language!" from behind the counter with a cigarette in her mouth.

But I could still call. That was the part nobody could take off me. At thirty-one minutes their whole team walked into our jungle in a tight little line. Big Bird's Tidecaller was crouched in the trees, shaking with the effort of not moving. I said "Wait." Then I said "Wait" again, because they hadn't reached the narrow part yet. Then I said "Now."

Big Bird came out of the trees roaring, in the game and in real life. Tentacles burst up through the ground, five heroes stood stunned in a ring, and three seconds later the Riverside cafe had nobody left alive on the map. We took their Ancient at thirty-four minutes.

Big Bird stood up and screamed with his arms out, a man watching his team win a World Cup. A boy two rows over threw a spicy strip at him. He caught it in his mouth. Honestly, that was the best play of the night.

"Lucky," Streak said. He looked at me in a way he hadn't at six o'clock. "Their mid was garbage. Against Typhoon you'd get eaten."

"Typhoon's not real," Big Bird said. "Typhoon's a ghost. I KNOW EVERYBODY in this town, and I don't know Typhoon."

"Typhoon plays from Riverside," Liu Bo said.

"Typhoon's a college guy from Deyang," said Deng Wei. "My cousin said."

I didn't answer. I opened the room list. At the top of the ladder for "Qingshui 1-100 No Feeders," as there had been every week for two years in my first life, sat one name: Typhoon. Online now. Thirty-one wins, three losses.

I knew where Typhoon was. I'd learned it in the spring of 2008, and so did everybody else. A photo went up on a forum, then a thread three hundred replies long, and then the jokes in this exact room, by boys at these exact machines. Typhoon was a girl. Typhoon was He Xiaoyu from class 1. For two years, the mid who had beaten all of us had been sitting in the corner by the window at Starlight, ten meters from my chair, with her hood up.

After that she didn't come back. I remember what I thought at seventeen: it was a shame about the mid.

That was all I thought. A shame about the mid.

"Back in a minute," I said.

The corner machine by the window was 38. A small figure in a gray hoodie two sizes too big sat there, hood up, headphones over the hood, knees pulled up onto the chair. Nobody looked at the corner. The light was bad and the chair wobbled, and a kid who sat alone in a corner was nobody's idea of a legend.

As I passed the counter, Boss Mei turned a tile over without looking up. "Thirty-seven's screen is green," she said. "It's past seven. Nobody pays for green. You'll live."

I hadn't told her where I was going. I put it down to Mei seeing everything in her cafe the way Grandma saw everything in the market, and I sat down at 37. The monitor was green. I lived.

The hood didn't move. On her screen a Soul Fiend was taking a whole lane apart, three razes, three dead creeps, all in the time it took me to finish sitting down. My fingers twitched, and this time it was envy.

I tapped the desk between us.

Nothing.

"Typhoon," I said.

Her hands stopped for a fraction of a second, then started again. "Wrong guy." She'd pitched her voice low and rough. It was a good effort, the kind a person makes for a long time.

"One on one," I said. "Mid. Soul Fiend mirror. First to two kills or first tower. If I lose, I go away."

Then she looked at me. She had a thin, sharp face, light skin with a warm undertone, and short hair hacked unevenly under the hood, shorter on the left, as if the scissors had got bored halfway round. Her eyes were the sharp part. They went over me from my hair to my shoes in about half a second and didn't find much they liked.

"You're the one from class 3 who sleeps."

"I rest my eyes."

"You're thirty-fourth."

"You read the class 3 list?"

"I read everything on that wall. That's how I know who to ignore." She took her headphones off, still with that fake rough voice. "Fine. Two kills. When you lose, you go back to your fat friend and you never sit at this machine again. This is my corner."

"Fair."

She set up the LAN game faster than I could find the menu.

The first kill came at three minutes. I saw it coming. That was the worst part. I knew she'd fake the long raze and throw the middle one, because a mid that good always does, the way you know a cat will eventually knock the cup off the table. My brain moved my hero out of the circle early. My hands got there late. The middle raze landed on the half-step I hadn't finished, the next one came before I'd recovered, and my Soul Fiend lay in the lane in a puddle of his own souls.

"Wow," she said flatly. "Did you plan that?"

The second kill took longer and hurt more. I baited her into my creep wave with a good lie: low health, a stumble, an opening. It was the kind of trap that worked on Streak. She took the bait, and I threw the first raze. It hit. Then my ring finger landed on the wrong key and bought a Flask. In the time I spent drinking it, she killed me.

"Two-nil," she said, already reaching for her headphones. "You click like you're paying per click. You play like you're reading the instructions off the box. I've seen my uncle play faster, and he only has eight fingers. Go home. Do your homework, thirty-four."

I flexed my hands under the desk. They were warm and stupid and fifteen. Give them a year, I thought. A year, and the drills.

"Good game," I said quietly. "He Xiaoyu."

She went completely still.

Then she grabbed my wrist under the desk, hard, her thumb pressing between the bones. Her hand was small and freezing.

"Say that name in here one more time," she said. She had dropped the fake voice. Her real one was quicker and much meaner. "One more time, and I'll break your fingers. Every one. I'll start with the mouse ones, so you can't even play badly."

"Okay."

"I mean it."

"I believe you."

She didn't let go. Her eyes went past me to the third row, where Big Bird was eating something out of Liu Bo's bag while Liu Bo looked on in mourning. "Who told you? Streak? Did Streak see me?"

"Nobody told me."

"Then how—"

"I pay attention."

"You sleep in class."

"I pay attention while I'm asleep."

That nearly got a laugh out of her. She killed it before it was born. Her grip tightened. "What do you want? Money? I don't have money. If you think you're going to make me—"

"A team," I said.

She stared at me.

"Five players," I said. "Proper ones, for the county tournament in October and then whatever comes after it. I need a mid who's faster than me." I looked down at my trapped wrist. "Which, as we've just established, is everybody."

"With who? Him?" She jerked her chin at Big Bird, who had just found a ham sausage in Liu Bo's bag and was holding it up like a trophy.

"Yes."

"He plays like a fridge falling down the stairs."

"He falls down the stairs at exactly the right moment. That's rarer than you'd think."

She let go of my wrist. The marks of her fingers stayed. For a while she didn't say anything. On her screen, my Soul Fiend's corpse had faded and the duel's results were waiting for someone to close them. Her knee bounced against the desk.

"Everybody who's ever wanted to be on a team with me wanted Typhoon," she said at last. "Not me. You understand? If they knew—" She stopped there, the way people stop at the edge of a cold river.

I understood better than she could know. I didn't say so.

"Here's my price," she said. "You beat me. Once. Fairly. No tricks with my keyboard, no rumors, no name. One on one, mid, the way we just did. You beat me with that brain you think you've got, since your hands are rubbish. Do that, and I'll listen to the rest of your stupid team speech."

"And if I never do?"

"Then you forget my name, you never sit at thirty-seven again, and you live a long life of thirty-fourth place." She put her headphones back over her hood. "Take your time. I won't lose on purpose. I don't do charity."

"I wouldn't want you to."

"Good." She pulled her knees back up onto the chair and turned to her screen. Then, without looking at me, she added in the low fake voice: "Get out of my corner, thirty-four."

I got up. My knees didn't click. I groaned anyway, out of habit, and in the corner of my eye I saw her mouth twitch.

Halfway back down the row, I caught myself grinning like an idiot at a green monitor. Beating her once didn't sound like much, unless you'd watched her play. But I had a brain, a year of Saturdays, and ninety fen left in my pocket.
