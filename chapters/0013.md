# Chapter 13: The Shoe Thrower

Teacher Gao didn't announce the midterms. He wrote them.

He came in after the lunch break with his tea jar tucked under one arm like a baby he didn't trust, set it down on the desk, picked up a stick of chalk and wrote in the top left corner of the blackboard, the corner nobody ever erased:

MIDTERM 10.12 (THU) – 10.13 (FRI)

Then he drew a box around it. Then he went over the box a second time, hard enough to make the chalk squeal, and forty-six ninth graders made the noise a bus makes going over a pothole.

"Questions," he said. It wasn't one.

A boy by the window raised his hand halfway. "Teacher Gao, golden week is in the middle..."

"Golden week is seven days without me. Use them." He opened his textbook. "Page forty."

I did the sum on the inside of my wrist with a fingernail. Twenty-four days to the midterm. Twenty-seven to Thunder Net City. On the calendar they sat three squares apart, like two men who'd been told to stay away from each other at a wedding. Somewhere in there I had to climb from thirty-fourth to twenty-fifth, which in this class meant passing nine kids who had been memorizing things since kindergarten while I had been memorizing hero respawn timers since I was nineteen.

Page forty was factoring. Of course it was.

Teacher Gao worked three examples, drank tea, and looked out over the room the way a farmer looks at a field to see which part is going to flood. His eyes stopped on me.

"Lin Chen. The pig farmer." Somebody at the back snorted. "x squared minus seven x plus twelve. From your seat."

I looked at it. I knew there were two numbers hiding in there, like two heroes in the fog, and I knew roughly where they stood. Roughly was the problem.

"Three," I said. "And... the other one."

"The other one." He let it hang there. "Sit down."

"I'm sitting."

"Sit down more."

I slid an inch lower in my chair. The class laughed, and it wasn't a mean laugh, which was somehow worse. Two rows ahead, Su Wen didn't laugh. He was writing, small and neat, and his lips were moving very slightly, in a count I was starting to recognize.

---

At the ten-minute break he turned around.

He didn't say anything at first. He looked at my open textbook, then at me, then at the window, and his lips moved without a sound: one, two, three. I'd watched him do this over a keyboard. He counts to thirty, and at thirty he taps once, harder. I waited. Twenty-six. Twenty-seven. The kids around us were throwing an eraser at the class clown. Thirty. Tap.

"Library," he said. "Last period. Activity class."

"Is that an invitation or a sentence?"

"Both." He pushed his glasses up. "I tutor you. Factoring first, then linear equations, then whatever Teacher Gao calls you up for next. And in exchange..." He lifted his English book an inch. Under it was a page of graph paper ruled into a grid, hero names down the left side, columns across the top, and in every box a number in pencil. Some had a tiny "±2" written beside them. "You fill this in."

I knew what it was before I could read it. Cooldowns, the time each skill needs before it can be used again, which the game never shows you for the enemy. You keep them in your head or you die. Su Wen had been keeping them in his head. Then, apparently, he'd been keeping them on graph paper, measured by hand in a storeroom that smelled of noodles.

"How did you get these?"

"Counting." He said it as if I'd asked how he got to school. "Some of them I counted nine times. They still don't agree."

"That's a bad trade for you. One table against twenty-four days of me."

He thought about it. "My numbers are wrong," he said. "Yours aren't. You don't even look them up. I've watched you." He put the English book back down over the grid. "That's a fair trade."

---

The library was on the second floor of the old building, a long room with a damp patch on the ceiling the shape of Taiwan, wooden tables gone gray at the edges where twenty years of elbows had rubbed them, and a rack of last month's newspapers hanging on bamboo poles like laundry nobody wanted. The librarian sat by the door knitting something orange. In the other life she'd still been at that door when I graduated, and the orange thing had still not become a sweater.

Activity class, last period on Mondays and Wednesdays, meant half the ninth grade was on the basketball court and the other half was pretending to read. We took the table at the far end, by the window, where the light was best and nobody could see what was under your textbook.

Su Wen taught the way he counted. He wrote x² − 7x + 12 at the top of a clean page and drew a little cross under it, two lines like an X.

"Two numbers," he said. "Multiply to twelve. Add to minus seven. Write every pair that makes twelve."

"I know it's three and four."

"You know it's three. You didn't know the other one."

"I was pretty sure."

"Write every pair."

I could feel it coming up from my chest, the long, leaky sigh of a man who has filled in tax forms, the sigh Xu Qing had written a rule against in meetings. Was this a meeting? I decided not to find out. I let the air out through my nose like a tire with a slow puncture, and Su Wen glanced up at me and wrote nothing down, which I appreciated.

One and twelve. Two and six. Three and four. Then the minus signs, which he made me write separately because, he said, "Minus signs leave when you're not looking." Minus three, minus four. Multiply, twelve. Add, minus seven.

"Now this one." x² − x − 6.

I wrote the pairs. I got it wrong. Plus three and minus two, which multiplies right and adds wrong. He put one finger beside the mistake without a word and waited until I saw it, and I saw it, and the shame was very small and very specific, the way it is when you're thirty-one and a fifteen-year-old has just been patient with you.

"Minus three, plus two," I said.

"Yes." He wrote the next one. "You guess first and check after. That's fine. It's fast. But you have to check."

"That's what a support says to a mid."

"I am a support," he said, and slid the graph paper across.

My half took less time and was much harder to look natural doing. I took his pencil and went down the grid. Most of his numbers were close, off by a second here, two there, the kind of error you get from a boy holding his breath in a back room and watching the mouse instead of the clock. Lyon's frog was right. So was the Hexer's finger of death. His Tidecaller ultimate was ten seconds slow.

"This one's wrong."

"I counted it nine times."

"You counted from when you saw the tentacles. The cooldown starts when he casts it. A Tidecaller on the other team casts from the trees, in the fog, and you only see it when the tentacles come up around you. You see it late every time, so you start late every time." I crossed out his number and wrote the right one. "Every timer you start from what you see is going to be late by however late you saw it."

He looked at the number for a long moment. Then he wrote, very small, in the margin: start from cast, not from sight. Then he underlined it twice.

I kept going. Near the bottom I wrote a number for Storm Sprite and stopped with the pencil still on it, because the number in my head belonged to a version of the map that wouldn't exist for another year and a half, after Lantern had changed it, and I'd learned the new one so well that I'd buried the old one. I rubbed it out and wrote it again, slower.

"You changed it."

"I was thinking of a different..." Patch, I didn't say. "Hero."

"Storm Sprite is the only Storm Sprite."

"It's been a long day."

He accepted that the way he accepted my factoring, by not writing it down.

---

The library door opened and the librarian said, "Teacher Su," in the voice people use for someone who outranks them a little, and Su Wen's pencil stopped mid-tap.

It didn't slow down. It stopped, the way a clock stops in a power cut. With his other hand, without looking, he slid the factoring page over the graph paper so that the top sheet was all minus signs and little crosses.

Teacher Su came down the room with a stack of returned books against his chest. He was thinner than I remembered, the way adults are always smaller when you meet them again from the wrong end of time: narrow shoulders, a gray cardigan buttoned all the way to the throat, gold-rimmed glasses, pale skin with a pink undertone around the nose, hair combed flat with water so you could see the teeth marks of the comb. He was the kind of man whose pens all pointed the same way in his pocket.

He saw his son first, and his face did a small, satisfied thing. Then he saw me.

I watched the memory arrive. It took about two seconds, which, if you count it from the cast, was quick.

"Lin," he said. "Lin Chen. Fourth grade."

"Teacher Su."

"A left sneaker. Blue canvas." He tilted his head. "Size thirty-four."

Of course it was thirty-four. The number was following me around this town like a stray dog I'd fed once.

"I still have it," Teacher Su went on. "In our shoe cabinet. Your grandmother never came for it."

I remembered that now. I remembered limping home in one shoe and one sock along the school wall, and Grandma at the door, looking at my foot and saying that a family that throws its shoes on teachers' balconies can learn to walk on one, and the sneaker never being mentioned again in that house, in this life or the last. I bit the inside of my cheek hard enough to taste it.

"I'm sorry about the shoe, Teacher Su."

"You were nine." He set the books down on the end of our table and looked at the page under his son's hand. The crosses. The minus signs. My two wrong answers, crossed out in Su Wen's neat pencil, and the right ones beside them. The line between his eyebrows let go. "Factoring."

"Teacher Gao announced the midterm," Su Wen said. His voice was perfectly even. His finger was not tapping anything at all. "Lin Chen is having difficulty."

"I am," I said, which was the easiest true thing I'd said in two weeks.

Teacher Su nodded slowly, the way men nod when a thing in front of them is turning into a proverb. "Good," he said. "Good. The one who has helps the one who lacks. When I was at normal college we had study groups. Half the class passed because of the other half." He looked at his son with an expression I'd never seen on him in either life, not at a balcony door and not across a classroom. He was proud. Not of the grades, which he got delivered every term like the electricity bill. Of this. "Your mother will be pleased."

Su Wen said nothing. I could practically hear him counting.

"Wednesdays," Teacher Su decided. "Activity class. This table. I'll tell Teacher Gao it's a study group, so nobody bothers you." He picked his books back up. "Until the midterm, and then we'll see what the rankings say. If they say something good, it continues."

"Yes, Ba."

"Thank you, Teacher Su."

He looked at me once more over the books, and I saw him decide, kindly and completely, that the shoe boy had grown up into a struggling student with the good sense to sit next to his son. Then he went to the shelves.

I kept my face exactly as it was. It is very difficult to keep a straight face when you're fifteen and the adult in front of you is building a monument to your virtue out of a cover story. It's harder when you're thirty-one and you know what the cover is covering.

---

Xu Qing had been in the history section the whole time. I only knew because, as Teacher Su left, she came around the end of the shelf with a book on the Qin dynasty under one arm and her green notebook open on top of it, pen already moving.

She stopped beside our table. Her ponytail did not move at all.

"Wednesday," she said, writing. "Activity class. Library. Study group, by order of a parent." She looked at Su Wen. "Rule five."

"It isn't a lie," Su Wen said.

"Then you won't mind it being written down."

"I don't mind."

"Good." She wrote one more word, which I couldn't read upside down, and then she looked at my page. At the crossed-out wrong answers. At the right ones. Her mouth made a very small shape that might have been approval and might have been a muscle cramp. "Twenty-five," she said, and walked off to have her book stamped.

"What did she write?" I asked.

"I don't know." Su Wen finally lifted the factoring page and checked that the graph paper was still there, the way you check your pocket after a crowded bus. "Probably 'pending.'"

---

The bell went at five-forty and the whole school poured down the stairs and out of the red gate into the street in blue and white. Su Wen walked beside me as far as the corner where the teachers' housing started. He had his English book under one arm and the graph paper folded twice inside it.

At the corner he stopped and looked back at the school, at the second-floor windows of the old building, and then he leaned in an inch and said, very quietly, as if somebody in the crowd might be keeping a notebook:

"He believes it, because it is partly true."

And I laughed. It came out of me before I could stop it, loud and cracked, an actual fifteen-year-old's laugh, and two eighth-grade girls turned around to look at the boy laughing at nothing on the corner. Su Wen pushed his glasses up and waited for me to finish, counting, I was fairly sure. I laughed for most of a beat. He didn't tap at the end.
