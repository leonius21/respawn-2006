# Chapter 23: Corridors

Grandma packed the bag twice the night before. Once while I watched. Once after she thought I was asleep, when I heard the cloth rustle and something heavy go in.

In the morning it weighed what a thermos, a towel, her reading glasses and the household register should weigh, plus about two jin.

"What's the jar?"

"Chili cabbage." She was tying her shoes with her left leg out straight in front of her, stiff as a plank of the stall cabinet. "For the doctor."

"We're paying the doctor."

"The money is for the hospital. The jar is for her." She held the broom out, bristles up, so I could pull her to her feet with it. "You don't walk into a person's room with your hands empty. Who raised you?"

"You did."

"Then carry the bag."

The county hospital was across the bridge, past the county offices, at the top of the one road in Qingshui that bothered to be a hill. A man on a motor-trike at the corner wanted three yuan. Grandma said two, and kept saying it in different tones of voice until it was true. I paid him with the two coins she'd given me the evening before. She watched them go into his hand and didn't stop me.

Qingshui County People's Hospital was three buildings of white tile, streaked gray where twenty years of rain had run down them, with a red cross on the roof and one arm of it rusted brown. At twenty to eight the forecourt was already full of people holding plastic bags of X-rays and children.

I had never been inside. Not as a kid. Grandma treated colds with ginger and fevers with a wet towel and an opinion. And not later, when it would have mattered. If she came here at the end of my first life, and I assume she did, because people do, I was in a hotel in some other city, warming up my hands.

So there was no reason my feet should know the place.

They knew it anyway. In the registration hall Grandma started for the first window, which had the longest line. I steered her by the elbow to the second.

"General outpatient's here. The first one's for people with insurance cards."

The glass at the second window had a crack running corner to corner, taped over with brown tape gone soft at the edges. I knew the tape would be there a half second before I saw it.

Grandma squinted up at me. "How do you know?"

"Qiu Fang told me."

"Qiu Fang sells eggs."

"She's been sick."

"Everybody's been sick," Grandma said. That was the end of it. She leaned on the counter to tell the registration woman what was wrong with her.

"A cat."

The woman's pen stopped. "Orthopedics. Or—for the cat?"

"For me. The cat's fine."

The bone doctor was on the second floor of the main building. The stairs were at the back, and on the landing between floors there was a tall window, and as we came up the first flight I already knew that if I looked out of it I'd see the river bending under the bridge, brown with rain, and the row of plane trees along the far bank with the gap where one had come down.

I looked. There was the river. There was the gap.

Every county hospital in Sichuan had been poured from the same drawing by the same exhausted man around 1975, I told myself. I'd been in plenty of hospitals. Team doctors in Chengdu. A wrist clinic in Shanghai. It was a staircase. Staircases look alike.

Grandma took it one step at a time, the broom handle thumping on each stair, her weight on my shoulder.

"You're slow," she said, when we were both breathing hard at the top.

"I'm carrying the bag."

"The bag weighs nothing."

"The bag has a jar in it."

The doctor was a woman of maybe fifty named Tian, according to the card on her door, with gray at her temples pinned back hard and a white coat with a brown tea ring on the pocket. Her eyes had the pink rims of someone who had been in that room since yesterday. She said "Sit," and "Leg," and put her hands on Grandma's knee.

Grandma hissed. "Your hands are colder than my cabinet."

"Everybody says. Bend it. Stop. Straight. How did it happen?"

"A cat ran under me."

"At the market?"

"There are cats at the market."

Doctor Tian pressed two fingers into the side of the knee, where the swelling was, and looked up at me rather than at Grandma. "Was the cat carrying a crate?"

"Chili cabbage," I said. "About twenty jin."

Grandma turned her head and gave me a look that would have kept pickles for a year.

"X-ray," said the doctor, writing. "Old building, ground floor. Bring me the film."

"No."

The pen didn't stop. "No?"

"They take a picture to take your money," Grandma said. "My knee's my knee. I know what's in there. Bone."

Doctor Tian put the slip on the desk between them, and when Grandma didn't pick it up, she reached for the next file on her pile. "Then come back when it's worse. It will be."

I picked the slip up. Grandma was looking at the window with her jaw set, and I knew that face. Some of it was stubbornness. Most of it was arithmetic. She'd already priced the picture and decided the leg wasn't worth it.

I crouched by her chair so I was lower than her.

"Nainai. Do it for me. Not for the leg."

"And what do I get?"

I'd been ready for no. I hadn't been ready for a market. I dug in my pocket and brought out the stall key on its faded red string. I'd kept it since yesterday morning. I'd had a plan for tomorrow, five-thirty, the jars.

"You get this back. You say when the stall opens. Not me."

She looked at the key for a while.

"You were going to open tomorrow."

"Yes."

"I know. Last night I didn't hear it go on the nail." She took it, closed her fist around the string, and put the fist in her cardigan pocket. "Fine. One picture."

Doctor Tian, who had apparently been listening while reading another woman's file, said, "Through the back courtyard is faster," without looking up.

I already knew that. That was the trouble.

Downstairs my feet went right instead of left. I was halfway down a corridor under a sign that said INTERNAL MEDICINE before I noticed. It smelled different from the rest of the building, something sweet and chemical under the disinfectant, and at the far end a smaller sign had an arrow on it I didn't stop to read. I turned around. Grandma was still on the bottom step, sorting out the broom, and hadn't seen.

The shortcut was where I knew it would be: out a side door, across a courtyard of cracked concrete with a boiler house puffing steam, between lines of white sheets hung to dry, and in through the back of the old building. I didn't decide to take it. I just did.

The X-ray took four minutes. Grandma told the technician, while lying on his cold table, that his machine was a way of taking money out of old women. He said he'd heard that. She said then he should listen.

Coming back, the film in a big brown envelope under her arm because she wouldn't let me hold it, we reached the courtyard door, and I turned toward the east building again. Toward that corridor, from the other end. I'd gone four steps before I caught it.

When I looked back, Grandma had stopped.

"Bathroom," I said. "Qiu Fang said it was over here."

She looked at me for a long time across the wet concrete, with the sheets moving behind her. She didn't say anything. Then she turned toward the stairs, and I followed her.

Doctor Tian clipped the film to a light box and pointed with the cap of her pen.

"The cushion in the joint. See how it's thin here? Worn. That's age, and sixty years on your feet. This band, the ligament, is strained. Lifting made it worse." She tapped the film twice. "No operation. You don't need one, and you wouldn't let me."

"No," Grandma agreed.

"A brace. A cane. Six weeks, no crates, nothing heavy, and no standing all day. You can sit at your stall. You can't be your stall." She wrote. "Pills for the pain and the swelling. A medicated patch at night. Come back in a month." She looked at the household register, then at me. "Who lives with you?"

"Him."

"His parents?"

"Guangdong."

"Guangdong has telephones." The doctor capped her pen. "Somebody should tell them."

Grandma reached into the bag and set the jar on the desk.

Doctor Tian looked at it. "I can't take that."

"It's not for you. It's for your hands." Grandma pushed it a centimeter closer. "Eat something hot with it."

Something happened at the corner of the doctor's mouth that she didn't let finish. She put the jar in her bottom drawer, beside a thermos and what looked like somebody else's jar, and said, "Next."

At the cashier's window the total was a hundred and twenty-six yuan. Grandma untied the handkerchief at the counter and counted with a licked thumb, twice, and tied it again. It was a lot thinner tied. I did the math without wanting to: almost exactly two of our shortfalls, minus eighty fen. I hated that my head did that.

We sat on a bench in the back courtyard while the pharmacy called numbers. The new cane, aluminum, with a gray rubber foot, leaned between us. The brace was already on under her pants and she kept touching it, as if checking it hadn't left.

"Your mother will cry," Grandma said.

"Then I'll call tonight."

She watched the sheets. A nurse came out and unpegged two, folded them against her chest, and went back in.

She didn't tell me not to.

Xu Qing's ruling had said silence until the doctor. The doctor had spoken at about a quarter to ten, at the light box, with the cap of her pen. I thought Xu Qing would want the time written down.

At home the broom went back behind the door, bristles down, a broom again.

Grandma made dinner sitting on her stool, with the cutting board across her knees and the cane hooked over the back of the chair. When I came out of my room the Novia was on the table next to the salt. I hadn't seen her put it there.

The dormitory number was written on the rice-shop calendar by the door, in her pencil, under the word DONGGUAN. I had never dialed it. As far as I knew, nobody had ever called it from this end.

I took the phone out onto the landing, because it hears better outside. She'd always said that on Sundays. I'd always thought she meant the signal.

It rang seven times.

"Wei? Building Three." A man, Mandarin with a Hunan edge, a television going somewhere behind him.

"Is Lin Jun there? This is his son."

"His son!" The voice changed shape. "The top-twenty-five son? From Sichuan?"

I didn't say anything. I couldn't, for a second.

"He told the whole floor. I'm Ou, the foreman. Lao Ou. Your father's in the shower, he just came off shift." A pause. "This is long distance, kid. You're paying for the wait. Is it bad?"

"It's my grandmother."

"Ah." The television got quieter, as if he'd turned his body away from it. "Then I'll bang loud. Stay on. Xiao Guo! Run to Building Four, get Zhou Hong, tell her the phone. Run."

I heard him walk off. I heard a door take three hard knocks and somebody shout something wet and annoyed back. I watched the seconds climb on the little gray screen. On Sundays that was Grandma's job.

Then breathing, close, and water still in it.

"Who died?"

"Nobody, Ba. Nobody died."

My father let out a sound that wasn't a word.

I told it plain, the way I'd practiced on the stairs coming up from the market. Sunday. A crate of chili cabbage. Her left knee went. I carried her home. The bone doctor today. Worn cartilage, a strained ligament, worse from lifting. No operation. A brace and a cane, six weeks no lifting, no standing all day, medicine, back in a month. A hundred and twenty-six yuan.

"Who paid?"

"She did. From the stall."

"Sunday," he said. "We talked Sunday."

"She asked me to wait for the doctor. The doctor talked this morning."

He didn't answer that. Behind him a door banged, feet came fast, and my mother's voice said, "Give it, give it to me—" and then was in my ear without any hello at all.

"Chen-chen? What happened, what—is she—"

I told it again, shorter. Halfway through, she started crying, not loudly, the kind you can hear someone trying to fit around the words.

Then it turned.

"She lifted it herself? At her age? I told her at New Year. I told her twice, I said, get the Liu boy to carry, pay him a few jiao, and she said—she said I talk like a city woman. That stubborn old—" My mother caught herself. "Six weeks. Who'll sell? She'll sell. She'll sit there with the cane and lift with her other hand. I know her."

"I know."

"I'm coming home. Jun. I'm going back."

"With what?" My father, farther from the phone. They were sharing the receiver. I could picture it, the two of them bent toward one cord in a hallway that smelled of soap. "With what money? The fare there, the fare back. And then what job? They'll give your machine to the next girl before your bus is out of Dongguan."

"Then you tell me what. We just stay here and—"

"Ma," I said. "Don't come."

They both went quiet.

"And don't send money. She'll put it under the rice bag and pretend it isn't there." The screen said seven minutes and something. "Call her. Tomorrow. Tell her you're angry. Your own words. Not 'take care of yourself.' Angry."

"You want me to scold your grandmother?" My mother sounded scandalized and wet at the same time.

"She hears it better from you than from me."

For a while there was nothing on the line but the television in the background and somebody far away laughing at it.

"Tomorrow at lunch," my father said. "I'll use Lao Ou's phone."

"Okay."

"Chen-chen." My mother again, closer, her nose stuffed. "Did you eat?"

"Not yet."

"Eat."

The receiver shifted hands. My father cleared his throat, and when he spoke his voice wasn't steady. "You did right to call," he said. "Don't skip school."

"I won't."

The line clicked. The screen held the number for a second, nine minutes and some seconds I didn't read, and went dark.

When I turned around, Grandma was in the doorway, leaning on the frame instead of the cane, a bowl of rice in her hand with pickled radish on top. I didn't know how long she'd been there. Long enough. The Novia had never been a quiet phone.

She put the bowl into my hands. It was hot all the way through.

"Your father's voice shook," she said. "Eat."

She wasn't angry. She went back in and left the door open behind her, and I sat down on the top step with the bowl and ate, while the light from the kitchen lay across the landing and the stall key hung on its nail by the door.
