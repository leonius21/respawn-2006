# Chapter 1: Sunrise, Wrong Century

The first thing I knew was the cold. The second thing I knew was that it was in the wrong place.

The cold was the last thing I'd had. A rented room in Qingshui in December, the electric heater unplugged to save money, a blanket that didn't reach my feet, and a tiredness so deep it felt like the mattress was slowly eating me. I remembered thinking I'd get up in a minute and make tea. Then nothing.

But the air on my face now was thick and wet and warm. It smelled of brine and chili and somebody frying garlic at an hour when no decent person fries garlic. A rooster was going off outside like a car alarm. A motorbike coughed past, then another. And I was still cold, deep in my bones and my fingers, the way you're cold after staying in the river too long, while sweat soaked through my T-shirt at the same time.

I opened my eyes. Yellow plaster ceiling. A water stain shaped like a sleeping dog. I had named that dog when I was seven and hadn't seen him in fourteen years.

I sat up too fast. My hand went to the small of my back on its own, the way it had every morning for five years, and I braced for the hot wire of pain and groaned in advance. It was a long, wounded, government-pensioner groan, the kind you give a flight of stairs.

Nothing hurt.

"What are you moaning about?" A voice came from the next room, rough as a cheese grater, in Sichuan dialect so thick you could stand a chopstick in it. "You're fifteen, not ninety. Get up. The porridge is going cold and I'm not heating it twice."

I didn't get up. I sat on the bed with my hand on my perfectly healthy back and listened to a dead woman complain about porridge.

---

You'd think the date would be the easy part.

There was a tear-off calendar on a nail by the door, the free kind the county pharmacy handed out at New Year: a fat block of thin pages, each with one huge red number and a lot of small print about which days were lucky for weddings. The top of the block said 2006, Year of the Dog. The page said Friday, September 1.

That meant nothing. Grandma tore pages when she remembered, which was usually around the tenth, when she'd rip off a week and a half in one go and complain that time was a thief. For a second I had the stupid feeling I'd stood here before, reading this exact page more than once. Well, sure. I'd lived this year. I'd stood in front of this calendar probably a hundred times.

The television sat in the corner of the front room: a fat gray CRT with a lace doily on top and a plastic vase of plastic peonies on the doily, because nothing in Grandma's house was allowed to be naked. She had it on with the sound low. The provincial morning show was running the weather. A woman in a red jacket waved at the Sichuan basin like she was shooing chickens off it. Along the bottom of the screen, in white characters: Saturday, September 2.

Beside the vase, plugged into a charger with its cord wrapped in tape, was a Novia flip phone. It was silver gone gray at the edges, and the hinge had cracked and been glued back. My parents had bought it for her so they could call from Dongguan on Sundays. She treated it like a live grenade. I flipped it open. The little screen lit up blue.

07:14. 02/09/2006.

Three witnesses. I stood there in my sweaty T-shirt, holding my grandmother's phone, and did the math, because my brain does the math whether I want it to or not. I was fifteen. It was 2006. AotA was a custom map on version 6-point-something that maybe a few hundred thousand people in China had heard of. And Grandma, Lin Guifen, sixty-eight, was alive. She would stay alive until January 2014.

Seven years and four months. I'd spent the last of those months in a hotel in Shanghai, and I'd missed the funeral for a group stage we lost anyway.

"Holy shit," I said, very quietly, to the phone.

"Chen-wa." She was in the kitchen doorway. "What are you doing with my phone? It's not Sunday. Your mother doesn't call until Sunday."

I looked at her. I'd forgotten how small she was. In my head she'd grown, the way the dead do, into something like a mountain. The real one came up to about my chin. Her gray hair was twisted into a bun and stabbed through with a black clip. She wore a blue apron over a flowered shirt, sleeves shoved up past the elbow. Her forearms were brown and corded from forty years of hauling clay pickle jars. Her face was creased deep around the mouth from shouting prices across the market. Her eyes were small and black and missed nothing, which was the worst thing about her when I was fifteen and the best thing about her ever since.

She was holding a ladle. She pointed it at me.

"Look at you. You look like a rat somebody fished out of a latrine."

"Thanks, Nainai."

"Come here." She didn't wait. She crossed the room in four quick steps, and I saw it, the hitch in the left leg she always smoothed over by moving fast. She slapped her palm on my forehead, then on her own, then back on mine. "Aiya. You're soaking. You're shaking. You've got a fever."

"I don't have a fever."

"You're shaking."

I was. I couldn't stop. I'd been cold for a very long time, and apparently the body needed a few minutes to get the message.

"Sit," she said. "Sit, sit. You melon-brained wa, sleeping without a sheet, the fan blowing on your head all night, this is how it starts. Your father had a fever at ten and he's been stupid ever since."

"I think Dad was always—"

"Sit!"

I sat at the folding table. She went into the kitchen, and I heard the lid of a pot, then water bubbling. Along the front-room wall stood her pickle jars. They were big brown clay things, a dozen of them, each with a water-filled rim around its lid to keep the air out. One of them gave a slow wet burp as the brine settled. I had forgotten that sound completely. It was the sound of every morning of my childhood. For eight years after she died, I hadn't heard it once.

She came back with two eggs in a bowl of hot water and a big bowl of rice porridge with a heap of diced pickled radish on top, pale yellow and shining.

"Two eggs," she announced, like a doctor giving a diagnosis. "Eat both. You sweat it out."

"Nainai, it's not that kind of—"

She cracked the first egg on the edge of the table, peeled it in one long strip with her thumbnail, and put it in my hand. "Eat."

I ate the egg.

She sat down across from me with her own bowl. It was plain porridge. No egg. I noticed that, because the old me, the thirty-one-year-old me who'd spent his last years counting coins for instant noodles, knew exactly what it meant. The fifteen-year-old me had never noticed it once.

She glanced at the window. Gray sky over the market roofs, the kind of Sichuan sky that looks like a wet towel. "Is it going to rain? If it rains I need the plastic sheet for the stall. Last week the cabbage woman's whole table washed into the gutter. Her face. You should have seen her face."

"Just check it on your phone, Nainai," I said, around the egg.

She looked at me. She looked at the Novia on top of the television. She looked at me again.

"Check it how?"

Shit.

"Call," I said. "Call the weather line. Twelve-one-two-one. They tell you the weather."

"That costs money."

"It's like two jiao."

"Two jiao to hear a girl tell me what I can see through the window." She kept looking at me with those small black eyes, the way she looked at a cabbage somebody was trying to sell her at the wrong price. "When did you learn about the weather line?"

"School."

"Hm," said Grandma, who had never once in her life believed the word "school" coming out of my mouth.

I picked up the spoon and took a mouthful of porridge with radish. That was where it went wrong.

I'd tried to find this taste for years. In Chengdu, at little family places with plastic stools. In Shanghai, at expensive "authentic Sichuan" restaurants where they served pickles on slate with a sprig of something on top. In a Korean barbecue place in Busan, once, drunk, ordering every side dish on the menu. None of them came close. I could never explain it, so I stopped trying. Whatever she did with the brine and the peppercorns and the old jar had died with her, and nobody had written it down, because who writes down pickles?

It was sour and salty and faintly sweet, with a little bite at the end that crept up the back of your nose. It was exactly right.

I put my head down over the bowl and the tears just came. I didn't decide to cry. My face did it without asking me, like my hand going to my back. They dripped off my nose into the porridge, and I kept eating, because if I stopped eating I would have to look up.

"It's the chili," I said. "Too much chili."

"There's no chili in that radish."

"There's—a lot of chili."

"I've made that radish since before your father was born. I know what's in my radish." She didn't move. She didn't ask anything else. After a while she reached across and peeled the second egg, slowly, and set it on the edge of my bowl.

"Bad dream?" she said.

"Yeah."

"Dreams are dreams." She tapped the table next to the egg with one finger. "Eat."

I ate the second egg. She finished her plain porridge and watched me the whole time. Behind her the provincial weather lady promised scattered showers over the basin, and one of the jars gave another slow, satisfied burp.

---

She left for the market at eight. Saturday was the big day. People came in from the villages, and if you weren't set up by half past, the good spot by the tofu man went to the woman from Shuangqiao, and then Grandma would spend the whole week saying the woman from Shuangqiao's pickles tasted of feet.

I tried to carry her jars down the stairs. She slapped my hand away. "With a fever? Are you trying to die?"

I didn't have an answer for that one.

At the door she stopped, tied her apron tighter, and dug in the front pocket. Out came a handkerchief, folded into a square. She unfolded it on her palm. Inside was a ten-yuan note, folded small and gone soft as cloth from being folded and unfolded.

She pressed it into my hand and closed my fingers over it with both of hers. Her hands were dry and rough and surprisingly strong.

"Buy yourself something. Lunch. A notebook. Medicine, if you're still hot at noon." Then she let go and pointed at my face, the way she pointed at people who tried to haggle. "And you don't go near that net cafe on the main street. Above the hardware shop. That smoky place with the woman and her mahjong. The Liu boy from the third building went up those stairs with good marks and came back down with glasses and nothing in his head. The television says those places are electronic heroin. You hear me?"

"I hear you."

"Say it."

"I won't go near the net cafe, Nainai."

She narrowed her eyes at me for three long seconds, decided that was the best she'd get out of a melon-brained wa, and went down the stairs one at a time, quick on the right leg, careful on the left, a jar of radish on her hip.

I stood in the doorway with the note in my fist.

Ten yuan. Saturday, September 2, 2006. That net cafe on the main street.

I knew this story. I'd told it a hundred times. In 2011, to a journalist from an esports site who thought it was adorable. In 2013, at a press table in Shanghai, between two sponsors' logos. "The first time I went to Starlight," I used to say, with the little smile the PR girl had taught me, "my grandmother gave me ten yuan and told me not to." Everybody laughed. It was a good line. Somewhere along the way I'd sanded it down so smooth that I forgot it had happened to a real woman with a bad knee, who ate plain porridge so I could have two eggs.

Last time, I'd gone up those stairs because she told me not to. I spent all ten yuan, five hours, sat in the smoke until it was dark, and walked home with nothing for lunch money for the week. Then I lied to her about it, badly, every day, for two years.

I knew what was up those stairs now. Forty old machines and a broken air conditioner. The game that was going to eat my life. Kids I'd play with and kids I'd beat and kids I'd forget, and maybe a few I could actually do something for, this time, if I didn't screw it up.

I folded the note one more time and put it in my pocket. Four yuan, I decided. Two hours. The rest went back into her handkerchief tonight, along with a bag of the good peppercorns from the stall by the bridge, and whatever lie I told her would at least be a nice one.

Then I put on my shoes, without groaning, and went down to the main street on purpose.

Somewhere around the noodle shop, I noticed I wasn't cold anymore.
