# Chapter 10: The Monitor's Notebook

The back room at Starlight had five machines, one fan, and a bare bulb, and the fan was aimed at the Uncle Kong boxes, as if the noodles were the ones in danger of overheating.

Twenty-two minutes into the game, I had stopped noticing the heat. We were three kills down against a five-stack from Deyang whose names all started with DY_4th, and they had the swagger of boys whose school had a real computer lab. Xiaoyu was hunched in the corner seat, hood up, knees on the chair, playing a mid hero she'd picked only to annoy me. Su Wen sat beside me with his glasses pushed up his nose, counting under his breath. Big Bird filled the third chair.

The fifth machine belonged, unofficially and against Boss Mei's daily screamed wishes, to Fang Hu. He was playing Sand Lord, and for eighteen minutes his hero had never been more than a screen away from mine. When I walked, he walked. When I stopped, he stopped. When I went to the shop, he went to the shop and bought the same thing. That was a problem. I was the carry and he was not.

"Hu Zi," I said. "You can't build my items."

"I'm learning by copying."

"You're learning to be poor twice."

"Officer Fang came yesterday," Big Bird said, at full volume. He had been waiting all game to say it. "Friday. Two bowls. He didn't say anything about anything. He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, 'More vinegar.'"

"And?"

"I gave him so much vinegar he coughed. He didn't tell Dad. Dad doesn't know." He beamed at the screen. "I'm free until seven-thirty if I bring back Boss Mei's bowl."

"Their Earthbreaker wasted his big stun on Bird at twenty," Su Wen said, tapping the desk. "It's down for another ninety seconds. Eighty-nine. Eighty-eight."

My hands were slower than my head, as usual, but I knew what five boys from Deyang would do when they saw our Tidecaller standing alone by their jungle, picking his nose in hero form. They would come and kill him. Boys always came.

"Bird. Don't move."

"I'm not moving."

"You're moving a little."

"I'm breathing."

They came out of the trees in a neat line. Su Wen slowed two of them. I walked my hero forward one step, the bait step, and said, "Now—"

The door opened.

"She asked," said Monk, from somewhere behind her. "Then she stopped asking."

Xu Qing stood in the doorway of a net cafe's illegal back room in a school tracksuit zipped to the exact top, with her green notebook open and her pen already uncapped. Her ponytail did not move. The rest of her didn't either. She had a narrow face, pale with a pink undertone that was turning darker across the cheekbones, and the look of someone who had walked up a dark staircase rehearsing a speech.

Behind her, out at the counter, I heard Boss Mei ask, "Inspector?"

"Class monitor," Xu Qing said, without turning round.

"Worse," said Mei, and dealt a tile.

Every head in the room froze. Not every hand. Big Bird hit his ultimate. Tentacles exploded up through the river, four of the Deyang boys hung in the air, and nobody on our team did anything about it, because Su Wen had gone white and Xiaoyu had pulled her hood down to her nose and I was looking at the notebook.

"Six twenty-eight," Xu Qing said, writing. "Starlight Net Cafe. Rear storeroom. Lin Chen, class three. Su Wen, class three." The pen paused at Su Wen's name as if it hurt the pen. "One unidentified person, hood. One primary pupil. One." She looked at Big Bird. "Large."

"Zhao Dapeng," said Big Bird helpfully. "Zhao Beef Noodles. Two doors down."

"I followed you from the school gate," she told me. "You walk slowly. You stop to look at shops you've seen every day of your life. Then I stood on your stairs for forty minutes, deciding." She closed the notebook on her finger. "On Monday I will report this to Teacher Gao. Su Wen, I will tell your father tonight. He's at home. I checked."

Su Wen made a very small sound, the kind a kettle makes before it decides to boil.

I stood up. She watched me do it, and I saw her register what was missing: no wheeze, no hand on the lower back. She'd been listening for it.

There were a lot of things I could have said. Please was one. Don't tell was another. In the first life, at fifteen, I would have said something smart and she would have reported us out of spite. At thirty-one, I'd sat across tables from sponsors, team owners and one tax official in Wuhan, and I knew the only thing that worked on people who'd come in angry with a plan.

You let them be the most serious person in the room.

"Can you give me ten minutes?" I said. "Then you decide. Whatever you decide, I won't argue."

She hadn't expected that. The pen hovered.

"Ten," she said. "From now."

The only seat that wasn't a computer chair was the sofa, which had a spring in the middle that went for the soft parts. I folded my jacket over the spring and gestured. She sat on the very edge, upright, like a judge who suspected the bench.

"Su Wen, the form."

He had it in his English book, two copies, folded in quarters, because Su Wen was the only one of us who'd gone to Thunder Net City on West Street and asked politely. I spread one on her knee.

"Qingshui First Net Cafe AotA Tournament," I said. "Sunday, October fifteenth. Teams of five. First prize six hundred yuan and a hundred free hours." I watched her eyes do the math on six hundred, which was more than half of what some fathers on this street made in a month. "We practice six to seven. Nobody past nine. Su Wen's English Sundays don't move. We're not skipping school, we're not stealing, nobody's borrowing money from anybody who'll break their legs." I tapped the bottom line. "Entry is fifty yuan. Five of us, ten each. Right now we've got about nine yuan between us, so we need to raise forty-one, and we'll do it by working, and—"

"It isn't fifty."

I stopped.

She'd turned the sheet over. On the back, in print so faint it looked ashamed of itself, was an asterisk. She read it aloud in her assembly voice. "'Each registered player also pays a deposit of five yuan, refundable after the team's final match.' Five players, twenty-five. Seventy-five yuan. If you register a substitute, eighty." She looked up. "Also you've made a subtraction error. You don't have nine. You said 'about.' 'About' isn't a number."

Su Wen, very quietly: "I read the front."

Two days I'd been building this speech in my head, the reasonable budget, the honest kids, and a fifteen-year-old with a pen had just knocked twenty-five yuan out of it. Out by the counter, somebody shouted peng.

"You're right," I said. "Seventy-five. Thank you. Can you write that down? I'll get it wrong again."

Her pen paused for a full second. Then she wrote it down. It seemed to annoy her that she wanted to.

"And grades," she said. "You promised grades. You're thirty-fourth."

"I'll be higher at midterms."

"How much higher."

Treat her like an adult. Adults want numbers. "Top twenty-five."

She wrote that down too, and underlined it, and I understood that I'd just signed something.

"Then there's the guardian signature," she said, tapping the form. "Under eighteen. Each player."

"Ponytail," said Big Bird, who had at some point turned back to his monitor and was now pushing the bottom lane alone against five people. "You've got very nice writing. Can you do my dad? He signs like a fish skeleton. Three lines and a lump."

The temperature in the room changed.

"That," said Xu Qing, standing up off the spring, "is forgery. That is the falsification of a document. I am the class monitor. I will not write anybody's father."

"You could do mine," Fang Hu offered. "My dad's is all straight lines."

"Your father," said Xu Qing, "is a police officer. Everybody knows your father is a police officer. You're wearing his jacket."

Fang Hu looked down at the rolled sleeves. Then he leaned over the form and counted the boxes with one finger. Five lines for players, one for a substitute.

"Where's mine?"

"You're eleven," said Xu Qing.

"I'll be twelve in November."

"The form," she said, "does not care about November."

He kept looking at the empty substitute line anyway, until I turned the form face down.

"Nobody's forging anything," I said. "Bird, nobody."

"I was being polite."

"Be polite with your mouth shut."

Xu Qing sat down again, on the jacket this time, and did something I had never once done in two lives with a tournament form. She read the whole thing. Front and back, the numbered rules, the small print about late arrivals and machine faults and "behavior detrimental to the reputation of the host establishment." Her lips moved slightly. Su Wen leaned over her shoulder and they read together.

"Nobody in this room," she said, without looking up, "has read past rule three."

"Rule three's where it gets boring," said Xiaoyu, in her low Typhoon voice, from under the hood.

Xu Qing turned and looked at the hood for a long time. The hood looked back.

"I'm from Deyang," the hood said.

"Hm," said Xu Qing, and wrote something.

Then she put her finger on rule seven and read it out. "'Teams representing a licensed internet cafe may submit the signature of that cafe's licensed operator as sponsor, together with the business license number, in place of individual guardian signatures.'" She looked at me. "You have a net cafe. You're sitting in it."

I had been worrying about that signature since Thursday. I'd made lists of possible adults: the tofu man, Old Tan, a cousin in Deyang I wasn't sure existed. And the answer had been printed on the paper the whole time, behind an asterisk, under a fan pointed at noodles.

"Counter," said Xu Qing, standing. "All of you."

"I'm winning," said Big Bird.

"You're losing," said Su Wen, glancing at his screen.

"I'm losing slowly."

Monk was at the counter, eyes half shut, filling the Red Ox fridge one can at a time. Boss Mei sat at the folding table beside it with three women from the street, a cigarette in her mouth, a cardigan over her shoulders, and the tiles under her hand in a wall that did not look like it was going her way.

Xu Qing put the form on the corner of the mahjong table. One of the women gasped.

"Mei Ayi. Rule seven. Sponsor signature and license number."

Mei did not look up. She held out her hand. Xu Qing put a pen in it. Mei signed in the sponsor box, without looking at the box, exactly inside its lines, then wrote a long license number in one pass and drew a tile with her other hand.

"You haven't read it," said Xu Qing.

"I know what it says." Mei discarded. "Team name?"

"It's blank," Su Wen said. "We weren't sure whether—"

She'd already written it. Team Starlight. Fast pen, slow on the last character, as though the stroke order had a step in it the rest of us didn't know about. Then she pushed the form back across the table without a glance.

"Who holds the money?"

"I do," said Xu Qing, before anybody could argue.

"Good." Mei blew smoke at the ceiling. "Boys with money are just boys who'll have no money later. Hu Zi, you little vandal, if I find your bum on my fifth machine again I'm sending you home in a box with the noodles."

"I'm being walked," said Fang Hu, with dignity. "It's not dark yet."

Mei's counter drawer stood open an inch. On top of the receipts lay a brown envelope, still sealed, with the red stamp of the county Public Security Bureau in one corner. Mei saw me look. She pushed the drawer shut with two fingers and went back to her tiles.

Xu Qing collected the funds right there, on the corner of the counter, under Mei's ashtray. Big Bird gave two yuan, damp from his apron. Su Wen counted out three coins and put the rest back with a small, private wince. I put in one yuan and kept ninety fen for emergencies. Fang Hu produced a five-jiao coin from his shoe.

"Hood," said Xu Qing.

"Not on the team," said the hood.

Xu Qing wrote Hood: 0 (pending) and looked at the total. "Six yuan fifty. Sixty-eight fifty to go." She didn't even move her lips for that one.

Then she sat down on an Uncle Kong box in the doorway of the back room, opened the green notebook to a fresh page, and wrote across the top in characters so neat they looked typed:

Starlight Team: rules.

We stood and watched her write. The fan clattered behind us. Our Ancient exploded quietly on Big Bird's monitor, the Deyang boys typed gg, and nobody consoled him.

It took her four minutes. Then she read them out.

"One. Every Saturday, each member shows me his weekly quiz papers. Members not in school," and she looked at Big Bird, "will do one page of arithmetic I set. Members in primary school," Fang Hu, "the same, and you'll show your Chinese homework too, because I've seen your handwriting on the bathroom wall. Two. Home by nine. Three. All money goes to the treasurer. Four. Receipts for everything. Five. No lying to parents." She paused. "Without first telling me what the lie is. Six. Su Wen's English classes are not to be missed for any reason. Seven. No playing during school hours, including lunch. Eight. Nobody touches the form but me. Nine. Lin Chen, top twenty-five at midterms, underlined. Ten. Hood will remove hood." The hood said no. She wrote (pending) again, harder. "Eleven. Lin Chen will not sigh like an old man during meetings."

"That's specific," I said.

"It's been a problem."

Su Wen had his color back. He kept looking at rule six.

Xu Qing closed the notebook, stood, and smoothed the front of her tracksuit. It did not need smoothing. Her ears had gone pink, and her voice went up a level of formality to compensate.

"I want to be clear," she said. "This is not support. I don't support this. It is my duty as class monitor to prevent members of my class from ruining their futures in an unsupervised manner."

"So now they're supervised," said Monk, from the fridge.

She ignored him with tremendous effort.

I looked at the poster on the wall behind the counter, the one Mei had taped up crooked on Thursday without being asked. Sunday, October 15. Thunder Net City. Four weeks. Seventy-five yuan, a carry who'd only promised one game, a mid who wasn't on the team, a support whose father could end us with one dinner-table conversation, an offlane who'd just lost to five people alone and was proud of it, and a treasurer with eleven rules.

In the first life I'd never had a single one of these.

"Twenty-nine days," said Su Wen.

"Four weeks and one day," Xu Qing corrected, and wrote it at the bottom of the page.
