# Chapter 11: Let Him Finish

I'd written two lines in the back of my notebook, on the night I bit my own hand to check I was real, under the stocks and the houses and the Bitcon I still couldn't find.

Ask Ma what they eat. Let Ba finish a sentence.

Last Sunday I'd managed neither. I'd survived one silence, the one after "thirty-four," and spent the rest of the call talking over every other pause like a man throwing gravel into potholes. This time I'd read the two lines three times over my porridge, and Grandma had watched me do it and said nothing, which from her was a kind of interrogation.

At five past twelve she came up the three steps from the market. She'd left her jars under a sheet of plastic with Auntie Yang watching them from the cloth stall. She came home every Sunday at this time and had never once said it was for the call. She washed her hands and dried them on her apron. Then she took the Novia down from the top of the television, from between the plastic peonies and the doily, and unwrapped the handkerchief one corner at a time.

She looked at the phone. She looked at me.

Then she handed it over.

"Don't drop it," she said. "Go outside. It hears better outside."

Last time she had held it to my ear herself and stood there like a nurse with a drip. I'd been promoted. I took the phone out to the front steps with both hands, the way you carry something that belongs to an old woman, and sat down on the middle step.

My body still braced for the knee. It didn't come. It never came. Fifteen-year-old knees take a step like they've never heard of steps.

The lane was quiet in the noon way. Rice steam came out of every window, a radio somewhere was playing Sichuan opera with the volume of a threat, and the drain cover by the Chens' gate was muttering with last night's rain. The Novia's little green screen said 12:09. I watched it go to 12:10. At 12:11 it buzzed in my hand like an insect in a matchbox, and I nearly did drop it.

"Wei?"

"Ba."

"Mm." Then the line. Long distance in 2006 didn't go quiet. It hissed like a wok, and underneath the hiss, very far away, someone else's conversation leaked through in Cantonese, a woman laughing at something I would never know. My father breathed on the other end of it.

"You ate?" he said.

"Ate."

The old me, both old mes, would have jumped in here. Fine, Ba, school's fine, Nainai's fine, okay, okay. I'd done it my whole first life. I'd let him get halfway into a hundred sentences and then said okay, okay and passed the phone back to Grandma. At fifteen I'd thought he had nothing to say. At thirty-one I'd stopped being able to ask.

I sat on the step and didn't say anything.

The silence went on. Four seconds. Six. The Cantonese woman laughed again. I counted the way Su Wen counts, under my breath, and I'd got to nine when my father took a breath I could hear all the way from Guangdong. It was the breath of a man about to lift something heavy.

"The foreman, Lao Ou, he said if the January order goes out on time, and it should, the line's been fast this month, he might let a few of us go before the twenty-eighth, before the rush, so we could get tickets that aren't standing, so your mother doesn't have to stand the whole way to Chengdu like last year, with her feet." He stopped. "That's what he said. Might."

I sat with that.

It was the longest sentence I had ever heard my father say. I mean ever, across both lives. It had commas in it. It had a subordinate clause about my mother's feet. I hadn't known he had those.

"That'd be good," I said, and my voice did something on "good" that I covered by clearing my throat. "That'd be really good, Ba."

"Might," he said again, like he was afraid the word would break if he put it down too hard. "Your mother wants the phone."

There was a scuffle, the plastic knock of a receiver changing hands. Then my mother came on, already halfway through a question, because she always started talking before the phone reached her ear.

"—eating properly? Your grandmother says you're eating, but she'd say that if you were eating paper. Are you eating meat? Is she buying meat? Don't let her give you her share, she does that, she pretends she isn't hungry."

"I'm eating. She made tofu." I looked at the sky over the lane, a low gray lid. Rain was coming back. "Ma. What do you eat?"

"What?"

"At the factory. In the canteen. What do they give you?"

She went quiet.

Not the expensive kind of quiet my father did. Something else. Through the hiss I could hear people behind her, a crowd's worth of Sunday voices, somebody shouting a name, a metal door. My mother said nothing for a long moment, and I understood with a horrible slow clarity that in all the phone calls of two lives nobody had ever asked her that. Not me. Maybe not anyone.

"Rice," she said finally. Her voice had gone careful. "Cabbage. Winter melon. Always winter melon, they must grow a mountain of it somewhere. Tuesdays there's pork, two pieces, and the Hunan girls fight over the fatty ones." A small laugh, surprised at itself. "There's no chili. They don't know what chili is down here. The jar your grandmother gave me at New Year, I finished it in March. I've been scraping the lid."

"I'll make her send another one."

"Don't make her do anything, her knee—" She caught herself. "How's her knee? Is she going to the doctor? Chen, tell me honestly, is she—"

The phone left my hand.

Grandma had been standing just inside the front door the whole time. Of course she had. She'd taken the Novia out of my fingers so fast I was still holding the shape of it, and now she was holding it a hand's width from her mouth like a bowl of soup she meant to finish, and she was shouting.

Grandma believed long distance meant volume. Dongguan was far, so Dongguan got all of it.

"IT'S RAINING," she told Guangdong. "Three days! The whole street is mud, Old Tan's drain is coming up again, I told him, I told his father, I'll tell the street committee. And the radishes! You want to know what radishes cost? One yuan two a jin! One-two! That Gou from the wholesale yard, with his scales, his scales have a thumb in them, I've seen the thumb—"

Faintly, from the phone, I heard my father: "Ma. Ma, the minutes."

She did not hear my father. She had decided not to have ears for this portion of the call.

"—and I said to him, Gou, I said, take your thumb off that scale or I'll take it home in a jar—" and she finished the sentence in dialect so old and so specific that I won't translate it, partly because I'm not sure it was legal and partly because three provinces away I heard my mother say "Ma!" the way you say it to a child licking a railing.

The screen ticked over. 5:14. 6:02. 6:51. My father's long sentence had probably cost a yuan. Grandma's radishes cost more than the radishes.

At 7:20 she stopped as suddenly as she'd started, said "Here, the boy," and pushed the phone back at me. Then, without a word, she lowered herself onto the step beside me.

It took her a while. One hand on the doorframe, one on my shoulder, her weight going down slow and crooked onto the good side. Her left knee made a small dry sound, like a twig under a shoe, and her mouth went thin. For once the old-person noise on the front steps wasn't mine.

My mother hadn't asked about the knee again. Grandma had seen to that. She'd buried the question under two minutes of rain and radishes, on purpose, and now she was sitting next to me with her hands folded on her apron, looking at the lane as if she'd just come out for air.

I didn't look at her knee. I looked at her hands. Neither of us said anything about any of it.

"Chen?" My mother sounded out of breath, like she'd been the one shouting. "Are you still there?"

"Still here."

"Your grandmother," she said, and then didn't finish, and I could hear her deciding to laugh instead. "Your father wants to know about school."

"Ma. The test. I was thirty-fourth." She knew. He'd have told her. I said it anyway, because I needed it on the table before the next part. "Midterms, I'm going to be top twenty-five."

Silence. She passed it on; I heard her say twenty-five to someone. Then the scuffle again, and my father.

"Twenty-five," he said.

"Twenty-five."

"Last year you said top twenty."

I had no memory of this. None. Some earlier, stupider version of me had stood exactly where I was sitting and promised my father top twenty and then, presumably, come forty-something. I'd forgotten it the way I'd forgotten the capital of Mongolia. He hadn't. He'd been carrying it around a factory floor in Dongguan for a year.

"I know," I said, which was a lie, and then, because it was the only true thing available, "This time I'm working at it."

"Mm," said my father. It was not the sound of a man who believed his son. It wasn't the sound of a man who'd given up on him either. It was a man filing something away to check later. I'd heard Teacher Gao make the same noise over a quiz paper.

Next to me, Grandma sniffed. I couldn't tell which side she was on.

The screen said 7:48. Then 8:10. I waited for "This line costs." I waited for it like you wait for a step in the dark. It didn't come. Seven had come and gone, and my father, who had cut the first call of this life off at seven minutes on the dot, was still on the line asking whether the school had fixed the roof over the science room, and listening to the answer, which I made up.

At nine minutes my mother got the phone back and dropped her voice to the register she used for crimes.

"Chen. Do you need anything? I can put twenty in with next month's, your father won't—"

Twenty yuan. I thought of the 68.50. I thought of Xu Qing's green notebook and rule five, no lying to parents without first telling her what the lie is, and of Xu Qing's face if I came to her with a fund that had my mother's secret twenty in it. I thought of a woman scraping a chili jar lid in March.

"No, Ma. Keep it." I hesitated. "Buy pork on a day that isn't Tuesday."

She laughed, a real one this time, and something in it went wet at the edges. "Listen to him," she said, to someone. "Listen to him." Then: "Your father again. Quick, he's making the face."

"Study, then," my father said. He paused. The hiss filled in for him. I thought for a second the line had gone, and then he said, roughly, like a man reading an instruction off the side of a box, "Don't ruin your eyes."

Click.

The screen said 11:03. Then it went back to the clock: 12:22.

I sat there holding it. Don't ruin your eyes. He meant books. He meant the bare bulb I'd read under as a kid, the one my mother used to switch off on me. He had no idea I was about to go and spend the afternoon two feet from a CRT monitor that flickered at the speed of a migraine. It was the clumsiest thing anybody had said to me all month. I'd have paid for the minutes myself.

Grandma pushed herself up off the step, hand on my shoulder again, heavier this time.

"Your father talked," she said.

"He did."

She considered the lane, the gray lid of the sky, the muttering drain. "Must be sick," she decided, and went inside.

I wrapped the Novia in its handkerchief the way she did, one corner at a time, and put it back on the television between the plastic peonies and the doily, screen side down, because that's how she left it. She was in the kitchen tying her apron with her back to me. The rice bin stood by the stove with the cup in it.

I didn't ask. I took the basin, scooped three cups, and ran the tap. The first water came off milky white and I tipped it into the bucket she kept for the plants; she'd skin me if it went down the drain. I swirled it with my fingers the way I'd watched her do it ten thousand times without ever once trying it myself: second rinse, third rinse, until the grains showed through clean. It took two minutes, maybe less. My hands knew how even if I didn't.

Grandma watched from the stove, chopsticks in her fist.

"Too much water," she said, when I set it on the burner.

She didn't fix it.

We ate at the folding table with the door open and the rain starting again, soft, on the steps where we'd been sitting. She gave me her piece of tofu skin and pretended she'd dropped it on my side by accident. I gave it back. She gave it back. I ate it.

At one I put on my jacket. Ninety fen in my pocket, which at Starlight bought exactly twenty-seven minutes, and I wasn't planning to sit down for any of them. Streak's crowd played Sundays from one, and before Monk's one game at four-thirty I wanted to stand behind their chairs for a few hours and watch how they lost when nobody was making them.

"Eyes," Grandma said, from the kitchen, without turning round.

So she'd heard that too.
