# Chapter 26: The Moon Is the Same One

Grandma gave me my Mid-Autumn orders at the door, one hand on the new cane and her eyes on my collar.

"Eight o'clock. Not eight ten. I'm steaming the fish once."

"Eight."

"And do up that button. You look like you sleep under a bridge."

I did up the button. She watched me do it as if she expected the button to try something, then shut the door, and I heard the cane go back across the floor toward the radio.

The cardboard sign was gone from Starlight's street door. Four squares of tape were left at the corners, holding nothing. Upstairs, four days of being shut had let the smoke settle into the curtains, and the fridge hummed louder than I remembered, the way a house sounds after a funeral when everyone has gone home. Mei was behind the counter in her cardigan and slippers. The black shoes were nowhere. A cigarette, the mahjong tiles in their box, the ledger open. She looked exactly like herself. That took some effort, I guessed, after four days of looking like somebody else.

"Two," she said, before I'd reached the top step.

I put two coins down. What stayed in my pocket wouldn't have bought a bowl of noodles.

"That's an hour," she said. "Twenty-two died while I was gone. Raise it from the dead and you can sit on fourteen till five. After five you pay like a person."

Machine 22 had been dying for as long as I could remember. In my other life it had hung on until the summer I left, when a boy from the bus station kicked it in the power supply over a lost game and it finally stopped forgiving people. Today it only clicked. I took Mei's screwdriver to the back of it, blew out a fist-sized mouse of dust, reseated the memory card, and it started on the second try, groaning about it the whole time. I left the screwdriver beside the keyboard to give back later.

The team came up the stairs one by one, each of them a little surprised to find the place open, as if four days had been long enough to doubt it.

Big Bird came at twelve twenty with a greasy paper bag that smelled of lard and old peanuts. "FIVE-KERNEL," he announced. "TEN FOR TWO YUAN. The woman was going to throw them in the river."

"Nobody likes those."

"I like those."

Su Wen came at twelve thirty with his library bag. "Is the library open today?" I asked.

"No." He sat down. "My father didn't ask."

Monk came out of the back room in yesterday's T-shirt, lifted his chin at me, and sat at the end of the row. Machine 38 was empty, the chair pushed in, the keyboard squared to the edge of the desk.

Streak's holiday crowd was already in the corner: Streak, Deng Wei, Liu Bo, Deng Wei's cousin from Deyang, who really was from Deyang. Four of them. Four of us. Streak heard us sit down and turned around in his chair.

"Four against four," he said. "Unless you're scared."

We weren't scared. We should have been.

It took eleven minutes to find out how much four days had cost. Big Bird's last hits came in late or not at all. "MY HANDS FORGOT," he told the room, "THEY ONLY REMEMBER BOWLS." And my mouse felt wrong. After five minutes on Thunder's optical one, Starlight's ball dragged across the pad with a little grinding hitch, and my thumb web started aching before the first rune. Monk farmed. Monk always farmed. But when I called a gank at the river, Big Bird went to the wrong river, the top one, and stood there in the reeds for six seconds asking where everybody was.

The only one of us who'd come back sharper was Su Wen. He tapped the desk at every thirty seconds, steady as a metronome, and said the timers in his small, even voice: "Her freeze is back in twelve." "Their eye on the ramp dies at fourteen-ten." He was right every time. We just didn't get there in time to use it.

Streak's group took the game in thirty-four minutes. Streak typed *gg* and then, after a pause, *welcome back*. That was worse than anything Deng Wei could have said, and Deng Wei said plenty.

"Again?" said Big Bird.

"Not yet," I said. "Su Wen."

He took the graph paper out of his library bag. He'd been carrying it since September under a geography workbook. Now it was full: every hero we'd faced down the left side, the minutes across the top, small pencil marks for each ultimate and each Watcher Eye, with how long until it came back.

"You've been practicing," I said.

"Four nights." He straightened the sheet. "At dinner. I timed my father's tea. It takes four minutes to cool enough to drink. It's very consistent."

"Your father's tea."

"He drinks it at three minutes forty." Su Wen frowned. "Every night. He burns himself."

So we drilled. Last hits for ten minutes each, Big Bird counting out loud and helping nobody. Then rotations: when Su Wen tapped three times, whoever was named left his lane, no questions, no "wait, I'm farming." Big Bird left late twice and early once. I left on time and my hands still arrived half a second after my head did. Monk didn't count at all. He simply went, and when he got there, he was right.

"How did you know?" Big Bird asked him.

Monk thought about it. "Felt long."

Fang Hu came up the stairs at two thirty, slowly, which meant he was bored. Mei didn't look up.

"Out."

"I'll dust," Fang Hu said. "For a jiao."

"Two jiao and you leave."

"One jiao, and I dust, and THEN I leave."

Mei looked at him for a long second, then threw him a rag. He caught it against his chest and started on the counter, the fridge door, the monitor of machine 1, the sign that said NO SMOKING.

Around three a man at 31 shouted that his screen had frozen with his character "inside a tree," and Mei went down the row in her slippers to swear at it for him. I took the screwdriver back to the counter. Fang Hu was dusting under things now, very thoroughly, the way he did anything that came with money, and he lifted the ashtray to wipe the ring under it.

There was a slip of paper there, pink, folded once, a red stamp showing through the back.

He unfolded it before I could say anything. "Ad," he read. "Min. Is. Tra. Tive." His finger moved under the characters. "Pen. Al. Ty." He looked up. "Ge, what's a penalty?"

"Give it here."

He gave it to me because I'd said it like an adult, and he still did what adults said when they said it that way. I held it low, against my leg, and read it.

Qingshui County Culture Bureau. Receipt. Starlight Net Cafe, operator Mei Lan. Reason: admitting minors to the premises. Administrative penalty: 2,000.00 yuan. Business suspended for rectification, October 2 to October 5. Payment received, October 2, 2006. A red stamp, a little crooked. A clerk's neat signature.

The cardboard sign on the door. The black shoes on the bridge on Monday morning, the handbag under her arm. *Can you read? Then you've read it.* Under the ashtray, too, where she kept things so they wouldn't blow away in a room with no wind.

I knew which night it was. I'd been under machine 14 that night with my knees at my ears, holding my breath, and so had the boy holding the rag. Big Bird had been at her counter buying noodles. She'd kept us out of sight, and I had never once asked her what that night cost.

Two thousand yuan. My parents sent home eight hundred a month. The fund was twenty-three sixty. I didn't do the division.

"Ge. What's a penalty? Why's it pink?"

Mei's slippers were coming back up the row. I folded the slip along its old crease, slid it under the ashtray, and set the ashtray down on its own ring of ash.

"It's football," I said. "When somebody fouls."

"Who fouled?"

"Dust."

Mei came behind the counter. "Are you dusting or reading?"

"Both," said Fang Hu.

"Pick."

He dusted. Mei sat down, pulled the ledger toward her, and her eyes went to the ashtray and stayed there one breath longer than an ashtray deserved. Then she turned a page.

Xu Qing arrived at four, in a pressed blouse, with her notebook and the fund envelope in a plastic sleeve. She stood at the end of our row and didn't sit.

"Rule twelve," she said. "No fund money will be spent in a rival establishment. This includes chairs."

"Nobody paid for the chair," said Big Bird.

"This includes chairs obtained by conversation."

Big Bird opened his mouth, closed it, and ate a five-kernel mooncake to have something to do with his face.

"I need the fund," I said.

Xu Qing's pen stopped. "For what purpose?"

"For the sponsor. I'll tell you after."

She looked at me for a while. She'd made a rule about lies, and another about silence, and I could see her holding my sentence up against each of them to see which one it fit. Then she wrote, and turned the notebook so I could read it: *Transfer to sponsor. Purpose: pending.* She handed me the envelope.

I took it to the counter and put it down beside the ledger. "Twenty-three sixty," I said. "Toward—"

I didn't get any further. Mei looked at the envelope. She looked at the ashtray. She looked down the room at Fang Hu, who was dusting the top of the fridge on tiptoe. Then she pushed the envelope back across the counter hard enough that it slid off the edge and I had to catch it against my stomach.

"Sponsorship doesn't take sponsorship."

"It's not—"

"Fourteen's yours till five." She lit a new cigarette off the old one. "I don't say things twice."

She had, actually. Twice now I'd tried to fix a woman's trouble by grabbing hold of it, Grandma's stall on Monday and Mei's fine today, and twice the woman had taken it back off me without thanks. I walked the envelope back to Xu Qing.

"Purpose?" she said.

"Declined."

She crossed out *pending* and wrote *Declined*, and didn't ask anything else, and I have liked her for that ever since.

"Again," I said, and sat down at fourteen.

Game two started at four twenty. At four exactly my mouse had started to stick, just as Su Wen had promised it would at Thunder; I popped the ball out, rolled it on my pant leg, and put it back without looking. Big Bird still had too little gold. Twenty-two minutes in, he'd farmed less than he should have had at fifteen, and he knew it, and he kept saying sorry to nobody in particular. But at twenty-six minutes Su Wen said, "Her freeze. Eight. Gone," and tapped three times, and Big Bird left his lane on the third tap without a word, and Monk was already in the river when we got there, because it had felt long. Streak's Frost Maiden died with her ice still down. Deng Wei's cousin died running toward her, which was loyal and pointless.

We won at forty-one minutes, slowly, because nobody had the gold to end it sooner. Streak typed *gg* and nothing else. That was better.

At five I got up from fourteen and stood behind Big Bird's chair, because I couldn't pay like a person. Somewhere in the first ten minutes I stopped watching his screen.

Two thousand yuan. I'd been running this team on loose change: a jiao here, a receipt there, seventy-five yuan and one Sunday at the end of it. Mei had just paid more than twenty-six times that for one night, and she'd earn it back two yuan an hour from boys who left noodle cups on her keyboards. The fifteenth wasn't a plan. It was the first step of one, and I'd been staring at the step.

I took the calendar pages out of my pocket, found one with a clean back, borrowed Su Wen's pencil without asking, and wrote on top of Big Bird's monitor.

*1. HANDS*

*Thunder: optical mice, fast screens. We train on ball mice and old CRTs. Ask Luo: can players bring their own mouse?*

*One optical mouse for the team after the fee. Thirty-something yuan at the electronics stall by the bus station. Ten minutes each a day, in turns. Later, one each.*

*Mouse pad: back of an exercise book. Clean 14's ball every day. Every hour.*

*Don't get used to anything you can't carry.*

*2. MONEY (real, slow, legal)*

*Column. 100 per 1,000 characters. One a month if they keep buying. Never the Sunday part.*

*Repairs. Every shop on the main street has a computer nobody understands. Viruses, keyboards, power supplies.*

*Replay lessons. Teach people their own mistakes, not ours. Charge.*

*Prize money: never count it before it's paid.*

*NOT: stocks. Scratch cards. Peaches.*

*3. TOURNAMENTS (dates are guesses)*

*Oct 15, Thunder. 600 + 100 hours.*

*Winter: a cafe cup in Deyang? Mianzhu? Ask Luo where Hammer's team went last year.*

*Spring: the Chengdu cafe circuit. Real teams, real money. Bus 30 each way, times six.*

*Summer: Chengdu final. Then Shanghai, nationals.*

*Later: the map becomes its own game. Then it's a job. Then it's stadiums.*

*4. TRAINING*

*Every day: last-hits 10 min, everyone. One drill. One game. Then stop.*

*Bird: one word, "go." Teach him to wait for it. Never yell at him.*

*Su Wen: timers for five. The Eyes. Give him the map, not the gold.*

*Typhoon: wins her lane every time. Teach her what happens after the lane.*

*Monk: sleeps till two. Train around his shift, not through it.*

*Me: hands, two hours a day. No talking in games unless it's a call.*

*Sundays: one replay, all of us, one screen. One mistake each. Mine first.*

*Grades: Xu Qing. School first or no team. Sleep counts as practice.*

Big Bird died. "You're on my map," he said, without turning around. I moved the paper an inch.

Su Wen read over my shoulder for a long time. He read it top to bottom, then went back up to check, as if it were an exam paper.

"Your sevens look like ones," he said.

"I'll fix them."

He didn't go back to his count. His finger came down on the page, on the spring line, the one about Chengdu, and stayed there.

"In spring I have the zhongkao," he said. "June. Before that, mock exams. Every Saturday from March." He said it in his cooldown voice. "My father will not sign anything with Chengdu on it."

"Rule seven. Mei signs."

"Mei can sign for a net cafe. She can't sign for my father."

Big Bird's hero came back to life with a chime. Somebody across the room laughed at something on a screen.

Su Wen looked down the page to the training part, to his own name, and his thumb tapped the edge of the monitor once.

"You wrote me down for next summer," he said, more quietly.

I didn't have an answer that wasn't a lie, so I didn't give one. He went back to his count. I looked at the page for a while, at *Su Wen: timers for five. The Eyes. Give him the map, not the gold,* and didn't cross anything out.

I'd made lists like this in my first life, at twenty-two, at twenty-five, in team houses at three in the morning. They'd all been about me. My hands, my hero pool, my contract. This one had five names on it, and two of them couldn't get into a net cafe without borrowing a laid-off worker's ID.

Under the training part there was still room at the bottom of the page. I wrote *5.* and then *Hu Zi* and stopped, and couldn't think what came after it. Behind the counter, Mei turned on the light over the ledger.

I folded the page twice and put it in the pocket with Xiaoyu's note. Outside, the light had gone.

The roof door was at the back, past the boxes of noodles, a metal stair and a bolt that stuck. In my first life I'd known about it for two years and never once gone through it. Up top there was a water tank streaked with rust, a broken plastic chair, Mei's laundry line with one cardigan on it, and a row of scallions growing in paint tins. The town spread out below us in its smoky evening, the river beyond the market roofs, the hills on the other side going dark blue.

The moon came up out of a bank of cloud over the hills, big and orange, and for a moment the whole roof went quiet.

"Six thirty-eight," said Su Wen.

Big Bird handed the mooncakes round. Everybody bit, and then everybody started picking. The green shreds came out first, then the red ones, then the bits that were either winter melon or a mistake. Big Bird ate his whole and then ate everyone else's discards off a napkin, one by one, perfectly happy.

Xu Qing cut hers into four equal pieces with the edge of her ruler and ate them in order. Su Wen ate a third of his, looked at his watch, said, "Eleven minutes to walk. My mother sets the table at seven," and went down the stairs holding the rest. Monk wrapped two mooncakes in a napkin and put them in his pocket, and nobody asked, and he didn't say.

Fang Hu leaned on the parapet with his chin on his hands, staring at the moon, talking to it about the air conditioning at Thunder.

The metal stair rang. Mei came up in her slippers with her cigarette and two mooncakes in red paper. She walked behind Fang Hu without stopping and dropped one into the hood of his big jacket.

"Crumbs on my roof, you sweep it."

He reached back, found it, and held it up to the moon to show the moon.

Then she came and stood beside me, not looking at me, and put the other one into my hand. Lotus-seed paste, heavy, the good kind, the oil coming through the paper to my fingers.

"The moon's the same one it always was," she said. "Eat."

I had spent two Mid-Autumns of my first life in the room under our feet, at machine fourteen with headphones on, while Grandma's fish went cold under a plate at home. After that there had been a team house in Shanghai, with somebody else's family on the television, and then a few years when I didn't notice which night it was at all.

I didn't say anything. I ate it.
