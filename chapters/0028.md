# Chapter 28: Same Time Next Week

Grandma's phone rang at nine minutes past twelve. She let it ring twice so nobody in Dongguan would think she'd been sitting next to it.

She had been sitting next to it since half past eleven. First she moved the peanuts closer to it, then her cane, then me.

"You," she said, and pushed it across the table.

I pressed the green button. "Ma."

"Is she sitting? Chen-wa, is she sitting down, is the knee bad today, did she take the pills, the night plaster, is she putting it on at night or in the morning, because the doctor said—Lin Jun, hold it closer, he can't hear—"

"I can hear."

"He can hear," my father said, behind her. The receiver knocked against something, and then both of them were on it at once, two heads over one phone in the corridor of Building Three, breathing in different rhythms.

"I'm sending three hundred," my mother said. "Tomorrow. The post office by the gate opens at eight, I already asked, I asked Friday."

"And Lao Ou says we can go early." My father, flat, the sentence carried a long way to get here. "At New Year. The tenth. Not the sixteenth."

In my first life they came home on New Year's Eve, mostly. One year they came on the second day, because the train sat outside Changsha for nine hours, and I was already on the computer when they walked in and didn't get up. My mother stood in the doorway with a bag of Dongguan sausages, waiting for me to finish the game. I finished the game.

Grandma held out her hand. She didn't ask. She just held it out with the palm up until the phone was in it.

"Three hundred what," she said in dialect, loudly enough that she could have skipped the phone. "Keep it. You'll want it when you're old. I'm already old, I've got what I've got. A leg and a half and a stick." A thin sound came out of the earpiece. "Don't cry, Zhou Hong. It's your money you're crying on." The sound got thicker. "And you. The tenth, the sixteenth. Is Lao Ou your father now? You come when it's New Year. Like people."

She handed the phone back to me with the face she used on short-weight scales.

I could hear my mother trying to stop crying and my father telling her to stop. That had never once worked on anybody. If I said nothing, Grandma had won, the money stayed in Dongguan, and my mother cried on the bus back to Building Four. If I said "Ma, don't send it, she's fine," I was doing what everybody in this family had done for years, putting a wall up and calling it a roof.

"Ma," I said. "Listen. The doctor money came out of the stall roll. All of it. And the stall can't open without stock. She has to buy cabbage before she can sell any cabbage."

Grandma's eyebrows went up.

"So send it for that. For the brace and the stock. Not for her. For the stall." I looked at Grandma while I said it. "Nainai decides when the stall opens and what she buys with it. That's hers."

A pause on the line. My mother blew her nose away from the phone, politely. "Can I—how long does a money order take, a week, more, it's—"

"Can't you just send it straight to her phone?"

Silence from both of them, and from Grandma.

"Money," my father said. "To a phone."

"Credit. Phone credit. I meant, so she can call you."

"Your grandmother doesn't use the credit she's got," my mother said, and it was almost a laugh, wet. "She has eleven yuan on there since May."

"Twelve," Grandma said, to the peanuts.

"And Ba," I said. "Don't give the leave back to Lao Ou. Keep it. The tenth. Write it down somewhere."

"Write it down where," Grandma said.

"On the calendar."

My father breathed out through his nose. The line hissed with it. "The exam. Thursday," he said.

"Thursday and Friday."

"The whole floor knows."

"I know they know."

"Good. Don't skip school." Then, faster, "Your mother's minutes," and the line went dead with my mother still saying something that began with "and."

I put the phone down on its towel. Grandma looked at it for a while.

"For the cabbage," she said. "Not for me. The cabbage can say thank you."

She got up on the second try, waved my hand off, and went to the rice-shop calendar by the door with the pencil she kept behind it. She lifted the pages through October and November to December, and stopped, because the calendar ended there. The rice shop gave the new ones out with the first sack of the year.

She turned December over and wrote on the back of it, slowly, in characters that leaned on each other: *Twelfth month, the tenth, they come.* Then she let the pages fall back to October.

"What are you looking at," she said. "Eat your peanuts."

---

At twenty past four I put eighty fen on Starlight's counter. Mei looked at it lying there and then at me.

"One twenty short," she said.

"I know."

She opened the ledger, wet the pencil on her lip and wrote my surname, and next to it 1.20, under a column of names I wasn't going to read while she was watching me. "Fourteen," she said.

Big Bird slapped two coins down beside my eighty. "TWO. FOR HU ZI."

Fang Hu was already standing behind machine fifteen with both hands on the back of the chair, the way he'd stood behind it all week, as if the chair might be taken from him by a passing adult. Mei looked at the coins, at Fang Hu, at the clock.

"He's out at five," she said.

"IT'S A FORTY-MINUTE GAME."

"Then he's out at five, and you lose at ten past."

She took the coins anyway. Fang Hu sat on his knees to see the screen properly, said "Ge, Ge, I'm the doctor," and picked Hex Doctor before anybody had told him what to pick. He had watched Su Wen play it twice from behind Su Wen's chair. He thought that was the same as playing it.

Su Wen had come at four with a library book under his arm and the look of a boy who had timed his father's tea before leaving. Monk sat at the end of the row, eyes half shut, his headset on one ear. Across the room Streak, Deng Wei and Liu Bo had found two more boys from the skewer line on Bridge Street, and they were all in the Lianzhan room waiting, Deng Wei typing "hurry up chopstick" three times in a row because he'd found out the chat accepted it.

It went well. That was the strange part afterward, how well it went. Streak walked past the rune at four minutes and again at six, and stayed in his lane both times, a lot sooner than I'd guessed he would. I'd been paid ten yuan to make my opponents better, and here they were, better. My hands were slow in the lane. I lost two easy creeps to a sticky mouse ball and one to plain thinking about the call, and on my right Su Wen tapped the desk at the top of every thirty seconds and said things like "Their Eye at the bottom goes in forty" in a voice that didn't expect thanks.

Fang Hu talked the whole time. "I hit him, Ge, did you see, I hit him with the bouncy thing, it bounced, did it bounce on two or three, Ge—" and it had bounced on two. He was terrible and delighted, and he died once walking into the fog to get a better look at a tree, and Big Bird roared "WHY" at the whole room. Mei shouted "Volume" without looking up from her tiles.

By minute twenty we were ahead by two towers and a lot of kills. At twenty-two Streak's side gathered by the Old Wyrm's pit. It was the right idea, too late. Su Wen said, "Their stun hero has nothing for another minute." Monk's Drow drifted to the edge of the river. Big Bird waited in the trees, quiet, because I'd asked him to and because he was learning that waiting was also a thing you did with your hands.

"Go," I said.

Big Bird went. Monk went. "Hu Zi, stun the one in front."

Nothing bounced.

"Hu Zi."

On my left his Hex Doctor stood beside a tree on the riverbank with his little staff up, doing nothing. I turned my head. Fang Hu's hand had left the mouse. It was flat on his jacket, over the left side of his chest, pressing, and his face had gone the color of the wall behind the fridge. His mouth was slightly open. He was breathing fast and shallow, and when I looked at him he tried to smile, and the smile came out before the rest of his face was ready for it.

My fingers were on the inside of his wrist before I'd decided anything. Two of them, below the thumb, where it was thin. My eyes went to the game clock in the corner of the screen. 22:14. I counted.

I didn't know why fifteen. My head said fifteen and I did fifteen, watching the seconds turn over above a fight I'd stopped playing, counting under my breath. It was quick. It was too quick, I thought, though I didn't know what it was too quick compared to. Somewhere around nineteen the beat stopped. Just a gap, the length of one missing, and then it came back hurrying, as if it had dropped something and run to catch up. At 22:29 I had twenty-nine.

"Ge." Fang Hu laughed, a short breathy one. "Are you a doctor?"

"No."

"Your hand's cold."

I let go. I didn't know where to put the hand, so I put it back on my mouse and clicked my hero somewhere into the river for no reason at all. In the headset Big Bird's hero was dying loudly.

"HU ZI?"

"Stood up too fast," Fang Hu said.

"YOU'RE SITTING."

"In my head I stood up."

A paper cup of water was on the desk by his keyboard. I hadn't heard slippers. Mei was back behind the counter with a tile in her hand, looking at it, and the cup had steam coming off it in one thin thread, from the thermos she kept under the counter for her own tea.

"Drink," I said.

He drank it in three swallows and said it was hot, and then he said it again, and some color came back up from his collar into his face, the way it does in kids, unfairly fast.

"Lin Chen," Monk said.

I looked at the screen. We were four. The Hex Doctor stood by his tree. Streak's side had won the fight at the pit and was coming, five of them, toward our middle towers. My hands were bad for the next fifteen minutes. I knew it as it was happening. I pressed the right keys a half-second after I meant to, and twice I looked left instead of at my own screen to check whether a small boy was still breathing at the normal speed, whatever that was.

Monk didn't look left. Monk's Drow stood behind our last tower and shot whatever came near it, farther and farther from her targets, which is what she's for, and when the third push broke on the steps of our base he walked forward through the wreck of it as calmly as he walks the mop down the main room at two in the morning. Big Bird stayed alive behind him because Monk said "Back" once. Su Wen kept counting. Thirty-eight minutes. Streak's Ancient fell with Fang Hu's Hex Doctor still standing beside his tree in the river, because nobody had remembered to move him.

"gg," Deng Wei typed, and then something about my mother, quietly.

Monk took his headset off. He looked across the room at Streak, longer than he usually looks at anyone.

"Next Sunday," he said. "Same time."

Streak opened his mouth, closed it and nodded. Big Bird stared at Monk as if a chair had volunteered for something.

"Five twelve," Mei said. "Staff. Out."

The game clock said twenty-five minutes. Fang Hu climbed off his knees, wobbled, made a show of the wobble so it would look like a joke, and went to the stairs.

---

He talked the whole way to the rice-shop corner. About the bounce, about how it had bounced on two, about whether the tree he'd been standing next to was the same tree he'd died walking toward earlier, about Monk saying five words (he had counted them), the most Monk had ever said, "Ge, ever, in the history." I walked on the outside. The street smelled of frying garlic and wet cardboard. Golden week was over; the shutters on the clothing shops were half down already for school tomorrow.

"Ge. Why'd you count?"

"Habit."

"Whose habit?"

"Mine."

"Why, though? Why count?"

I didn't have anything for the second why. He waited for it with his head on one side, and when nothing came he lost interest and asked about the tree again.

At the corner he looked left, right, left, and then back up at me instead of at the road.

"I'm fine, Ge. Look." He jumped twice on the spot. "See?"

"I see. Go home."

He crossed slowly and went off past the rice shop at a walk. It was the second time all week I'd seen him walk anywhere. I stood there with my thumb rubbing the two fingers I'd used. Twenty-nine. A gap around nineteen. I didn't know what a boy's wrist was supposed to say in fifteen seconds. I had never once in my life held anybody's wrist to find out, but my fingers had gone straight to the place, and my head had already said fifteen before I asked it anything.

I didn't go home. I went back up the stairs, slower than I'd come down them.

It was just after six. Mei didn't look up. The room was full of the evening crowd and the after-holiday smoke, and in the far corner, by machine 38, there was a gray hood pulled up over a head of short hair, two hours before we'd said.

The screen in front of her was off.
