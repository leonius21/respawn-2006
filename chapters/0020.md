# Chapter 20: Okay for Now

The week after the river path was all screws and fractions.

I got home that Sunday at four minutes past nine by the television clock. Grandma looked at the screen, then at me, and said, "Four." It was the whole conversation again, only shorter. She didn't go to bed until I'd eaten.

After that I was early everywhere. Machine 14 from six to seven. A power supply on 31 that smelled like burnt hair. A mouse on 9 whose rubber ball someone had stolen, the most Qingshui crime imaginable. On Wednesday I had library with Su Wen, who taught me to complete the square while his father walked past our table twice to admire us. Fang Hu came down Starlight's stairs every evening already talking. On Thursday he showed me his essay. It now read: *My father is a policeman. He is at work. He makes noodles until they give up.* That was three sentences, maybe forty characters out of three hundred. I told him the third one was the best sentence anyone in his class would write all year. He folded the paper into his receipt pocket and didn't say anything for almost a whole block. For Fang Hu, that was a coma.

Then it was October first, the start of golden week. Every shop on the main street had a red flag in its doorway, and the buses from Chengdu came in so full they leaned.

Monk walked past the pickle stall at eight that morning, on his way home from the night shift. His eyes were half shut, the usual amount. He stopped in front of the jars.

"Letter," he said. "Mei's."

Grandma pushed a plastic bag of mustard greens at him. He took it without a word of argument, which is how I knew how tired he was, and kept walking.

"Go on," Grandma said, before I could open my mouth. "Go read your letter. I can count my own radishes."

"I'll set up first."

"You'll stand there with your face going like that? Go."

I stacked the last two crates of jars beside the stall where she could reach them. "How's the knee?"

"Okay for now."

It was what she'd said every time I asked for four weeks. I'd started to hear it as one word.

---

Starlight at nine-thirty on a Sunday morning smelled like the inside of a sock that had been smoking. Six overnight kids were asleep at their machines, heads on folded arms, screens dimmed. Boss Mei sat behind the counter in her cardigan, counting a stack of eight-yuan overnight receipts and moving her lips.

She didn't look up. She lifted the glass ashtray, slid a thin envelope out from under it and held it toward me between two fingers. There was a gray ring of ash on the front, right over *Mei Lan*.

"Kept it under the ashtray," she said. "So it wouldn't blow away."

"There's no wind in here."

"Because I keep things under the ashtray." She went back to counting. "It's a yes."

"You opened it?"

"Rejections come back fat. They send your pages home to cry." She licked her thumb. "That one's thin. Congratulations. My ten percent is already sitting in the drawer, waiting for the rest of it."

I didn't sit at a machine, so she didn't charge me. That was its own small miracle. I went out and sat on the third step down, where the light from the street door was good, and opened it.

Printed letterhead: GAME FRONTLINE, an address in Beijing, a little drawing of a joystick. The rest was a form letter with blanks filled in by hand in blue ink.

*Dear Qingshui Laoren,*

*Thank you for your submission, "Three Rules for the Middle Lane." We are pleased to inform you that it will appear in our November issue, with minor edits for length. Remuneration will be sent by postal money order after publication. We would welcome further submissions.*

The signature looked like a spring someone had stepped on.

I read it three times. I'd been printed before, in the other life. My face had been on a magazine cover in 2013, a bad photo, with a headline about the future. Later the same magazine ran a column about choking with my name in the second sentence. Neither of those had felt like this. Nobody in Beijing knew who I was. A man there had read fifteen hundred characters by an old man from a town he couldn't find on a map and decided they were worth printing.

About a hundred and fifty yuan, less Mei's tenth, less whatever Beijing thought about punctuation. Su Wen would want to know. Su Wen would want to know for the rest of his life.

Then I looked up and saw the notice.

It was taped to the inside of the street door at eye level, on cardboard torn off an Uncle Kong box, in red marker in Mei's square handwriting:

CLOSED OCT 2–5

No reason. No apology. No "for renovation," which every closed shop in China said, renovated or not.

I turned around on the step. "You're closing? In golden week?"

Her voice came down the stairwell. "Can you read?"

"Yes."

"Then you've read it."

I waited. A tile clicked against the counter up there. Nothing else came.

I didn't remember this. I remembered a lot of golden weeks at Starlight. Every one of them had been forty machines full and a line on the stairs, and Mei charging a yuan for the privilege of standing in it. She'd never closed. Maybe she had and I'd been too busy to notice. That was the most likely answer, and it didn't make me feel any better.

But the letter was warm in my hand. I put the cardboard sign in a drawer at the back of my head and ran.

---

The market on the first day of golden week was the whole county in one street. People were home from Guangdong with new shoes and loud voices. Grandmothers held grandchildren they hadn't seen since spring. A man was selling plastic flags from a bicycle. I was running through all of it with the envelope held up like a ticket, and I was twenty meters from the stall when I saw her bend for the crate.

It was one of the heavy ones, the big glazed jars of chili cabbage. She'd lifted ten thousand of them. She got it to her waist and turned, and her left knee folded sideways.

It didn't buckle. It went.

I was still running. I was too far away, and I knew it before my legs did. She went down hard on her side on the wet concrete. The crate went down beside her, one jar burst, and red brine spread across the ground toward the drain, carrying cabbage with it.

"Nainai!"

By the time I got there Qiu Fang from the tea-egg stall had her by one arm. Grandma was already telling Qiu Fang to let go, in a voice you could have heard in Deyang.

"Don't touch me. Don't lift. I'm sitting. I meant to sit."

"You fell," I said.

"I sat fast."

I knelt in the brine. Her face had gone a gray I'd seen before, seven years from now, on a hospital pillow in a photo my mother sent. I hadn't come home for that one. She was holding her left knee with both hands, and her knuckles were white.

"Let me see."

"There's nothing to see. It's a knee." She looked past me at the broken jar. Then she said something to it about its mother, its mother's kiln, and the river the clay had been dug from, in a dialect so old Qiu Fang laughed out loud and then covered her mouth.

That was how I knew she wasn't dying. It didn't help my hands stop shaking.

The tofu man came over from his cart and stood there wiping his palms on his apron. Grandma pointed at him without looking up. "You. Watch the stall. You owe me."

"I don't owe—"

"You owe me since 1998."

He watched the stall.

Then she caught my wrist. Her grip was fine. Her grip was always fine.

"It's Sunday," she said, low. "Ten past twelve, your mother calls. You don't say one word about this. Not one. Your father has a line to stand on and a foreman who counts minutes. You hear me?"

"Nainai—"

"Not one word."

And there it was. Rule five. No lying to parents without first telling the class monitor what the lie was. I had signed under it, and I'd meant it, because the rules were the only reason Xu Qing let us exist. Now my grandmother was sitting in a puddle of chili brine, ordering me to break it.

I stood up to look for a way to get her home. I saw a perfect ponytail by the egg stall.

Xu Qing was holding a list in her own handwriting and a plastic bag that held, as far as I could tell, exactly one jin of eggs. She had seen everything. She was already walking toward us, careful of the brine, with the face she wore for hygiene inspections.

"Grandmother Lin," she said, and bowed a little. "Are you injured? Should I find an adult?"

"I'm an adult," said Grandma.

"A second adult."

"Who is this?"

"Class monitor," I said. "Xu Qing. Xu Qing, I need to tell you a lie."

Xu Qing went very still.

"Rule five," I said. "My grandmother wants me to not tell my parents she fell. On the call. Today. At ten past twelve."

Grandma looked from me to her. "You need permission? From her?"

"I signed something."

"In my day class monitors cleaned the blackboard."

"I also clean the blackboard," said Xu Qing. She set her eggs down on an upturned crate. She took out the green notebook and a pen, and stood in the middle of the market, between a broken jar and a man selling flags, considering the law.

"Rule five covers lies," she said finally. "A lie is saying something false. If you say nothing, it is not a lie. It is a silence."

"So I can lie."

"You can be silent. Silence is permitted until the doctor has seen the knee." She looked at Grandma. "Has a doctor seen the knee?"

Grandma didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Then the silence ends when the doctor speaks." Xu Qing wrote. I leaned over. *Lin Chen — silence (until doctor). Approved.* She underlined *doctor* twice.

Grandma watched the pen move. "Does she write down when you breathe?"

"Only when I sigh," I said.

Xu Qing didn't look up. "Rule eleven."

Then she picked up the biggest pieces of the broken jar and stacked them by the drain, sharp edges down, in order of size, and took her eggs and went. She didn't say goodbye. She looked back once from the end of the row.

---

I carried Grandma home on my back.

She refused for two full minutes and then climbed on. She did it the way she did everything she didn't want to do, all at once and furious. I braced for a crate's weight and got less than a crate. That frightened me more than the fall. My legs were fifteen and didn't hurt. That should have felt like a gift, and it felt like an accusation.

She held the envelope. She'd taken it out of my hand when I picked her up, because I needed both hands, and she said a letter in a puddle was a wasted letter.

"What is it?"

"A magazine in Beijing is going to print something I wrote."

"About the game."

"About the game."

She was quiet for half a street. Her chin was on my shoulder. She smelled like brine and the medicated oil she pretended she didn't use.

"They pay you?"

"After it's printed."

"Hm." Another half street. Then: "I'll get you to a doctor," I said. "A real one. I'll find the money, I'll—"

"Fix," she said. "Everybody wants to fix."

"It's your knee."

"Knees go, wa. Mine's sixty-eight years old. It's allowed." She shifted on my back, and I felt her wince through my whole spine. "You ran today. I saw you. Running from the top of the street like the building was on fire."

"I didn't get there in time."

"Anybody can run once. A dog runs once." Her arms tightened a little around my neck. "I don't need you to run. I need you to be here next year. When it's boring. When nothing's broken. You understand? Coming once is easy. Staying is the thing."

I didn't answer. I couldn't have, for a while. In January 2014 my phone had buzzed in a hotel in Shanghai, and I'd let it buzz because we had a semifinal in the morning. I'd told myself I'd go home after. After is a very long word.

"I'll be here," I said.

"Don't promise. You promise like your father." She let out a breath against my ear. "Just be."

We turned onto our street. The tofu man's deaf mother waved at us from her stool and shouted something cheerful about the weather. It was the wrong thing, and I loved her for it.

"The county hospital," I said. "The bone doctor sees people on Tuesdays. Qiu Fang said."

"Qiu Fang says a lot of things."

"Tuesday. I'll take you."

Grandma said nothing for six steps. I counted them.

"You carry the bag," she said. "I'm not walking through a hospital carrying my own bag like I have no grandson."

"I carry the bag."

"And after golden week, you don't skip one day of school for this leg. Not one. I won't have that boy's father thinking I'm the reason."

"Teacher Gao."

"Whoever."

"Deal," I said, and she flicked my ear for saying it like a businessman.

---

At ten past twelve the Novia buzzed on the bed beside her, where I'd laid her with a pillow under her knee and a cold towel over it. She answered it herself. She talked about the crowds and the flags and the price of radishes. When my mother asked about the knee, I heard Grandma say "Okay for now" without a wobble in it. I sat in the doorway and was silent, exactly as approved.

Afterward I took a pin from her sewing tin and put the letter on the wall above my bed. It went beside the calendar pages I'd torn off and pinned up the week before, where the twelfth and thirteenth were circled in blue and the fifteenth in red. I stood back and did the math, the kind I could actually do.

The November issue would reach the newsstands at the end of October, if I was lucky. The money order would come weeks after that. A hundred and fifty yuan was going to arrive in Qingshui sometime around the first frost. The tournament fee was due on the fifteenth. The fund was 11.60, and 63.40 was still pending in Xu Qing's handwriting. The money I'd written for would show up after the thing I'd needed it for was over.

Late again. Just with paperwork this time.

"Chen-wa."

Grandma was in the doorway. She'd gotten there on the broom, using it upside down like a crutch, which she was absolutely not supposed to do. She leaned on the frame and looked at the letter on my wall for a long moment.

"That 'okay for now,'" she said. "I'm finished with it. It's over. Tuesday, we find out what it is."

Then she went back toward the kitchen, slowly, one broom-thump at a time. For once I let her go slowly and didn't get up to help.

I looked at the calendar pages. Fourteen days to the fifteenth. Four of them were behind a cardboard sign on Starlight's door. That left ten. Tonight was one of them: the game with Monk at four-thirty, Xiaoyu at eight, home by nine.

I went to the kitchen and washed the rice before she could get to it.
