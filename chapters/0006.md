# Chapter 6: What Guifen Saw

Lin Guifen had been hiding her left knee for four years, and she was better at hiding it than the knee was at hurting.

The trick was never to let it decide when she stood up. She stood up when she chose, all at once, one hand flat on a crate as if she were checking the lid. If the knee had something to say about it, it could say it on the inside. She had raised a son that way and was halfway through a grandson. A knee was nothing.

Wednesday was a thin day at the market. The vegetable women sat on their stools fanning themselves with cardboard. The tofu man slept upright behind his white blocks with a wet towel on his head. Nobody bought pickles in the middle of a September afternoon except the customers of the woman from Shuangqiao, who in Lin Guifen's opinion deserved what they got.

At three she put the lids on her jars and told the tofu man to watch them. He did this by going on sleeping in their direction. She went round behind the market to the tea house for a few rounds.

The tea house was a tin roof, a camphor tree, and eleven bamboo chairs that had been sat in so long they leaned the same way as their owners. Auntie Pan was already shuffling, both forearms on the table. Across from her sat Qiu Fang, Old Tan's wife. Her husband's hardware shop was at the bottom of the stairs to that smoky place with the woman and her mahjong, so Qiu Fang knew who went up and who came down better than the police did. The fourth chair belonged to the tofu man's mother, who was deaf in one ear and saved the other for important things.

They played the local way. Everyone threw out one whole suit before the first round was done, and nobody stopped when someone won, so a game bled on until only one player was left standing. Lin Guifen liked it. It suited a town.

She was two tiles from a good hand when Qiu Fang said, without looking up, "Your Chen-wa has got very fond of our stairs."

"What stairs."

"The stairs. Six o'clock Monday, six o'clock yesterday, up he goes like he's late for a shift. Comes down at seven smelling like my husband." Qiu Fang threw a tile. "Skinny little thing. Walks like an old man. I thought he'd done his back."

"He's buying notebooks."

"Upstairs from a hardware shop?"

Auntie Pan made a noise into her tea. "Every grandson in Qingshui buys notebooks up there. That Mei woman will build a second floor out of our notebooks."

The tofu man's mother turned her good ear toward the table. "Who's dead?"

"Nobody," Lin Guifen said. "Yet."

She already had the speech. She had given the first version on Saturday, the one about the Liu boy from the third building who went in with good marks and came out with glasses, and it had clearly gone in one of the boy's ears and straight out the other. Tonight he would get the second version. It had the feather duster in it. She was arranging the order, the duster first or the Liu boy's glasses first, when she threw away the one tile she had been waiting for all afternoon.

Qiu Fang laid her hand down, tile by tile, slowly, to enjoy it. "Thank you, Lin Guifen."

Lin Guifen said something about Qiu Fang's ancestors and a three-legged donkey. The tofu man's mother heard every word, asked her to say it again more slowly, and moved her lips along the second time to learn it.

It cost her one yuan fifty. She had no business losing one yuan fifty on a Wednesday.

---

By seven the light had gone the color of weak tea, and the market was mostly packed away. Lin Guifen sat on her stool behind the jars and counted.

Twenty-six yuan, five jiao, in coins and soft notes and one five that had been somewhere sticky. Take out the radishes for next week, the salt, the spot fee for the old man who collected it in a cookie tin. Take out the one fifty to Qiu Fang, may her chickens lay square eggs. What was left went into the handkerchief, folded small, folded again, and into the apron pocket where her hand could find it without anyone seeing her look.

Eight hundred came from Dongguan every month, on time, the way her son did everything, without a word in it. Eight hundred was school fees and electricity and the gas cylinder and meat twice a week and the boy's shoes, which he kept growing out of on purpose. The pickles were the rest. The tin on top of the wardrobe was what was left after the rest.

On Sunday she had counted that tin twice after he told her about the notebooks. It was lighter by more than notebooks cost, unless he was buying notebooks for the whole county. She had let it go because he told her looking straight at her. The liars in her family looked at the ceiling. His father had looked at the ceiling since he was four.

She was rehearsing the feather duster when she saw him coming between the empty stalls.

School jacket over one shoulder, hair standing up on one side. Qiu Fang was right about the walk. He came like a man at the end of a long day, not a boy at the end of a short one. She could smell the smoke on him from three stalls away. She took a breath for the first line.

"You're still here," he said. "You've been standing since six this morning."

"I sat."

"On what?" He looked at the stool, which she had already folded, and then he had the crate. He hadn't grabbed it. He had lifted it before her hand got there. He set it on her little two-wheeled cart, then the second crate, then the stool, and he made a noise lifting the last one, a small grunt with a sigh in it. Then he stopped and looked at his own back, puzzled, as if a dog had failed to bark.

"What's wrong with you?"

"Nothing," he said. "That's what's wrong with me."

She didn't understand it, so she let it go. He took the cart handle. The left wheel squeaked, as it had for two years. He walked home at her pace. She noticed that because it wasn't his pace. Twice she sped up to test him, and twice he sped up with her, no faster.

At the three steps up to the door he didn't help her. He stood beside her with the cart and watched her go up. It was like watching a bowl someone has left too near the edge of the table: you don't touch it, but you're ready. She went up all three without using the wall, out of spite, and the knee told her exactly what it thought of that on the inside.

---

She fried potato strips with vinegar and dried chili, warmed the noon rice, and put out a dish of her radish. He ate the radish first. He had done that since Saturday, slowly, with his head down.

When he finished he put his bowl in the basin. He washed it himself. He had done that since Saturday too. Then he said, "Thank you, Nainai."

The first time, on Saturday over the porridge, she had thought he was making fun of her and nearly hit him with the ladle. He wasn't. He said it plainly, the way grown men say it. His father had said it to her exactly once, at the bus station the day he left for Guangdong, and it had come out sounding like sorry.

The feather duster speech stood up in her chest, looked around, and sat back down.

"Thank you," she said. "Listen to him. You think this is a restaurant? Next you'll want a bill."

"I can't afford the bill."

"No, you can't." She lowered herself onto the bench across from him, slowly, because her knee had worked a full day, and that was when he said it.

"Your knee. Does it hurt worse when it rains?"

"What knee."

"The left one."

"It hurts when nosy wa ask about it."

"There's a doctor at the county hospital who does joints." He said it carefully. "Tuesday mornings. I could go with you."

"Doctors are for people with money and nothing to do." She pointed her chopsticks at his schoolbag. "You. Homework. You were a melon-brain last week, and you're a melon-brain this week."

He looked at her for a moment longer than a boy looks at his grandmother. Then he said, "Okay," and after a pause, quieter, "Okay for now," and got out his math.

---

She did the dishes slowly so she could watch him.

First the math. He wrote, crossed out, wrote again, and muttered. "Two and three. Minus two, minus three." Then he sat back and looked at the page, pleased.

"Two and three what?"

"Two and three, Nainai. They multiply to six, they add to five." He said it as if it were obvious and had personally offended him for years.

She didn't ask what six and five had done to him.

Then the math went away, and he reached for the calendar pages.

That was another thing. The tear-off calendar by the door had been wrong since about 1998. She tore it when she remembered, ten days at once, and nobody had ever minded. On Monday she had torn it right herself, for once, and since Tuesday it had stayed right, because now he tore the page every morning before he brushed his teeth, read the date, and put the page in his pocket. She had found a little stack of them in the drawer, smoothed flat. Now he turned one over and drew on the back.

A square. A line from one corner to the other, and two more lines along the edges, so it looked like a field cut for planting. A wobbly ribbon across the middle for a river. Little circles and crosses, numbers squeezed in beside them, two, four, six, in his small hard handwriting. He drew a circle, stared at it, rubbed it out with his thumb, and drew it again a little to the left.

It looked like the charts the fortune-teller by the temple drew for women who wanted to know about their sons. It looked like nothing she knew.

What she knew was his face. When he was seven he had copied his first characters at this same table, tongue between his teeth, terrified of getting a stroke wrong. This was almost that face, but older. He wrote like someone copying a thing out quickly before he forgot it. He filled one page and reached for another without looking up.

She could have asked. Qiu Fang would have asked. Qiu Fang would have taken the page, held it up to the light and told the whole tea house what was on it by morning.

Lin Guifen took down the jar of fermented rice from the shelf above the stove instead. She had made it in August, and it had gone sweet and a little sharp, just right. She boiled water, spooned the rice in, cracked an egg and stirred it so it came apart into soft yellow threads, and added the last piece of rock sugar from the paper bag. Fermented rice soup with egg was for growing boys and for women who had just given birth, and he was at least one of those.

She set the bowl beside his pages without looking at them.

He looked up. His eyes were red at the rims. He opened his mouth.

"If you say thank you, I'll pour it on your head."

He shut his mouth. Then he grinned, quickly, a boy's grin, the first one she'd seen on him in days, and bent over the bowl.

"Eat it hot," she said. "It's no use cold."

---

The wall between their rooms was one brick thick and some plaster that had given up. She lay on her side in the dark with the knee arranged on a folded towel, where nobody could see it, and let it ache as much as it liked.

His light went off a little after eleven. She heard the bed creak, the cover shuffle, one long sigh that sounded fifty years old. Then nothing for a while, then his breathing.

On Saturday morning she had put her hand on his forehead and he had been cold right through. By evening he was lukewarm, like bad tea. She had decided it was a fever, because a fever was something she knew what to do with.

Now his breathing was slow and even, in and out. In the front room the pickle jars gave one of their slow wet burps as the brine settled. Somewhere out on the main street a motorbike went by and faded.

She lay there and counted his breaths the way she counted the takings, without meaning to, folding each one small and putting it away. She didn't stop until she'd lost count, and then she started again.
