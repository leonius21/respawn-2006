# Chapter 19: Walk with Me

He didn't wait to see whether I'd follow. He turned and walked, hands still clasped behind his back, and I went after him. The other choice was to stand alone in the orange circle looking guilty of something, and I'd already done enough of that tonight under a hood that wasn't mine.

He didn't take the main street. He went down the side steps by the rice shop, past the tailor's shutter with the faded qipao painted on it, and onto the river path.

The river in Qingshui at night smelled of wet concrete and of green things that had been dead since summer. Lamps stood along the embankment every fifty meters, and every third one was out, so we walked from light into dark and back into light again. An old man sat on an upturned bucket with a fishing rod and a radio playing Sichuan opera so low it sounded like a cat arguing in another room. Frogs were going in the reeds. Mosquitoes found my ankles right away.

Fang Jianguo walked half a step ahead of me with his back straight. I'd seen that back four times in my first life, in two years, at the top of Starlight's stairs. Each time he walked the rows slowly, looked at the machines, and the machines seemed to sit up. I never once heard him raise his voice. Kids twice my size went quiet when he passed. Grown men put out their cigarettes.

Tonight there was no uniform, no clipboard man, no flashlight. Just the jacket, the cheap pants and those shoes. Black, plain, polished hard enough to catch the lamplight. On the left one, the lace had broken at some point and been knotted back together. The knot sat on top of his foot, small and ugly, and it was the only thing about him that didn't look issued.

We passed one lamp. Then another.

The thirty-one-year-old in me wanted to offer him a cigarette. I didn't have one, and I wasn't old enough to have one, and I'd quit in 2019 anyway. I put my hands in my pockets. The ninety fen clinked.

"You know my name," I said.

"Your grandmother sells pickles at the market."

"...Yes."

"Good pickles."

I couldn't tell if that was a compliment or a confession. Two Wednesdays ago, under a desk in Starlight's back room, Fang Hu had told me my feet smelled like pickles. I decided not to think about how good a policeman's nose might be.

"Thank you," I said. "I'll tell her."

"Don't."

So I wouldn't.

The opera behind us faded. The path ran along behind the market, roughly toward Grandma's building, and I couldn't decide if that was a kindness or just where the path went.

"You walk my son to the corner," he said.

"When the streetlights come on."

"Every day."

"Every day he's there. Some days he isn't."

"Who told you to."

It wasn't really a question. He said it flat, like he was filling in a box.

I thought about lying and found I didn't want to. Lying to this man would be like lying to weather.

"Boss Mei asked me."

He walked three steps without saying anything. For him, that wasn't long. Then: "Why you."

"I was there."

"Hm."

A moth went around the next lamp in tight, stupid circles. Below us the water slid past, black, carrying a plastic bag that kept trying to look like a fish.

"Does he run?"

"Everywhere," I said. "He runs off from the corner every night. He doesn't look at the road much."

"He looks."

"Not much."

Fang Jianguo didn't argue. I took that as the closest thing to agreement his face had.

"Does he get out of breath?"

I opened my mouth and shut it again. The frogs filled in for me.

Yesterday at the corner. The curb, the second where his face went somewhere else, his hand flat on the left side of his chest over the pocket where he kept his receipt. His fingers in my sleeve. Then the grin, and "Stood up too fast," and he was gone, arm waving over his head.

I'd told myself kids get dizzy. Kids get dizzy all the time. Kids skip lunch and stand up fast and grow two centimeters overnight and their blood can't keep up. I'd told myself all that in the time it took him to reach the end of the block.

"Yesterday," I said. "At the corner. He got dizzy stepping off the curb. He held onto my sleeve for a second. He said he stood up too fast."

Fang Jianguo stopped walking.

He didn't turn around. He stood in the dark between two lamps with his hands behind his back, and I stopped too, because walking past him felt like walking past a closed gate.

"Did he sit down?"

"No. He laughed. Then he ran."

"He always says that."

"That he stood up too fast?"

He didn't answer. He started walking again, and I followed, and I filed away "always" in a part of my head I didn't like opening.

"Does he talk too much?"

"Yes," I said.

He looked at me for the first time since the stairs. Short hair going gray at the temples, a shaving nick on his jaw, warm tan skin gone sallow under the orange light. His eyes were Fang Hu's eyes with all the noise taken out.

"Is that the wrong answer?" I asked.

"It's the answer."

"He talks the whole way," I said. "Every night. Five minutes of walking, and he can fit in maybe forty subjects. Big Bird's ears. Whether Monk shaves his head or it just grows like that. On Thursday he wanted to know if frogs have ears."

"They do."

"That's what I told him."

"He asked me," Fang Jianguo said. "In July."

I tried to imagine it. This man and that kid at a table, the boy asking about frogs, the man saying "They do," and the boy's face falling a little because it hadn't been a long enough answer to hold onto. Then the boy carrying the question around for two months to see if somebody else had more.

I'd only said "They do, they're just inside." Seven words. Apparently enough.

"At home," Fang Jianguo said, and then didn't say anything for a while. A motorbike crossed the bridge up ahead, its headlight sweeping the water. "At home he says 'I ate.' And 'Nothing.'"

He said it like reading out an inventory. Two items.

I didn't have anything clever for that. I didn't try.

"He's writing an essay about you," I said.

That got another look.

"For school. 'My Father.' He showed it to me yesterday."

"What does it say."

I could have made something up. Something about pride, about the uniform, about noodles boiled until they gave up. It would have been easy and kind, and it would have been mine instead of his son's.

"It says you're a policeman," I said. "And that you're at work."

The frogs kept going. Somewhere behind us the old man's radio swelled for a high note and fell back.

"That's all?"

"He says it's finished. It's just short."

Fang Jianguo nodded once, to the river.

Then he looked down, because the knot on his left shoe had come undone again. The two broken ends of the lace lay on the concrete like something dropped.

He crouched.

It was the first time I'd seen him lower himself for anything. He went down on one knee on the wet path, in his good cheap pants, and bent over the shoe, and from where I stood I saw the back of his neck. It was sunburned in a stripe where the uniform collar usually stopped. His fingers were thick and the lace ends were short. He had to pinch them close to the leather and work them over each other, slowly, the same way the knot had clearly been tied a hundred times before: under, over, pull. He pulled it tight, looked at it, and pulled it tighter.

For maybe ten seconds he wasn't a raid. He was a man on a dark path with a shoe that needed a new lace, and he hadn't bought one, because the old one could still be knotted.

He stood up. His knee didn't crack. Mine wouldn't have either; I was fifteen.

"Mei Lan's place is licensed," he said.

"I know."

"Licensed places get inspected."

"I know."

"Good."

That was all. He didn't mention the night of the thirteenth. He didn't mention a back room, or machine 14, or the smell of instant noodles somebody had stirred too hard to cover the smell of five kids breathing under desks. He didn't say he'd seen us. He didn't say he hadn't. He just looked at the next lamp along, and whatever he knew stayed behind his face, where he kept everything.

I thought about asking. I really did. Did you see us? Did you count the shoes?

I didn't. Some doors you knock on, and you live in the answer for the rest of your life.

We reached the steps by the bridge, the ones that came up two streets from Grandma's. He stopped at the bottom. Up the other way, across the bridge, were the police housing blocks, where a light was probably on and a pot of noodles was probably going cold beside a boy who said "I ate."

"Home by nine," Fang Jianguo said. "Both of you."

He didn't wait for me to answer that either. He went up the bridge steps, hands behind his back again, the knotted lace a small dark lump on his shoe, and the orange light took him and then the dark did.

I stood at the bottom of the steps.

The frogs. The moth. The river with its plastic fish.

Both of you.

I went back over it like Su Wen going over a cooldown, counting from the cast instead of from when I'd noticed. He'd asked if his son runs. If he gets out of breath. If he talks too much. He'd asked who told me to walk him, and why me, and he'd heard the answers.

And he hadn't said stop.

He was a man who gave orders the way other people gave the time. If he'd wanted the walks to end, he'd have used three words, maybe two. He'd used five, and one of them was "both."

Tomorrow the lights would come on over the rice shop the way they did every evening, and a kid in an oversized jacket would come down Starlight's stairs talking before his feet hit the bottom. I'd be standing in the circle.

I didn't know what time it was. Grandma would be by the window with the television on, pretending she wasn't watching the door.

I took the steps two at a time and ran.
