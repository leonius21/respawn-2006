# Chapter 21: The Sign on the Door

By two o'clock on the first day of golden week, Starlight was so full that Mei Lan had stopped writing names in the ledger and started writing machine numbers. Names were for regulars. The holiday had brought her strangers: boys home from vocational schools in Mianyang and Deyang, first real money in their pockets, chins carried a little higher than in June. A semester away hadn't made them any taller. She took their two yuan an hour, gave them the machines with the bad mice, and let the broken air conditioner treat them all the same.

The drawer under the counter stuck where it always stuck. She worked it open with two fingers.

Ledger. Receipt pad. A ball of rubber bands nobody had ever thrown. Then the long brown envelope from the county public security bureau, red seal still whole. She lifted it out by the corners, laid it face down on the glass, and took the green cash tin from underneath it.

The carbon copy was folded into the lid. She didn't unfold it. She knew the number, and the box that was checked, and the signature at the bottom, every stroke straight, as if his pen came with a ruler. He had written it against the roof of his patrol car in the alley the night of the raid, under the bulb over her back stairs. He had held it out without looking up. She had taken it without looking down.

She counted the money out in tens. Then she counted it again. It came out the same both times. She slid the tens into a bank envelope, put the tin back exactly where it had been, laid the brown envelope on top of it with the flap down, and shut the drawer with her hip.

At half past two the stairs started climbing slowly. That was how she knew he was bored. When Fang Hu had somewhere to be, the stairs sounded like a drum kit going down them.

"Out," she said, before his head cleared the floor.

"I'm not even in."

"Then it's quick."

"It's a holiday." He came up one more step, hands in the pockets of the jacket that would fit him at twenty. "Everybody's on holiday. Even the school's on holiday."

"Holidays happen outside." She pointed her cigarette at the window. "Sky. Free. Go stand under it."

"The sky doesn't have a fridge."

"Neither do you. OUT."

He retreated exactly three steps, until his eyes were level with her slippers, and parked his chin on the top stair. From there he could see half the room and all of her.

"Ge's nainai fell down at the market," he said. "In the cabbages. Qiu Fang told everybody in line for tea eggs. Ge carried her home on his back, the whole way."

Mei tapped ash into the tray. "Did he."

"Over the bridge." The boy yawned, a big fake yawn, and she saw what it was covering: the stairs had cost him a breath, and he was paying it back where nobody could see. She looked at the ledger until he was finished breathing.

She had already known about the fall. The tofu man's mother had shouted it up the stairs at eleven, to nobody in particular. She let the boy tell it anyway.

---

Monk came in at twenty past four with his head on its second day away from the razor and sat down at fifteen without logging in. At four-thirty he turned his chair a quarter of the way toward the counter.

"Late," he said.

"Ten minutes," said Mei.

Monk looked at her for a second longer than he usually looked at anything. She lit a cigarette she didn't want and gave him the side of her face.

At four-forty the stairs came up three at a time. Lin Chen arrived damp at the hairline, and his school pants were dark at both knees where he had knelt in something. The smell reached the counter before he did: brine, garlic, the old woman's chili cabbage. That was new since this morning. When he had come up for his letter at nine, his knees had been dry.

He looked at the clock over the counter before he looked at anyone, and she watched him do the math. Ten minutes. He put coins on the glass. "That's what I've got on me. I'll bring the rest."

She swept them into her palm without counting. She would count them later, when he wasn't there to watch her not counting.

Big Bird pounded in behind him with two minutes of his father's dead hour already spent on the stairs. Two holiday boys from row two had paid for the right to be shouted at by him, and Lin Chen put the five of them against a team of strangers on Lianzhan. Fang Hu climbed the last three steps somewhere around the second creep wave. Mei chose not to see it. He took up his post behind Lin Chen's chair with his arms folded, drinks man, sir.

Machine fourteen sat in her line of sight from the counter. She had arranged the room that way years ago. She watched.

Lin Chen looked at the window twice and at the clock four times before the game was fifteen minutes old. He walked his hero into a stun she had seen him dodge with his eyes half closed. Big Bird howled at the monitor; one of the holiday boys said something about Lin Chen's mother, quietly, so it didn't count. They won in a little over forty minutes because Monk did not panic, and because the boys on the other side didn't have a Monk.

"Good," Monk said.

Lin Chen was already standing, pulling his jacket off the chair.

"Ge." Fang Hu was in front of him before the jacket was on. "Big Bird's dad makes beef soup, a giant pot, every day. I can carry it to your nainai. Every day after school. I know the bridge. I'm fast on the bridge."

"My dad'd give it," Big Bird said. "He likes old ladies. He likes them better than me."

"I won't spill. I'll hold it with both—"

"You're a kid," Lin Chen said. "Stay out of it."

He didn't say it unkindly. He said it the way you set a cup down out of an elbow's reach, and then he was zipping his jacket.

Fang Hu's grin didn't fall. It flattened. A grin with the lights still on and nobody home. "Okay," he said. "Sure." He went back to folding his arms.

Mei's ash grew a centimeter and fell on the ledger. She brushed it off and said nothing.

"Fourteen's yours at six," she said, when Lin Chen reached the stairs.

"Not today." He was already two steps down. "Eight. I'll be back at eight."

At six she wrote 14 — empty in the ledger, and underlined it, though nobody was going to audit her.

---

At a quarter to seven Big Bird came up the stairs with her bowl of beef noodles held out in front of him in both hands, very high, like an offering to a god who might be picky.

"Boss Mei." He set the bowl down and didn't let go of it. "Four days. Where do I go?"

"Home."

"Home is where my dad is."

"That's what home is."

"That's the whole problem with it." He was looking at the bowl. "Is all that for you?"

She pulled it across the glass by its rim. He watched it leave, then went back down the stairs slowly, the way he went when his father could hear him.

The rain started around seven, soft, the kind that made the alley smell like rust. Fang Hu went home to eat the money his father had left on the table and came back at seven-forty, shaking the wet out of his hair like a dog.

"Out."

"Ge's walking me later. I'm early for it." He sat on the bottom step, where the rain couldn't reach and her voice could.

At ten to eight the hood came up the stairs.

Deyang was exactly as much from Deyang as the fridge was. The girl kept her hood up, her shoulders square, and her voice down when she remembered, and she went straight to thirty-eight and looked at thirty-seven. Empty.

"Where is he?"

"On his way," Mei said.

"You know that?"

"I know everything in this room."

"He's not in this room."

"Then he's on his way."

Deyang sat. Mei turned a tile over on the counter and turned it back. She did not, in fact, know where he was. The boy had left at five-thirty with brine on his knees and a broom-shaped problem at home, and the rain was getting heavier. She found she didn't like not knowing. She lit another cigarette to have something to do with her hands.

At eight o'clock by the counter clock, which ran two minutes fast and had for years, Lin Chen came up the stairs with the rain on him. His sleeves were soaked to the elbow.

"Late," said Deyang, without looking up.

"Sorry."

Mei said nothing about the clock. He put two yuan on the glass, and she dropped them in the till.

At five past eight the stairs filled with Streak, Deng Wei and Liu Bo, Streak holding his phone up.

"We saw the sign," he said. "Downstairs."

"Then you can read."

"Is it real? Four days?"

"From tomorrow." She held out her palm. "Tonight, looking at the ghost is a yuan."

"We only came to look at the sign—"

"The sign's free. It's downstairs. You're upstairs."

They paid, one creased yuan each, smoothed flat on the counter. Last week a yuan apiece had gone to the class monitor's red envelope. Tonight it went in the till, and the class monitor wasn't there to see it go.

There wasn't much to look at. The girl had promised two razes and she kept two razes, mana held back like money in a shoe. In the first game he walked into the gap she'd left for him on purpose and died at three minutes. In the second he looked toward the window at four minutes, just once, quickly, toward the bridge and whatever was on the far side of it, and at five minutes he was dead again.

Streak opened his mouth. Mei looked at him, and he closed it.

Deyang took the headset off and hung it on her neck. She was quiet for a moment, studying the boy's profile instead of the scoreboard.

"Next week," she said. "Bring your whole head."

She went down the stairs with her hood up. On the way past the counter she gave Mei a nod, half a centimeter, the kind one cashier gives another.

Lin Chen stood up. He didn't groan. He wiped one wet sleeve down the back of the chair without noticing he'd done it, and he looked at nothing, and something in Mei moved before the rest of her had agreed to it.

"Lin Chen—"

He turned.

She had his name in her mouth and nothing behind it. Everything behind it was the kind of thing you didn't say over a till in front of Liu Bo. Somewhere in the second it took him to turn around, she had nothing safe left to hand him.

"—your sleeves," she said. "That's my chair. You're dripping on my chair, chopstick. Chairs rot."

He looked at the chair. "It's plastic."

"Plastic rots slower. It still rots."

He wiped the seat with the other sleeve, which made it wetter, and said, "Sorry, Boss Mei," in a voice too tired for fifteen. Then the boy from the bottom step was shouting "Ge! It's eight twenty-five!" up the stairwell, and they were gone.

---

The window by machine forty had a crack held together with masking tape. If you stood close to the tape, you could see down the main street to the rice-shop corner, where the shop's one bulb made a yellow circle on the wet road.

She stood close to the tape.

They came into the light at eight twenty-eight, the boy talking with both hands, the older one listening with his shoulders. Then the boy saw something across the street, a cousin or a cat or a mooncake box, and bolted for it.

A motorbike with no headlight came out of the rain.

Lin Chen's hand closed on his arm and yanked, hard, hard enough that the oversized jacket bunched up to the boy's ear and his feet left the road. The motorbike went by close enough to splash them both and was gone.

Mei's palm was flat on the glass. She didn't remember putting it there. The cigarette had burned down to her knuckles, and she shook it out over the floor she'd mopped that morning.

Down at the corner, Lin Chen let go and said something short. Fang Hu rubbed his arm. Then he turned, looked straight up the street at Starlight's sign, and waved his whole arm over his head at the neon, at the window, at nobody.

She stepped back from the glass.

---

At ten she switched off the machines one row at a time, and the holiday boys complained in two dialects.

"Four days? Where do we go?"

"Home. You've got one. Go look at it."

When the room was empty, she sat down at the counter with the cash tin on her left and the brown envelope on her right, still face down, still sealed. Between them she set a single mahjong tile from her cardigan pocket, the seven of dots. It had lived in that pocket since summer, worn smooth on one edge from her thumb. She turned it face up. After a while she turned it face down.

The culture bureau kept one window open through the holiday for people who owed it money. Tomorrow she would take the bank envelope there and stand in line with the other people who owed. That was tomorrow.

She sat with the three things on the glass for a long time and didn't explain any of them, not even to herself.

Then she pulled the plug on the neon. STARLIGHT buzzed and went out, the LIGHT half last, as usual. She went down the stairs in her slippers to the street door. The cardboard was still there, red marker on the back of an Uncle Kong box, CLOSED OCT 2–5, its edges going soft in the rain. She checked that it faced out. It did.

She left it, and went back up the stairs one at a time.
