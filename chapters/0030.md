# Chapter 30: Four Yuan, Maybe Five

Fang Hu made it nearly to the rice-shop corner before he asked.

He'd spent the first two blocks being the rune guy. He told me about the SEVEN, and about how he'd called it before anybody, and about how Big Bird had picked him up with the tray still in his hands and not one cup fell, or one fell but it was empty so it didn't count. Then he went quiet for half a block, and when Fang Hu goes quiet he isn't resting. He's loading.

"Ge. What were you counting?"

"When?"

"Before. In the game. When I went funny."

A man outside the hardware shop on the next street was hosing his piece of sidewalk, and the water ran past our shoes toward the gutter. I stepped over it. "Seconds," I said. "Like Su Wen."

"Su Wen counts on the desk." He walked backward a few steps so he could look at me. "You counted my hand."

I'd told myself afterward that it was the kind of thing anybody picks up. Everybody's seen it on television: two fingers on the inside of the wrist, eyes on a clock. My fingers had found the place before I'd decided anything. I'd let that go. The boy had been sitting there white as a bowl.

"Your hand was there," I said.

He thought about that. I could see him deciding whether it was an answer, and then deciding he didn't care, which at eleven is a decision you can make in the time it takes to step off a curb.

"Next week I'm going to call it at ELEVEN seconds," he said. "Not ten. Eleven. Because Typhoon's faster, so she needs, wait, no, she needs less, so nine. Ge, is it nine?"

"It's ten. It's always ten."

"For you it's ten."

At the corner he ran off without saying goodbye, the big jacket flapping around his knees, and stopped once halfway up his street to look back and check I was still standing there. I was. I stayed until a lamp came on in his window.

---

On Monday the bell went at four, and I was out the gate before Teacher Gao finished saying "Thursday." The midterms started Thursday. They could wait one afternoon.

Old Tan was waiting by his tire pump under Starlight's sign, wiping his hands on a rag that made them dirtier. "Do you need anything? Tools?"

"One thing. Wait."

I took the stairs two at a time. Boss Mei was at the counter with her tiles laid out face down and a cigarette parked on the ashtray's edge, and before I'd opened my mouth she slid a CD across the counter at me. It was a burned disc in a cracked case, SUNRISE ANTIVIRUS 2006 written across it in black marker. It lived on the shelf behind her, third from the left. I'd borrowed it a dozen times in my first life. This time I hadn't asked for it yet.

"For the Jiangs' boy," she said. "Don't let him put his—"

"The Pengs."

Her hand stopped on the case. One beat. "Wrong one." She took her hand back. "Pengs. Old Tan talks. Scratch it, you buy it."

I put it down to Old Tan, who told the whole street everything and got half of it wrong.

"I'll bring it back tonight."

"Before Monk's shift." She turned over a tile, looked at it, and turned it back.

The tax bureau's family compound was across the bridge, behind the county offices, four pale-yellow buildings around a yard full of bicycles and a cement Ping-Pong table with a brick for a net. I'd never been inside in either life. I knew it from the street: in 2022 the gatehouse was a phone-case stall and the wall carried a painted slogan about honest government that the rain had worn down to the word "honest." Now the gatehouse had a man in it with a radio playing opera, and Old Tan nodded at him in the same voice he used for radishes, and the man waved us through without turning the volume down.

"I fix his bicycle," Old Tan said on the stairs. "Mr. Peng's. Eight years. His bicycle never got a virus."

"The shop on West Street?"

"Wanted thirty and three days. Peng said to ask somebody's son." He stopped on the third-floor landing and knocked. "I said I knew a somebody."

Mrs. Peng opened the door in blue cloth oversleeves pulled up to her elbows, the kind women wore at the post office counter, and she kept them on all evening as if the computer might splash. She looked at Old Tan, then at me, then at my school tracksuit, and her face came to its conclusion.

"He's fifteen," she said.

"He fixed Mei Lan's machine," Old Tan said. "The dead one."

"Mei Lan's machines are all dead."

That was fair. She let us in anyway.

The computer lived in the corner of the main room on a desk with a sheet of glass over the top. Under the glass were photographs: a wedding, a boy in a red scarf, the same boy older at a waterfall. The monitor wore a white crocheted cover, the same as the television across the room. The boy himself, Peng Kai, thirteen and thin, sat on the sofa holding a schoolbook upside down.

"My husband is at the office," Mrs. Peng said. "He is always at the office. If his things are gone, it's me who hears about it."

"I'll tell you everything I do," I said. "Before I do it."

I pulled the cover off the monitor and pressed the power button. The machine took most of a minute to wake, grinding to itself, and when the desktop finally came up, three windows opened on top of it in a row, bright yellow, CONGRATULATIONS! YOU ARE A WINNER! in English. A fourth opened behind them. The clock in the corner said it was January 2002.

"That," Mrs. Peng said. "Every time. It talks."

"It's not talking. It's advertising." I closed them. Two more came. "Something on here opens them when the computer starts. First I'm going to look at the list of what starts."

I typed the command into the little run box without looking, my fingers finding the letters the way they'd found them in a hundred team houses and rented rooms, and I heard her shift behind my chair. I slowed down. A fifteen-year-old in 2006 who typed system commands blind was either a genius or a liar, and Mrs. Peng had already decided which kind of child she had in her apartment.

"Where did you learn this?" she asked.

"Magazines." I pointed at the screen, at a list of twenty-six lines, most of them strings of letters with no meaning. "These all start when it starts. These four are real. The sound, the printer, your husband's typing program. These" — I ran my finger down — "are junk. I'm going to switch them off, not delete them. If anything stops working, you can switch them back on."

The text was tiny, so I put two fingers on the glass and spread them apart to make it bigger. The monitor crackled with static under my fingertips and did nothing else.

"What are you doing?"

"Dust," I said, and wiped the screen with my sleeve.

I wrote the junk names on a slip torn from the back of my math exercise book, every one, in pencil, letter by letter, while she watched over my shoulder. Then I switched them off and restarted the computer. Fewer windows came up this time. She didn't say anything, but she stopped standing directly behind me and started standing a little to the side. That was progress.

The home page took longer. Somebody had replaced it with a page called SUPER TREASURE GRAND WEBSITE DIRECTORY, three hundred blinking links and a cartoon girl winking in the corner. I set it back to a blank page and showed her where the setting was, and she leaned in and read the words aloud slowly, as if she'd be tested.

Then the disc. Sunrise took forty minutes to crawl through the hard drive, a green bar creeping across a gray window, and there was nothing to do but sit there. Old Tan left after ten, to his bicycles. Mrs. Peng went to the kitchen and came back with tea in a glass jar and set it by my elbow without a word. Peng Kai turned his schoolbook the right way up.

The scan found thirty-one things. I let it clean them. I set the clock to the right year while I was at it.

"It's 2006," I said, when she looked.

"I know what year it is."

"Now it does too."

She almost laughed, and caught it, and asked to see her husband's table.

It was gone. She showed me where on the desktop the icon had been, upper right, next to the card game, and her voice went thin. "That's his work. That's three years of his work."

I didn't panic, because I knew where things went when this program took them, but I could feel the room watching me now, the woman and the boy and the wedding photograph under the glass. I opened the quarantine window. There it was, his file, with a little red mark beside it: a macro virus had ridden in on something and settled into the spreadsheet, and the antivirus had locked the whole thing away rather than argue with it.

"It's in jail, not gone," I said. "I'll get it out, and then it'll ask you something. Say no."

I restored it. I opened it with the macros off. A warning box asked its question and I clicked no and turned the screen toward her. Columns of numbers, dates down the side. I didn't read them. I scrolled to the bottom to show there was a bottom.

Mrs. Peng breathed out and pulled her oversleeves down to her wrists.

When she went to fetch her purse, Peng Kai came over to the desk. He didn't look at me. He took a disc out from under the desk and put it on the glass, face up: FIFTY GAMES SUPER COLLECTION, a robot with a sword on it, bought at the market for five yuan, the same kind I'd bought in 2004.

"Everything bad on that computer came off this," I said quietly. "Stop installing from it."

"Don't tell my dad."

He was thirteen, and frightened, and at that age I'd have asked the same thing in the same voice. I picked my words. "If he asks me, I won't lie. But I'm not coming back to tell him."

He thought about that the way Fang Hu had thought about the wrist. Then he took the disc back and put it in his schoolbag, not under the desk, which I counted as a beginning.

Mrs. Peng paid me four one-yuan notes, flattened under her palm on the glass. Then she took my slip of paper with the startup names, read it, and slid it under the glass next to the boy in the red scarf.

She put the crocheted cover back on the monitor herself. "Kai. Take him down to the gate."

Peng Kai went ahead of me on the stairs with one hand sliding along the rail. On the landing between the third floor and the second, where the window stood open on the bicycle yard and his mother couldn't hear, he stopped.

"Jiang Tao has the robot disc too," he said.

"The same one?"

"He bought it the same day. We went to the stall together." He was talking to the wall. "He put it in their computer too. Last Friday. Now it's slow and the windows come up by themselves, and it's worse than ours. His mom thinks it's broken. She's going to take it to the shop on Bridge Street, and they want twenty yuan."

"Does his father know it's the disc?"

"No." He turned around. "You need to help him. I told him you fixed ours."

"Where do they live?"

"Building two, fourth floor. His mom's home after six."

I looked at the open window and the bicycles below it. Jiang. I was back at Mei's counter with her hand stopped on the case. *For the Jiangs' boy.* Old Tan had told her about the Pengs. Old Tan had only ever said Peng.

It was a small compound. Every boy in it had probably bought something from the same market stall, and Mei could have heard about it anywhere. I let it go.

"Wednesday evening," I said. "Four yuan. I'll show him what to throw away."

"I'll tell his mom." Peng Kai let out a breath he must have been holding since the desk. "Thank you."

---

Xu Qing was standing at the bottom of Starlight's stairs at twenty to seven with the green notebook open and a pen already uncapped. I hadn't told her when I'd be back. She'd asked Old Tan.

I held out the four notes. She took them without looking at me, counted them, and counted them again.

"Client?"

"A family. Private."

She wrote, *Repair. 4.00 (client withheld)*, and I could tell she liked the phrase, because she underlined it. Then *Fund 42.60. Remaining 32.40.* My eyes went to the edge of the page, where the corner of the fresh one from last night showed underneath.

"Rule fourteen," Xu Qing said. "The manager's notes are the manager's." She closed the notebook. "You were going to ask."

"I wasn't."

"You were inhaling."

I ran the disc up to Mei. She was still at her tiles, and she didn't look up when I set it down by the ashtray, but she put her hand flat on it before I'd let go.

---

Grandma was at the folding table with the brace on over her pants and the cane hooked on the chair back, shelling peanuts into a bowl she didn't need them in. I told her about the compound and the clock that thought it was 2002 and the file in jail, and she listened without stopping her hands.

"Four yuan," I said.

"Did they pay before or after?"

I opened my mouth, and nothing was in it. I hadn't thought to ask. In my first life someone had always handled the money, a manager or a club or a man with a gold watch, and I'd sat at the machine and played.

"After."

"Hm." She put a peanut in her mouth.

"Next time before."

She pushed the bowl of shelled ones across to me, and I ate them, and at a quarter to nine I went back to Starlight to fetch Fang Hu. He came down the stairs on his own and talked about nine seconds the whole way to the rice shop.

That night I lay on my bed with my hands behind my head and counted. Four yuan was two hours at fourteen. One hour was half a repair. The Jiangs would be the same virus off the same disc, so call it four again, and if they paid before, the money would be in Xu Qing's box on Wednesday instead of Thursday, and I was trying to work out how many Wednesdays there were before the fifteenth when the numbers stopped lining up and I let them go.
