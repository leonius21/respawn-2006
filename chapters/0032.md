# Chapter 32: Night Shift

At ten Monk moved the stool to the top of the stairs and started taking cards.

The overnight crowd came up in the usual weeknight order. First the two brothers from the tile works, who paid separately and argued all the way up about whose turn it was to buy noodles. Then the bus depot boy with the cough. Then three he didn't know, who held their ID cards out before he asked. That meant they'd already been turned away somewhere else.

He held each card up beside its face and looked from one to the other. Eight yuan, a machine number, the card back. The third stranger's card showed a man with a mole beside his nose. The boy holding it had a clean face, a fresh haircut, and the careful breathing of somebody trying to be twenty-three.

Monk tapped the mole on the card. Then he looked at the boy's cheek, where there wasn't one.

The boy took his card back and went down without a word. His friends stood there a second longer and then followed him. Since the second of October nobody argued at the top of Starlight's stairs for long.

Behind the counter Mei turned a tile over, looked at it, and turned it back. The Sunrise disc was on the shelf behind her again, third from the left, pushed in exactly level with its neighbors. Lin Chen had been and gone before nine, then. Mei's shelf only looked that tidy after somebody nervous had put a thing back on it.

The back room light was off. The sofa under the Uncle Kong boxes had come with the building, according to Mei, and nobody had ever had a reason to carry it down the stairs. Ling was asleep on it with her shoes on and her school jacket over her legs, a math workbook open on her chest. One braid was tighter than the other. He'd done them at six with the second elastic in his teeth, and the left one always came out tighter because he started with it.

He took the pencil out of her hand and closed the workbook on it.

When he came back out, Mei was looking at the back room door. She looked long enough to take one drag, then wrote something in the ledger that wasn't about the door, as she had on every night since August when there was nobody at home for Ling to sleep beside.

---

At midnight he mopped.

He lifted the mop off its hook in the back room without letting the handle touch the wall, filled the bucket at the bathroom faucet, and went from the back wall toward the stairs, as always, so the last wet patch was the one he'd stand on. Sunflower shells under 22. Something orange and sticky under 9 that had been a Coco Cola once. The tile brothers lifted their feet for him without looking away from their screens.

Then the fridge. He carried the drinks out of the back room four at a time: Coco Cola on the left, Red Ox on the right, the noodles stacked on top where the cold didn't reach anyway. The ham sausage box had been opened from the bottom. Three were gone.

He held the box up toward the counter.

"Zhao," Mei said, without looking. "Written."

He put the box back.

At half past twelve she slid a brown envelope across the counter. One character on the front in her handwriting, his surname, nothing else.

"Week."

He put it inside his jacket. Counting Mei's money in front of Mei was something people only did once.

Later, when the room had gone to the low clicking of men who'd stopped talking, he sat on the back room floor with his back against the sofa and the door half open, so a stripe of light came in and lay across his knees. Ling breathed behind his head. He took the tin out of his bag. It had held tea once; the paint on the lid was worn down to the metal where his thumb went. He emptied the envelope into it and counted the whole tin once.

Then he counted out a ten and a five and laid them on his knee.

Seventy-five, five names. He'd seen Xu Qing's notebook upside down on the counter the week the list was made, her square handwriting, his name with two words after it in brackets. On shift. Pending. She wrote the way she talked. He'd read the line twice and turned the notebook back the right way up.

In his left shoe, under the insole, the folded paper pressed on the ball of his foot. He didn't take it out. Window three. Monday, Wednesday, Friday, seven-thirty to eleven. A number in blue ballpoint that the clerk had underlined twice. He'd stopped unfolding it in September. It was there so he'd feel it when he walked.

Fifteen yuan didn't move the number much. It moved the line in the green notebook all the way.

He put the ten and the five back in the tin, on top, where his fingers would find them first, and closed the lid.

---

The man came up at twenty past one, one step at a time, both hands on the rail. Gray work-unit jacket with a pen still clipped to the pocket. He smelled of baijiu and of the stairwell, and he put eight yuan on the stool with great care.

"Overnight. Just a chair. I'm not playing. I'm sitting."

"Card," Monk said.

The man patted his jacket all over, found something, and held it out with dignity. It was a red plastic tag on an elastic loop, number 31, from the bathhouse on River Road.

Monk looked at the tag. Then at him.

"It's got my number on it," the man said.

"Card, or stairs."

The man straightened. "I'm forty-six years old." He said it to the room, not to Monk. "I've got a son older than you. I was in this town before this building, and some bald boy wants—"

Behind Monk the sofa springs went. A workbook hit the floor.

He didn't turn around. He kept looking at the man, not blinking much, the same flat way he watched the map when nothing had happened yet but something was going to. The man's voice ran out of air halfway through the next word.

Mei put a tile down on the counter, hard. "Lao Pan."

"Mei Lan, I'm only—"

"You're a xianggong tonight. Wrong number of tiles. You can sit there till dawn and never win a hand. Go home."

Lao Pan looked at her, then at Monk, then at the bathhouse tag in his hand. He took his eight yuan back off the stool, counted it in his palm, and went down holding the rail with both hands, still telling the stairwell about his son.

"Mop where he stood," Mei said.

Ling was in the back room doorway with her jacket zipped and the workbook under her arm, ready to go anywhere.

"Is it time?"

"No."

"He was loud."

"He's gone."

"Did he hit you?"

"No."

She looked past him at Mei. Mei was looking at her ledger. Ling went back and lay down facing the boxes, and said into them, "Wake me up properly. Not with your foot."

He had never once woken her with his foot. She said it every time, in case.

He mopped where Lao Pan had stood. While the floor dried he leaned on the mop handle and watched her shoulders until they stopped moving.

Back in September, at this counter, with Ling's bike chain spread on a newspaper between them, Lin Chen had said her name. Ling, just like that. Monk had never said it in this building, and Mei didn't talk about anybody to anybody. Monk had asked him how he knew. A pink bell, a rabbit, every girl in the county called Ling-something: an answer that sounded like one. He'd turned it over for about the length of one creep wave and let it go. In a town this size, people knew things. He didn't ask again.

---

At two Mei took her thermos and went upstairs. By three the room was asleep in its chairs. One of the tile brothers had his head on his arms beside a cup of noodles gone stiff. The bus depot boy's hero stood in its fountain with nobody steering it, and the game went on around him.

Xu Qing had taped a sheet inside the counter, below the edge where customers couldn't see. THUNDER NET CITY. SUNDAY OCTOBER 15. ARRIVE 9:00 (BY MY WATCH). FEE 75 CASH. Five names under it. Beside his own, in pencil, the bracket.

He read it the way he read the map before a fight: not who was strong, but where each one would be standing when it came.

Big Bird at Thunder's door at half past eight, filling it, already hungry. Su Wen wherever his father thought he wasn't, counting thirty under his breath and tapping on the table at the top. Typhoon against the wall with her hood up, either very early or exactly on time, nothing in between. The kid on whatever stool somebody gave him, calling runes ten seconds early and too loud. Lin Chen in the middle chair with a mouse that wasn't his, his hands a beat behind his head.

And himself, off Saturday's shift, eyes half shut. They were always half shut. Nobody would know the difference.

Then four-thirty, back here. He'd told Streak same time. Saturday night on Mei's stool, Thunder in the morning, Streak in the afternoon.

He looked at the pencil bracket for a while. Then he went and checked the bathroom faucet. It dripped if you didn't lean on it.

---

He didn't hear Mei come down. At half past five she was just at the counter again in her cardigan, counting the night's money into the drawer, a fresh cigarette not yet lit.

"Go," she said.

Ling was already sitting up with her shoes on, because she'd slept in them. He knelt and did her braids again, left one first. Then he opened the tin, took the ten and the five off the top, and folded them small. He unlaced his right shoe, pushed the money down into the toe of his sock, and laced the shoe back up. The paper in the left one. The money in the right.

Ling watched him do it and said nothing, which was new.

They went out the green door into the alley. The bicycle was chained to the drainpipe where he'd left it, wet with the night. Ling dug in her jacket pocket and brought out the bell. Bells didn't last a night in that alley, so it lived with her: the pink plastic one off her own bike, its rabbit sticker peeled down to one ear by her thumb. She screwed it onto the handlebar herself, tongue between her teeth, and rang it twice. It was very loud between the two walls. Somewhere above them a window banged shut.

"Okay," she said, and climbed onto the rack.

He didn't ride. He walked the bike out of the alley and into the market, where the first stalls were coming up out of the dark. The bun man was lifting the lid off his steamer. A woman was hosing down the fish slabs, and the water ran across the lane in a thin sheet he wheeled straight through. The pickle stall's plank cabinet was still padlocked. Ling held on to the back of his jacket with one fist and leaned her forehead against him, not asleep yet but trying.

At the end of the market, past the shuttered stationery shop, the school road went left and the hospital road went straight on. A tricycle stacked with cabbages came up out of the side street, and he stopped to let it pass. His right foot was aware of the toe of its sock.

He reached past Ling's knee and rang the bell once. Then he walked on.
