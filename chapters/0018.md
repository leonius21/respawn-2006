# Chapter 18: One Try a Week

Boss Mei heard me before she saw me.

"That's not ninety," she said, eyes on her tiles. "That's more. Did you rob the tofu man?"

"I carried for him."

Four-thirty that morning, in the dark, eleven crates from his tricycle to the market. His deaf mother had shouted encouragement at the wrong crates the whole time. He'd paid me two yuan again, in two damp notes that still smelled of soybeans when I flattened them on the counter. With what I'd had, that made two ninety. On Starlight's stairs, two ninety sounds like inheritance.

Mei swept the notes into the drawer. "Hour starts now. Deyang's already in the corner."

The clock over the fridge said 7:58. I was not late. I had never been so determined to be not late in my life.

It had been a long Sunday. The call at noon had been short and mostly about rain in Dongguan. My father said "Study" twice and hung up before Grandma could get to radishes. At four-thirty we'd beaten Streak's group again in the back room. It took forty-odd minutes this time instead of thirty-one, because Streak had stopped walking to every rune. Somebody had explained it to him. Opponents learn. It's the most inconsiderate thing about them.

Then I'd walked Fang Hu to the corner at ten to seven. At the curb by the rice shop he'd stopped, put one hand on the lamppost, and stepped down very slowly, both feet, knees bent, and looked up at me.

"Like you, Ge. Like a grandpa."

"Did you finish your essay?"

"It's finished. It's just short." He'd hitched up the jacket. "Dad's home tonight. He's making noodles. He boils them till they give up." Then the run, the turn, the whole arm over his head. I'd waved back on purpose this time.

Home, rice, Grandma's single word as I put my shoes on again: "Nine." That was the whole conversation, and we both knew the terms.

---

Machine 38 lives in Starlight's back corner, by the window that doesn't shut and lets in the smell of the river. Xiaoyu sat with the gray hood up and her thumbs through the holes she'd cut in the cuffs, a big pair of cafe headphones around her neck.

Next to her, at 36, on a chair she had clearly wiped first, sat Xu Qing.

"Why are you here?"

"Observing the conditions of recruitment." She didn't look away from 38's screen. "Typhoon is on the list in pencil. Tonight decides whether the pencil becomes ink or eraser."

"Your parents think you're where?"

"My mother believes I am buying a ruler." She lifted a ruler, still in its plastic sleeve. "I have bought a ruler."

Xiaoyu spoke without turning, in the low Deyang voice she kept for public. "Sit, thirty-four. Soul Fiend mirror. Mid only. First kill takes it. Best of three."

"Same as last time."

"Last time took eleven minutes." The hood tilted a degree. "Try for twelve."

I sat at 37. The keyboard had a crust on the space bar that I'd been meaning to clean for a week.

Two weeks ago I'd tried to beat her by being her: raze for raze, hand against hand. Fifteen-year-old fingers against He Xiaoyu's fingers is a car crash with only one car. But I'd also watched her for two years in another life. One thing about Typhoon never changed. She razed the instant you stepped into range, and she never ran dry, because nobody had ever made her.

Soul Fiend's three razes blast the ground at three fixed distances: close, middle, far. Three rings around him. Between the rings there are gaps. Standing still in a gap doesn't take fast hands. It takes knowing where the gap is.

Game one loaded. I walked my Soul Fiend to the middle of the lane and stood there.

---

They drifted in from the window row a minute later: Streak, Deng Wei, Liu Bo, and Deng Wei's cousin. The ghost Typhoon never played anyone face to face, and a crowd gathers when a ghost sits still. They arranged themselves behind our chairs and breathed on us.

Mei arrived in her slippers before the first creep wave met.

"Standing's a yuan."

Streak turned. "We're not on a machine."

"You're on my floor, looking at my ghost. Looking at the ghost is a yuan."

"Who's he even playing?"

"Some chopstick." She held out her palm. Three coins went into it, and then Liu Bo counted jiao out of his pocket one at a time while she waited, perfectly still, like a toll gate.

The hood turned toward her. "You're charging them," Xiaoyu said, the Deyang voice sliding up half a note, "to look at me?"

"Renting you. Selling costs more."

Somewhere sixteen years ahead, people would get very rich charging strangers to watch other people play games. Mei had just invented it with her slipper and a cardigan pocket, and neither of us would see a fen of the future version.

On the screen, Xiaoyu razed. Her middle ring blew up the grass a hero's width in front of me. I didn't move. She razed again; the near ring scorched the ground at my back.

"He's not even dodging," Deng Wei said.

"He doesn't need to," Streak said slowly. "Look where he is."

I was counting. Each raze takes a real bite of mana at these levels, and a Soul Fiend at level two can afford only a few bites before the bowl is empty. One. Two. I stepped half a step to a new gap and let her see me do it. Three. Four. Her blue bar sagged to almost nothing.

I walked in.

My fingers had the plan exactly: near raze, point-blank, then the rest. They hit the middle key instead. My raze blew a crater in the grass behind her, where she wasn't and had never been.

She turned around. She'd kept one, of course. One raze held back, always, the coin a careful kid sews inside her jacket. Her near ring went off right under my feet. I'd been standing in my gaps while she last-hit, so she had more gold and a level on me; her plain attacks did the rest. My Soul Fiend lay down in the lane.

Deng Wei groaned on my behalf, a long sound with a tune in it.

"That's free," Mei said from the aisle. "I only charge for looking."

---

She keeps one. I sat back while game two loaded and turned it over. Then what I needed was to be somewhere her last raze couldn't reach in time.

Game two, I didn't stand in the gaps at all. I drank. I walked into her razes and soaked them, and I used up my Flask in the first two minutes as if I didn't know what it was for. Meanwhile I held my creeps back with denies and bad last-hits. The two waves drifted toward her side until they were meeting right under her tower, ten seconds before the two-minute rune.

Her choice: walk to the river for the rune and let her tower eat a whole wave of gold, or stay. Streak would have gone. Xiaoyu has never been Streak in her life. She stayed and last-hit under her tower.

I walked to the river. The rune was Haste. It didn't matter which one it was. Taking any rune refills your Flask, and mine was empty because I'd emptied it.

I drank my way back to full and came at her red-hot fast. She'd seen me go and she was ready, holding her last raze. But a hero with Haste crosses the middle ring before a finger gets there. Her raze landed behind me.

Near raze. Right key this time. It landed, and her health dropped by a third.

She ran for her tower, straight, because the tower is where everybody runs. My middle raze caught her shoulder. For the far one I didn't aim at her at all. I aimed at the foot of her tower, where she had to arrive.

She arrived at the same time it did.

Behind me Streak said, "Oh," very quietly.

"Who's the chopstick?" Liu Bo asked.

Xiaoyu didn't say anything. Her right hand came off the mouse, flexed once, and went back.

Mei came down the aisle and dropped four coins on the desk in front of Xu Qing, one at a time.

"Sponsorship. Write it down before I remember who I am."

Xu Qing's pen was already moving. I leaned over. In the green notebook, under a heading she had ruled last week and never used, she wrote: *Income: spectators, 4.00. Sponsor: Mei Lan.* Then: *Remaining: 63.40 (pending).* She underlined the sixty-three twice. It was the first line in that column that hadn't come out of a child's pocket, and she knew it.

"Put me down for a cut," Xiaoyu said.

"You are in pencil," Xu Qing said. "Pencil does not receive income."

The hood went very still. Xu Qing's eyes moved, just once, to the frayed thumbholes in the gray cuffs, and stayed there a second longer than they needed to. She wrote one more word I couldn't see, closed the notebook, and checked her cracked watch.

"Eight-nineteen. A ruler stops being believable at eight-twenty." She stood and tucked the ruler under her arm. "One game all. I'll hear the result on Saturday. Accurately."

---

Game three, Xiaoyu held her razes.

For two whole minutes, not one. She stood in my gaps. She didn't just stand in them; she found them faster than I'd found them, a half-step here, a half-step there, so that every ring I threw blew up the dirt beside her. She held my wave at my tower at a minute fifty, the same trap turned around, and went to the rune herself. Then she came back full and walked in at me a half-step at a time, and I understood that the gaps I'd been living in were hers now.

Then she spent the first one, the one she'd kept for the kill. I saw the near raze coming. My head told my hand to step back. My hand did it a quarter-second after it was asked.

A quarter-second, in this game, is a country.

The lane went gray. The text came up in red. Behind me Deng Wei explained to the cousin how he would have won. Streak said, "Typhoon two, chopstick one," to nobody, and they drifted back to the window row as if nothing had happened, which for them it hadn't.

Xiaoyu pulled the headphones down. When she spoke it was low and fast, in her own voice, just for my side of the desk.

"You counted my mana."

"Somebody had to. You weren't."

"I am now." She stood. The hood didn't move, but something under it did. "You played like you'd watched me for a year."

"I watch everybody. It's cheap."

"Nothing about you is cheap. That cost me two razes and a rune." She pushed the chair in with her knee. "Next week I keep two."

"Then I'll count to two."

She went down the aisle. At the counter she stopped. "I want my yuan."

"Which yuan?" Mei said.

"The ones they paid to look at me."

"Sponsor's."

"Then the sponsor can look at me for free." The hood didn't wait for an answer. The stairs took her down.

Mei watched her go. Then she looked at me. Then she turned a tile over, face down, and left it there.

The clock over the fridge said 8:24. I'd paid for an hour and used twenty-six minutes of it.

"You've got thirty-four minutes left, chopstick," Mei said. "Of course it's thirty-four."

"Keep them."

"I always do."

At the counter, Monk was rinsing Mei's bowl from seven o'clock, the one Big Bird had carried up hours ago. He didn't look up.

"Close," he said.

"Lost."

"Close."

I didn't argue with him. I took the stairs at 8:25, two at a time, with ninety fen and a whole country's worth of quarter-second in my right hand.

The main street had gone quiet in the Sunday way: shutters down, a motorbike somewhere, steam from the skewer cart by the bus stop. The rice shop was dark. Its lamppost made an orange circle on the sidewalk, the same circle I stood in every evening to watch Fang Hu turn.

Somebody was standing in it.

Short haircut. Stiff back. No uniform: a plain gray zip-up jacket, cheap pants, the kind of shoes a man buys because they last. His hands were behind his back. He was facing the stairs I'd just come down. I didn't know how long he'd been waiting.

Officer Fang Jianguo watched me reach the bottom step. I had never told him my name.

"Lin Chen," he said. "Walk with me."
