# Chapter 29: Two Razes

I sat down at thirty-seven and didn't turn it on either. Two dark screens side by side, at six on a Sunday with every other seat taken, got us a look from the counter. Then Mei went back to her tiles.

"You're early," I said.

"You're slow." Xiaoyu kept her eyes on the black monitor. "I passed you on the street. Your little one walks like my grandpa."

"He stood up too fast."

It came out in Fang Hu's words before I could find my own. She turned her head about a centimeter, decided it wasn't her business, and turned it back.

"Why two hours?"

"Machine's free when it's off." Her hood moved in a shrug. "Ten a week. I'm not giving Mei two of it to watch you lose."

That answered how, not why. I let it go. In my first life I'd sat near her at Starlight for two years and never once asked where she went home to at night, and starting now, with a question she could see coming from across the room, felt late.

So we sat. The holiday crowd shouted across the rows, somebody's Fury rune got wasted on a creep, and somebody two rows over was eating sunflower seeds and spitting the shells into a cup. Her right hand lay on her knee. Every so often the fingers moved: three taps, a pause, one more, then a little slide of the thumb.

Short raze, middle, long. Walk. Raze again.

It was my rhythm. I'd never taught it to her. She had taken it the same way she'd taken the path to the rune through the gap in the trees above the river, the one I'd used in every duel since September. She was good enough that she needed neither of them. She copied them anyway, faster than I could do them, and beat me with them.

Somewhere around seven I stopped being annoyed about that and started being interested.

---

Big Bird came up the stairs at ten to eight carrying an aluminum lunch box in both hands.

"BEEF," he announced to the room. "FOR LUCK. DAD SAW ME TAKE IT."

"What did he say?"

"Nothing. He watched." Big Bird set the box on the desk between us with great care. "So it's allowed."

Su Wen came in behind him, sat on the end of the row and said, "My father is at a banquet. Retirement. Teacher Ma." That covered him until about nine-thirty, if Teacher Ma's colleagues gave speeches the way most teachers did.

Xu Qing arrived at eight exactly by the counter clock, sat on Big Bird's other side, opened her green notebook to the player list and laid two pens beside it, one ink and one pencil, perfectly level. Nobody asked about the pens.

Monk was on shift. He did a round of the room with the mop and stopped behind my chair and stayed there.

Fang Hu was the last one up the stairs, and the slowest.

"It's dark," Mei said, without looking up. "Children go home in the dark."

"Dad's on nights. The TV's broken." He was breathing through his mouth from the stairs. "You can't sleep in a house with a broken TV, everybody knows, it's too—"

Twenty-nine. My thumb found the spot on my own wrist before I'd decided anything. Go home was already in my mouth. Then I thought of the apartment past the rice shop, his father's chair, a TV that didn't work, and a boy lying on his bed listening to his own heart in the quiet. I didn't say it.

"Stool." Mei pointed with her cigarette at a spot by the end of our row where the counter had a clear view. "There. Nine o'clock you're on the street."

"I'll walk him," I said.

"Then his stool's on you. Two hours, a yuan each." She wrote. "Three twenty."

I turned on thirty-seven. Xiaoyu turned on thirty-eight. When she spoke again, for the room, her voice had dropped half an octave.

"Same as always. Three games."

"Three games."

Soul Fiend against Soul Fiend, middle lane, first kill takes it. Behind me, Su Wen started tapping the desk on the thirty-second beat.

---

The first game went the way the first four had.

She was faster on every last hit, and she knew it, and she let me see her know it. I was better than I'd been two weeks ago. Machine 14 had done that, and Su Wen's drills where he counted out loud while I tried to hit a creep on a number he hadn't said yet. I missed fewer. I still missed.

At a minute fifty she left the lane, ten seconds before the rune. I knew where she was going, because it was where I would have gone. I went too. She came out of the gap in the trees four seconds ahead of me, took the rune at two minutes exactly, and it was Fury. Fury doubles every hit for a while and leaves you standing very still afterward if you were on the other end of it. She turned around. Her middle raze landed on me. I swung back with mine, late by a hair. Her short one finished it.

Two razes. She hadn't needed the third.

"Again," Xiaoyu said.

Big Bird had eaten half the beef. "I'M HOLDING IT FOR YOU," he explained, with his mouth full.

Xu Qing moved the pencil one centimeter closer to the notebook.

"Eleven seconds," Su Wen said quietly.

"What?"

"From her lane to the rune. Eleven. You take fifteen. Both through the trees." He pushed his glasses up. "Same way."

Same way. Every time. If you know the road a person takes, you don't need to be faster than her on it. You only need to be standing at the end of it.

---

In the second game I played worse on purpose, and it wasn't hard.

I gave her a few last hits. I got caught by a raze I could have dodged and backed off to heal with my Flask, slowly, where she could see it. At three forty-five I was behind and short of health and she'd have expected me to want the four-minute rune badly enough to take a stupid road to it.

At three fifty I left the lane, walking toward the trees.

On her screen it would have looked like me going for the gap. On mine it was me stopping a few steps short of where the gap let out, turning my hero to face it, and waiting with my hand on the keys. On a fifteen-year-old hand that is a lot harder than it sounds. Fifteen seconds to walk the path for me. Eleven for her. She'd see me leave, she'd count my fifteen, she'd go, she'd be first.

I counted eleven from when I'd stepped into the trees. At nine I pressed the middle raze.

She walked out of the trees into it. It blasted the ground under her and her health dropped by a third, and for half a second she stood there, the way anyone stands when the floor they'd planned on isn't there. I put the long raze down behind her, where she'd run. She didn't run there. She came at me instead, which was right, and her short raze hit me, and mine hit her, and she had a little more health than I did and less time to use it. My creep wave had come up the lane while we were busy. It had been waiting for her, too.

The kill notice came up on both screens.

Big Bird stood up and the lunch box went over. Beef everywhere. "HE DID IT. HE DID— I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Boss Mei."

"You'll lick it," Mei said, from the counter.

Xiaoyu didn't take her hands off the keyboard. "That was my path."

"It was mine first."

"I'd have done it faster."

"You'd have had to think of it."

She looked at me, actually looked, and something went across her face that she didn't let get as far as her mouth.

Xu Qing set the pencil back level with the ink pen.

---

While Big Bird picked beef off the floor with his fingers, Fang Hu got up off his stool.

I noticed because he did it slowly. He went behind the counter, where Mei let him go, and came back with the tin tray Mei used for carrying noodle cups. On it were four paper cups of water. He walked it down the row. He offered it to Monk, who had his hands on the mop. He tried Su Wen, who was counting. He even tried the fridge. Then he stood next to my chair with the tray held at the height of a waiter's, offering it to the air above thirty-eight.

I didn't need anybody to tell me what he'd just worked out. If I won the next one, there'd be five names on the list in Xu Qing's notebook, and none of them would be his.

"Hu Zi. Put that down."

He put it down on the desk. One cup slopped.

"Look at the game clock. Not my screen, the clock up top." I pointed. "Runes come every two minutes. Ten seconds before each one, you call it. Loud. Out loud for the whole row."

"Why me?"

"Because I need my eyes."

"Why not Su Wen, he's the counter—"

"Su Wen's counting something else."

Su Wen tapped the desk. Thirty seconds.

"She'll hear it too," Xiaoyu said.

"Then it's fair."

Fang Hu looked at the clock on my screen, then at the clock on hers. They said the same thing. Then he got back on his stool, on his knees, and gave me a Young Pioneer salute so fast he nearly hit himself in the eye.

His color was better than at five. I checked without letting him see me check.

---

The third game she didn't touch the trees.

She played the lane straight and hard, and her hands showed me exactly how far mine still had to go. By minute three I was down a whole raze's worth of health and two items behind. She didn't need tricks against me. She'd only used mine because they were there.

"RUNE IN TEN," Fang Hu yelled at a minute fifty, and half the room turned around. At three fifty he yelled it again. Neither of us went. Going meant leaving the lane to the other one.

What I could still do was count, and Su Wen had spent two weeks making me count. I took my last hits late, so my creeps lived longer and hers died sooner, and slowly, wave by wave, the place where the two lines of soldiers met in the middle crept away from my tower and down toward the river. It cost me gold. It cost me a little health every time she razed me for standing where I stood. She took the gold and the health and didn't ask what I was buying with them.

At five fifty I was at a fifth of my health and she was at a little under half.

"RUNE IN TEN! TEN! NINE—" Fang Hu was counting down without being asked. "SEVEN—Ge, it's SEVEN—"

She went first. Her hands were faster; she was always going to get there first. She stepped into the river at six exactly, took the rune, Haste, and turned on me with her whole speed, and two razes came down, short and middle. My health bar went red, then thin, then a red thread.

She was standing in my creeps.

They'd been walking down to the river for three minutes. Five of them, all mine, all alive. They turned on the nearest enemy. A creep doesn't hit hard. Five of them, with nothing else to do, hit hard enough. Her health dropped in little bites, and I pressed the only raze I had left, the long one, with a hand that was shaking enough that I felt it in my wrist, and it landed where she was and not, for once, where she had been.

The kill notice came up.

My hero was still standing in the river with a sliver of red that you'd have needed Su Wen to measure.

For a second nobody in the row said anything. Then Big Bird made a noise that wasn't a word and grabbed me by both shoulders and shook me, and Fang Hu was yelling "I CALLED IT, I CALLED IT, SEVEN," and somebody in the next row asked what had happened and nobody answered him.

Monk, behind me, nodded once.

---

Xiaoyu took off her headset and hung it on the monitor. She pushed her chair back and stood up, and for a moment she just looked at her screen, at her hero lying in my river.

"Typhoon's in," she said, low.

Then she put her hand in her jacket pocket and took out a five-yuan note, folded in four, and unfolded it, and laid it on Xu Qing's notebook.

"Deposit."

Ten a week, she'd said. I watched her let go of half of it and didn't say anything. She wouldn't have wanted me to.

Xu Qing held the note up to the light from the monitor and checked the narcissus in the corner. Then she set it down, uncapped the ink pen, and said, "Name. For the form."

"Typhoon."

Xu Qing looked at the hood for one second longer than she needed to. Then she wrote it in ink on the fifth line, under Monk, and under it, smaller: Deposit 5.00. Fund 38.60. Remaining 36.40.

"FIVE," Big Bird roared, holding up a hand with most of a fork's worth of beef still stuck to it. "WE'RE FIVE."

"Six," Fang Hu said. "With the rune guy."

Nobody corrected him. Big Bird picked him up off the stool, tray and all, and Mei shouted "Water on my floor and beef on my floor," and Su Wen tapped the desk once more out of habit and then stopped.

Xu Qing capped the ink pen. She turned the page of the green notebook to a clean one, uncapped the pen again, and wrote a single line, her arm curled around it so that from where I sat I could see nothing but the top of the characters.

Then she looked up, and her eyes stayed on me a second too long, before she closed the notebook.
