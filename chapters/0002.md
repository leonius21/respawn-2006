# Chapter 2: Forty Old Machines

Starlight's stairs started in the gloom beside the hardware shop, between a coil of green garden hose and a rack of padlocks. They smelled exactly the way I remembered: machine oil at the bottom, cigarettes at the top, and in the middle a thin layer of Uncle Kong beef noodles, as if the building had strata.

The hardware man didn't look up from his abacus. Nobody in Qingshui ever looked up at a boy going up those stairs. That was the town's whole policy on Starlight: don't see it, then complain about it over dinner.

Halfway up I grabbed the rail out of habit, then let go, because I didn't need it.

The door at the top had a bead curtain and a paper sign in red marker: MINORS FORBIDDEN BY LAW. Under it, in smaller blue marker: NO CREDIT. NO CRYING.

Inside, forty CRTs hummed in five rows, curtains drawn against the morning, every screen throwing the same blue-green light onto the faces in front of it. The air conditioner over the door had a towel stuffed in its mouth and a sign reading BROKEN DON'T TOUCH, to which someone had added, in a different pen, MEANS YOU, LITTLE FATTY. The overnight crowd was still slumped in the back rows, boys with gray faces and towers of empty noodle cups, still playing as if eight in the morning was a rumor somebody had started.

At a folding table by the counter, Boss Mei was losing at mahjong.

She was younger than my memory of her. That was fair, since my memory had kept aging her for sixteen years without asking. Wiry, with a perm that had grown out into a frizz she'd pinned up with a ballpoint pen. A thin brown cardigan with the sleeves shoved past her elbows in the heat, pink plastic slippers, and a cigarette parked in the corner of her mouth like a toothpick. Her skin was pale and a little sallow, the color you get from living under fluorescent tubes. Across from her sat two aunties from the dry-goods shop and an old man in a string vest, all three of them winning.

She looked at the door when the beads clicked behind me. Then she didn't play her tile. She held it, a white dragon between two fingers, for a second longer than it should take anyone to throw away a tile.

"Mei!" one of the aunties said. "Play, or are you laying an egg?"

She played it. "Two yuan an hour," she told me, without taking her eyes off the table. "ID."

I'd prepared for this all the way down the main street. I put four yuan on the counter, opened my mouth, and what came out was: "Can't I just scan my face?"

The old man in the vest turned all the way around in his chair.

Boss Mei took the cigarette out of her mouth. "You can scan my slipper."

"On the... copier," I said. "Some places in Chengdu have a copier. For faces."

"Then go to Chengdu."

Fine. Plan B, which should have been plan A. "Put me on Wang Jianjun."

That got her to look at me properly.

In my first life, Wang Jianjun, or Lao Wang ("Old Wang") as the whole town called him, was a laid-off brick-factory worker who had once, in a moment of weakness and a bottle of baijiu, let Boss Mei copy down his ID number. According to Starlight's register, he had since spent eleven hours a day in her cafe, every day, for years. Every minor in Qingshui gamed as Wang Jianjun. I'd been him myself from fifteen to seventeen. When the culture bureau inspected, Lao Wang was apparently the most dedicated gamer in Sichuan, and also, somehow, in the back room.

"Who told you that name?" Boss Mei asked.

"Everybody knows Lao Wang."

"Lao Wang is forty-six and has a bad lung."

"He's very committed."

The dry-goods aunty laughed so hard she knocked over her tea. Boss Mei didn't laugh. She wrote something in her ledger, swept my four yuan into the drawer, and then took something else out of it and slid it across the counter. It was a little card, red and soft at the corners, stamped with a smeared star: ONE HOUR FREE.

"You came," she said. "Eat something first."

"I ate. Two eggs."

"Then eat a third. You look like a chopstick." She was already turning back to her tiles. "Twenty-seven. By the wall."

I put the card in my pocket and didn't think about it. Cafe owners gave out free hours to hook kids. I'd watched her do it to a hundred boys. The odd part was the tone, flat and sure, like a bus conductor calling the stop you were already standing up for. Then again, I'd been dead that morning, and everything sounded odd.

Machine twenty-seven had a cigarette burn on the mouse pad and a keyboard whose letters had mostly worn off. I sat down and made a noise. It was the full noise, the one that starts in the lower back and ends somewhere near the soul, and the boy at twenty-six took his headset off to stare at me.

"Long week," I said.

He put the headset back on.

The Lianzhan login screen asked for a username. My fingers typed it before I'd decided anything: *Second*.

I sat looking at it. In 2015 that word had stopped being a name. It had become a verb, a forum joke, a picture of me with my face in my hands that people posted under anybody's mistake. *He pulled a Second.* For seven years I'd played under other names, worse ones, on worse teams.

But it had been mine before it was theirs. I pressed Enter.

New account, bottom rank. The room was called *Qingshui 1-100 No Feeders*, which was optimistic. Ten local kids, half of them probably within forty meters of me. I picked Soul Fiend, the demon who blasts the ground at three fixed distances and grows stronger from every soul he takes. My hero. The one I'd played on the biggest stage in the world.

"Soul Fiend?" said a voice right by my ear. "Nobody picks Soul Fiend. He's too hard. You're going to die."

I turned around. A kid was hanging off the back of my chair like laundry. Eleven, maybe, small for it, wearing a man's denim jacket with the sleeves rolled four times and still swallowing his hands. Shorts. Both knees scabbed in different stages, like a history of falling down. A brown summer tan, a Young Pioneer's red scarf stuffed into his pocket instead of round his neck, and a grin with a gap in it where a front tooth was still deciding whether to grow back.

"Who are you?"

"Fang Hu. Everybody calls me Hu Zi. Little Tiger." He said it like I should have heard of him. Maybe I had. There was some old shape in my memory of a noisy little kid who lived in this cafe like a mouse lives in a wall. "Who are you? You're old. Are you from the senior school?"

"I'm fifteen."

"You sound like my teacher. Who's mid against you? Oh, it's Streak." He pointed two rows over at a boy with a bleached stripe in his bangs, who was announcing to his whole row that he was going to eat the new guy. "He's good. He's playing Fudge. Fudge is going to hook you and *chop chop chop*—"

"FANG HU." Boss Mei didn't look up from her tiles. "Out."

Fang Hu dropped behind my chair so fast he might have been shot. Boss Mei played a tile. Ten seconds later he was back at my shoulder, whispering now, which in his case meant only the closest two rows could hear him.

The game started, and the map loaded the way it had ten thousand times in my head. A square of land seen from above. Five heroes on each side, one player each. Three lanes running from our corner to theirs, each guarded by towers, a river cutting across the middle, forest packed in between. Down every lane, forever, little armies of computer-run soldiers called creeps marched out to die, and you only got their gold if yours was the hit that finished them. At the far end of everything stood the enemy's Ancient, a huge glowing tree of a building. Break it and you win. That was the whole game. It had taken me fifteen years to learn the rest.

I walked Soul Fiend to the middle of the map, and for about ninety seconds, I was home.

I could read all of it. Streak's Fudge stood exactly where a boy who'd just said "eat the new guy" out loud would stand, too far forward, swinging his cleaver at the air. The creeps came down the lane in their little marching clumps, and I knew every rhythm of it: which soldier would die first, how long a health bar lasts under two swords, the exact moment to hit. I could have done it with my eyes shut.

Which, it turned out, would have made no difference.

The first enemy creep dropped to a sliver of health, and I knew it, and I clicked. My hero's arm came down a quarter-second after Streak's cleaver. Gone. The second one, the same. The third I got. The fourth I missed by so little I said "oh, come *on*" out loud.

Soul Fiend takes a soul with every killing blow, and every soul makes him hit harder. So every creep I missed cost me twice. Once in gold, and once in the damage I'd have needed to get the next one.

"You missed," Fang Hu said. "You missed again. Ge, you're missing all of them."

"I'm warming up."

"You're warming up *him*."

My brain sent the order on time, every time. My hands got it the way the county post office gets a letter. In my first life I'd had fifteen years of practice in these fingers. These ones had played maybe forty games of anything, mostly the snake game on a classmate's phone. I could feel the gap like a step that wasn't there in the dark.

So I stopped trying to win with my hands and played the part I still had.

The rune appeared in the river at the two-minute mark, and Streak didn't go for it. He backed into the fog beside the lane and didn't come out. Kids who are about to do something clever always go very still first, like cats. I walked up the lane as if I hadn't noticed, then stepped once to the side, so one of my own creeps stood between me and the trees.

The hook came out of the fog, hit the creep, and dragged a confused little soldier across the lane into Streak's arms.

Fang Hu went quiet. It was the first time since I'd met him, and I'd met him about four minutes earlier.

"How did you know?"

"He went into the trees when the rune came up and never went for it. Kids can't wait."

"*You're* a kid."

"Shh."

Streak was out of mana and down to half health, and he'd wasted his hook. For the next minute I bullied him. My hits came late, but my positioning didn't, and every time he stepped forward I was already where he didn't want me. He dropped to a third of his health, then a quarter. Two rows over, he had stopped narrating.

He turned to run. He turned exactly the way they all do, back toward his tower, straight down the middle of the lane, and he was standing precisely at the distance of my middle raze. I knew that range the way you know the height of your own stairs. I pressed the key.

The key went down halfway and stayed there.

It didn't click, and nothing happened on the screen. Under my finger it felt like pressing a wad of chewing gum, because somebody, sometime in this keyboard's long, hard life, had spilled half a can of Coco Cola into it and let it dry into glue.

I pressed it again, harder. It made a small sticky sound, *tchk*, and stayed down.

Streak had stopped running. He'd seen me stand there doing nothing, and even an eleven-year-old would have understood what that meant. His hook came back up. It caught me clean and dragged me under his tower. His stench cooked me, his cleaver chopped, the tower shot, and my demon came apart on the riverbank while I was still trying to dig the middle raze out of the keyboard with my thumbnail.

Behind me, Fang Hu laughed.

It wasn't a polite laugh. He doubled over the back of my chair and howled, and then, because one witness wasn't enough, he stood up and performed it for the room, jabbing one finger down at nothing over and over with his eyes going wider each time. "He pressed it like this! Like *this*! And then he just stood there! Like a scarecrow!"

Two rows over, Streak stood on his chair and raised both fists. Half the cafe cheered. The boy at twenty-six took his headset off just to clap.

"FANG HU." This time Boss Mei turned around in her seat. "Your father is a policeman. If he walks in and finds you here, he arrests me, and then I come and find you. Out. I mean it this time."

"You always mean it."

"Out, you little turtle's egg."

He went, slowly, walking backward and still doing the finger.

I got the key free in the end, with a coin from my pocket. It came up with a sound like tape off a box. By then Fudge had grown huge on my death and wandered off to visit my teammates, who typed things into the chat that would have gotten them chased round the courtyard with a broom by any grandmother in Sichuan. I fought for twenty more minutes. I read every gank coming and called it in chat, and nobody listened, because I was the new account who'd fed mid. At thirty-one minutes the team typed *gg* one by one, the way people leave a funeral.

I sat looking at the defeat screen.

I should have won that game in my sleep. I had won that game in my sleep, more or less; I'd played it half-asleep at four in the morning in a dozen team houses, against people a lot better than a boy with a bleached streak. I looked at my hands on the dirty keyboard. They were thin, with bitten nails, pale knuckles, and a pen callus on the middle finger from homework I didn't remember doing. A child's hands, and they'd done exactly what a child's hands do.

I was so angry that it took me a moment to understand who I was angry at. Not Streak, and not the keyboard. I was angry at fifteen, and fifteen wasn't going to apologize.

I logged out with more than an hour still paid for. Boss Mei didn't look up when the beads clicked. "One game," she said to her tiles. "Hm." That was all.

Fang Hu was waiting at the bottom of the stairs among the garden hoses, of course. He saw me, jumped the last three steps, landed hard, coughed twice into his huge sleeve, and laughed it off like the coughing was part of the joke.

"Ge! Ge, wait."

I kept walking. He kept up, which took two of his steps to every one of mine.

"Can you teach me that?"

"Teach you what? How to get stuck to a keyboard?"

"No, the other thing. Before. When you hid behind the soldier and he hooked the soldier." He re-enacted it in the street, both roles, hook and confused soldier, and nearly walked into a motorbike. "How did you *know*? Streak hooks *everybody*. He hooked me nine times on Thursday."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you laughed at me in front of forty people."

"Thirty," he said. "Some of them were asleep."

The main street was getting loud now, motorbikes and steamer baskets and a radio somewhere playing a love song from three years ago. I was heading for the bridge, because there were peppercorns to buy and a lie to tell Grandma, and I'd promised myself it would be a nice one.

Fang Hu was still talking. Something about Streak's cousin, and a hero he wanted to learn, and whether I was really fifteen or just short.

I'd spent fifteen years in my first life as the best player on bad teams. I'd blamed the teams. Some of them deserved it. But I'd never once built one. I'd walked into rooms full of people who were already finished, already set in their bad habits and their pride, and expected them to fit around me.

Nobody in this town was finished yet. Not Streak, and not the boys asleep in the back row. Not this one either, with his scabbed knees and his tooth that hadn't made up its mind.

Five. You need five.

"Is that a no like a no," Fang Hu asked, "or a no like when my dad says no?"

"What's the difference?"

"My dad's no means no." He grinned his gap at me. "Yours sounded like a maybe."

I bought the peppercorns. He followed me home as far as the market, explaining the whole way exactly how wrong I'd been to pick Soul Fiend.
