The thing nobody tells you about being fifteen again is how much your knees don't hurt.

I kept waiting for it. Every time I stood up from the plastic chair at Starlight, some part of me braced for the click, the dull complaint from the left one, the little old-man noise I'd been making since about twenty-six. Nothing. My knees worked like knees. It was almost insulting.

"Why do you groan like that?" Big Bird asked, through a mouthful of spicy tofu skin. "You sound like my grandpa."

"Your grandpa has wisdom."

"My grandpa thinks the television is listening to him."

"Like I said."

On the screen in front of me, my hero stood in the river with a third of his health and no mana, which is the AotA version of standing in the street in your underwear. Three enemies were somewhere in the fog. I knew roughly where, because I knew what boys in a Sichuan net cafe did in 2006 when they saw a lonely hero in the river: all of them came. Every time. It was the most reliable thing in the county, after Grandma's opinion of my hair.

"Bird," I said. "Stay in the trees. Don't move until I say."

"I can take them."

"You can take a bowl of noodles. Stay."

He stayed, mostly. His hero's huge shoulder poked out of the treeline like a man hiding behind a lamppost, but the boys on the other side were fifteen too, and they were looking at me.

They came out of the fog together, which was the mistake. Three of them on one dying hero felt like a sure thing. I let them have two seconds of thinking it, walked my hero one step backward, toward the trees, and said, "Now."

Big Bird came out roaring. In the game and in real life. Half of Starlight turned around. His hero slammed the ground, and three enemy heroes stood stunned in a neat little row, like ducks at a carnival stall.

Here is the nerd part, and I'll keep it short: a stun is only as good as what you do during it. Most kids in 2006 panicked and clicked everything at once. My fingers wanted to do the clean version, the one I'd done ten thousand times on a stage in Shanghai. Skill, skill, item, step back. My fingers were also fifteen and had never been on a stage anywhere. They did skill, skill, and then hit the wrong key and bought a healing potion.

It worked anyway. Two of them died. The third ran home with a sliver of health, and across the room a boy in a basketball jersey two sizes too big stood up and swore at his monitor in a way that made Boss Mei shout "Language! This is a family business!" from behind the counter, which was funny, because she was smoking under a sign that said NO SMOKING and had a mahjong tile in her other hand.

"Lin Chen." Big Bird stared at me. "How did you know they'd all come?"

"Because they're kids."

"You're a kid."

"I'm a very old kid."

He laughed like that was a joke. I let him.

I walked home at nine with the smell of cigarettes in my school shirt and three yuan left in my pocket. The street was all motorbikes and dumpling steam. Somebody's radio was playing a pop song I'd forgotten I knew, and I found myself humming the chorus before I remembered that the singer would get married, divorced and arrested before I turned twenty-five. Some things you can't unknow.

Grandma was waiting up. She always pretended she wasn't. She sat by the window with the television on and a cardigan around her shoulders, and as soon as I came through the door she said, "You smell like a chimney, Chen-wa," and went to heat up the noodles she'd said she wouldn't save for me.

I sat at the little folding table and watched her limp to the stove. She thought I didn't notice the limp. Last time around, I hadn't.

"Nainai," I said. "Sit down. I'll do it."

She looked at me like I'd grown a second head. Then she sat down, slowly, and said, "Who are you, and what did you do with my grandson?"

I didn't have a good answer to that. I heated the noodles.
