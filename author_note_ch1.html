What this story is: a washed-up pro gamer dies broke and alone at 31, then wakes up at 15 in a small town in Sichuan, China, in 2006. The game that ate his life is still a free mod played in smoky net cafes. This time he wants to build the team he never had. It's an esports story with a lot of comedy, a grandmother who feeds everyone, and a mystery under it all. There are no stat screens or system windows: just memory, practice and stubbornness.

The story is set in real-world China, so here are a few things that might help if you're not familiar with it.

Names

  • Chinese names put the family name first. In "Lin Chen", Lin is the family name and Chen is the given name. Grandma is Lin Guifen, the same family.
  • Almost everyone has a nickname, and the story always tells you who it belongs to. Big Bird is Zhao Dapeng, Monk is Tang Yi, and so on.
  • Lao ("old") and Xiao ("little") in front of a surname are friendly ways to address someone: Lao Wang, Lao Zhao.

Words you'll see

  • Nainai: Grandma (father's mother).
  • Ge: "big brother". Younger kids use it for any older boy they like or look up to.
  • Wa: Sichuan dialect for "kid". Grandma calls Lin Chen Chen-wa.
  • Ayi / Shushu: "auntie / uncle", how kids politely address adults who aren't family.
  • Aiya: an all-purpose "oh no / oh my".
  • "Have you eaten?" is how many Chinese people say "I care about you".

China in 2006

  • Net cafes were where teenagers played games, because few families had a home PC. They were officially banned for under-18s, so kids borrowed adults' ID numbers and hid under desks when the police came. Parents called games "electronic heroin".
  • School runs six days a week. Grades are ranked and posted on the wall for everyone to see. The zhongkao (high school entrance exam) and gaokao (university entrance exam) decide a kid's future, so the pressure is enormous.
  • "Left-behind children": millions of parents left their villages and towns to work in factories in the big coastal cities, and their children were raised by grandparents. Lin Chen is one of them. His parents work in Dongguan and come home once a year, at Spring Festival (Chinese New Year).
  • Money: prices are in yuan. 1 yuan = 10 jiao = 100 fen, and kids count all three. In 2006, 1 US dollar was about 8 yuan. A bowl of noodles cost 3 yuan, an hour at the net cafe 2 yuan, and a factory worker earned about 1,000 to 1,500 yuan a month. When Lin Chen counts his coins, it really matters.
  • Golden week: the National Day holiday, October 1-7. Shops are busy, schools are closed, and everyone travels. Mid-Autumn Festival (in 2006, October 6) is the family holiday of mooncakes and the full moon.
  • Phones: no smartphones yet. Flip phones, text messages and QQ-style chat on PCs.

Sichuan

Sichuan is a province in southwest China, famous for spicy food, numbing peppercorns, hotpot, tea houses, mahjong, and people who take relaxing very seriously. Qingshui, Lin Chen's hometown, is fictional, but it sits in a real place: about two hours by bus from Chengdu, the provincial capital.

The game

AotA, "Attack of the Ancients", is a fan-made map for a fantasy strategy game, inspired by the real games that started the MOBA genre. Two teams of five heroes fight down three lanes to destroy the enemy's base, the Ancient. You don't need to know anything about games to follow the story: matches are told through the people playing them.

All games, companies, teams and brands in this story are fictional, and any resemblance to real ones is affectionate parody.
